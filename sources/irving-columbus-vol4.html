A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus — Vol. IV

Author: Washington Irving Full title: A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus Publication: 1829 (this edition: Baudry, Paris) Volume: IV (contains Book XVIII and Appendix)

Summary of Contents

This is Volume IV of Irving’s biography, containing the concluding Book XVIII (the final years and death of Columbus) followed by a substantial Appendix of 34 or more documentary, biographical, and geographical supplements. The volume covers Columbus’s return from his fourth voyage, his final applications to the crown, his death in 1506, and a concluding character study. The Appendix gathers a wide range of supporting material: genealogical records, biographical sketches of contemporaries and chroniclers, essays on pre-Columbian voyages and geographical theories, and documentation on the Columbus family.

Chapter-by-Chapter Detail

BOOK XVIII — Final Years and Death

Chapter I: Departure for St. Domingo, Return to Spain (1504) — Covers Columbus’s departure from Hispaniola (St. Domingo) after his fourth voyage, his difficult return passage across the Atlantic, and his arrival back in Spain. Treats the condition of the Admiral upon his return — physically worn and burdened by the loss of his official positions and the collapse of his governorship.

Chapter II: Illness at Seville, Application to the Crown, Death of Isabella — Describes Columbus’s residence in Seville following his return, the deterioration of his health, and his efforts to petition the Spanish sovereigns for restoration of his titles and revenues. Documents the death of Queen Isabella in November 1504, which removed Columbus’s most steadfast patron at court and severely weakened his position in negotiations with King Ferdinand.

Chapter III: Columbus Arrives at Court, Fruitless Application for Redress — Columbus’s journey to the court of King Ferdinand, his audience with the king, and the failure of his petitions for reinstatement as governor and restoration of his privileges. Covers the legal and political obstacles raised against his claims and the Crown’s reluctance to restore his former authority in the Indies.

Chapter IV: Death of Columbus (1506) — The final illness and death of Christopher Columbus at Valladolid on May 20, 1506. Describes his last days, his testamentary arrangements, and the immediate aftermath of his death. Notes the presence of his sons Diego and Fernando at his deathbed and the modest circumstances of his passing.

Chapter V: Observations on the Character of Columbus — A concluding, retrospective chapter offering Irving’s observations on Columbus’s personal qualities: his religious devotion, his perseverance in the face of adversity, his administrative strengths and weaknesses as governor, his temperament, his intellectual formation, and his place in history. Draws on the testimony of contemporaries such as Las Casas, Peter Martyr, and Fernando Columbus.

Appendix — Contents (34+ Items)

The Appendix to Volume IV contains a collection of supplementary essays, biographical notices, documentary records, and critical discussions. The following entries are included:

No. I: Transportation of the Remains of Columbus to Havana — An account of the removal of Columbus’s remains from Valladolid to Seville, their subsequent transfer to Santo Domingo (Hispaniola), and their final translation to Havana, Cuba, following the cession of Hispaniola to France in 1795.

No. II: Descendants of Columbus — A genealogical account of Columbus’s descendants: his sons Diego and Fernando, the line of Dukes of Veragua, and the subsequent history of the Columbus family through successive generations.

No. III: Fernando Columbus — A biographical sketch of Fernando (Ferdinand) Columbus, the Admiral’s second son, natural historian, scholar, and bibliophile. Covers his education, his travels, his collection of books (the Biblioteca Colombina in Seville), and his biography of his father.

No. IV: Lineage of Columbus — A discussion of the genealogical origins of the Columbus family, tracing ancestry and disputing various claims about the family’s nobility and background.

No. V: Birthplace of Columbus — An examination of the evidence concerning Columbus’s birthplace in the Republic of Genoa, addressing the rival claims of various Genoese localities.

No. VI: The Colombos — A historical note on the Colombo (Colombo) family of Genoa, their maritime traditions, and their relationship to Christopher Columbus.

No. VII: John of Anjou Expedition — An account of the expedition of John of Anjou, Duke of Lorraine, and his naval operations in the Mediterranean (1459-1462), in which the young Columbus is said to have participated.

No. VIII: Capture of the Venetian Galleys — A narrative describing the naval battle off Cape St. Vincent (1476) in which Columbus participated, involving a Genoese squadron and the capture of Venetian galleys.

No. IX: Amerigo Vespucci — A biographical notice on Amerigo Vespucci, the Florentine navigator after whom America was named. Examines his voyages, his writings, and the controversy over his role in the discovery of the South American mainland.

No. X: Martin Alonso Pinzon — A biographical sketch of Martin Alonso Pinzon, the Spanish mariner and co-discoverer who commanded the Pinta on Columbus’s first voyage. Treats his rivalry with Columbus and his subsequent career.

No. XI: The Pilot Who Died in the House of Columbus — An examination of the story, reported by Las Casas, of an unknown pilot who, having previously reached lands in the west, died in Columbus’s house in Madeira or the Azores and whose papers and charts influenced Columbus’s plans. Discusses the credibility of this account.

No. XII: Martin Behem — A biographical essay on Martin Behaim (Behem), the German cosmographer and globe-maker who corresponded with Columbus and constructed the famous terrestrial globe of Nuremberg (1492). Discusses his influence on contemporary geography.

No. XIII: Voyages of the Scandinavians — A survey of the early Norse voyages to North America: the discoveries of Bjarni Herjolfsson, Leif Erikson, and subsequent Norse expeditions to Vinland, Greenland, and Markland. Discusses the extent of knowledge of these voyages in Columbus’s time.

No. XIV: Circumnavigation of Africa by the Ancients — An essay on ancient accounts of the circumnavigation of Africa, including the Phoenician expedition under Pharaoh Necho (reported by Herodotus) and the voyage of Hanno the Carthaginian. Discusses what ancient geographers knew of the African continent.

No. XV: St. Brandan’s Island — A discussion of the legendary Island of St. Brendan (San Borondón), the mythical island in the Atlantic associated with the sixth-century Irish monk’s voyage. Traces its appearance on medieval maps and its persistence in geographical imagination.

No. XVI: Island of the Seven Cities — An essay on the legend of the Island of the Seven Cities (Antilia), the mythical Atlantic island supposedly settled by seven Portuguese bishops fleeing the Moorish conquest. Discusses its role in shaping expectations about discovery.

No. XVII: Discovery of Madeira — An account of the rediscovery and colonization of the Madeira archipelago by Portuguese navigators under Prince Henry the Navigator in the early fifteenth century.

No. XVIII: Las Casas — A biographical sketch of Bartolome de las Casas, the Spanish Dominican friar, historian, and defender of the indigenous peoples of the Americas. Discusses his Historia de las Indias, his Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias, and his use of Columbus’s journals and papers.

No. XIX: Peter Martyr d’Anghiera — A biographical notice on Peter Martyr (Pietro Martire d’Anghiera), the Italian-born humanist, historian, and diplomat at the Spanish court whose De Orbe Novo (Decades of the New World) provides a contemporary account of the early discoveries.

No. XX: Gonzalo Fernandez de Oviedo — A biographical sketch of Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo y Valdés, the Spanish historian and naturalist who served as official chronicler of the Indies. Discusses his Historia General y Natural de las Indias and his relationship to Columbus.

No. XXI: The Cura de Los Palacios — A biographical note on Andrés Bernáldez, the curate of Los Palacios, whose chronicle Historia de los Reyes Católicos includes valuable contemporary testimony about Columbus and the early voyages.

No. XXII: Navigatione del Re — A discussion of the Navigatione del Re (or Navigation of the King), a work or document concerning Portuguese royal navigation and discovery projects during the reign of King John II.

No. XXIII: Antonio de Herrera — A biographical notice on Antonio de Herrera y Tordesillas, the official Spanish chronicler of the Indies whose Historia General de los Hechos de los Castellanos en las Islas y Tierra Firme del Mar Océano (commonly known as the Décadas) is a major source for early Spanish exploration.

No. XXIV: Bishop Juan Rodriguez de Fonseca — A biographical sketch of Juan Rodríguez de Fonseca, the Spanish ecclesiastic and royal administrator who served as head of the Casa de Contratación and as Columbus’s persistent adversary in court.

No. XXV: Situation of the Terrestrial Paradise — An essay examining Columbus’s belief, expressed during his third voyage, that the Orinoco River region was the location of the Terrestrial Paradise (Garden of Eden). Discusses the patristic and medieval geographical theories that informed this conviction.

(Additional appendices beyond No. XXV exist in the collection but their exact sequence and numbering are not fully recorded in the available source. The total Appendix contains at least 34 numbered items.)
