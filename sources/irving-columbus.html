A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus

Author: Washington Irving Full title: A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus Publication: 1828 (this edition: A. & W. Galignani, France) Source file: irving_columbus_full.txt Quality note: OCR-scanned text (Anna’s Archive / Google Books). Body text is clean and readable; occasional page-header artifacts and line-break hyphenation issues.

Summary of Contents

This is Volume I of Irving’s biography, covering Columbus’s life from his birth in Genoa through the successful return from his first voyage and preparations for the second. It ends with the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), which divided the newly discovered territories between Spain and Portugal. The work draws heavily on the documents collected by Martin Fernandez de Navarrete, as well as Fernando Columbus’s biography of his father, and contemporary chroniclers such as Peter Martyr, Oviedo, Las Casas, and Herrera.

Chapter-by-Chapter Detail

BOOK I — Early Life and First Applications

Chapter I: Birth, Parentage, and Education of Columbus — Covers Columbus’s birth in Genoa, his family background (his father Domenico Colombo was a wool-comber), and his early education at the University of Pavia. Describes his early inclination toward the sea and his apprenticeship in the Mediterranean.

Chapter II: Early Life of Columbus — Follows Columbus’s early seafaring career: voyages in the Mediterranean under the elder Colombo (his relative), service on a Genoese squadron, a shipwreck off the coast of Portugal, and his arrival in Lisbon around 1470. Describes his marriage to Felipa Perestrello and his access to his father-in-law’s nautical charts and papers.

Chapter III: Progress of Discovery under Prince Henry of Portugal — Surveys the Portuguese Age of Discovery under Prince Henry the Navigator: the exploration of the African coast, the discovery of Madeira and the Azores, the development of the caravel, and the gradual Portuguese push southward. Sets the context for Columbus’s own ambitions.

Chapter IV: Residence of Columbus at Lisbon — Ideas concerning Islands in the Ocean — Describes Columbus’s life in Lisbon, his study of classical and medieval geography (Aristotle, Seneca, Pliny, Marco Polo, Mandeville), and his growing conviction that undiscovered lands lay to the west. Discusses the legend of Antilia and the Island of St. Brendan.

Chapter V: Grounds on which Columbus founded his belief of the existence of undiscovered lands in the West — Details the three categories of evidence Columbus assembled: (1) natural/physical signs (unusual currents, floating vegetation, carved sticks washed ashore); (2) classical authority (Aristotle, Seneca, Strabo); (3) reports from mariners of islands sighted to the west.

Chapter VI: Correspondence of Columbus with Paulo Toscanelli — Covers Columbus’s correspondence with the Florentine astronomer and cosmographer Paolo Toscanelli, who sent him a map and letter supporting the westward route to Asia. Also discusses Portuguese discoveries under King John II, including the exploration of the African coast.

Chapter VII: Propositions of Columbus to the Court of Portugal — Describes Columbus’s formal application to King John II of Portugal, the submission of his plan to a royal council, its rejection (partly due to the competing project of the Cape route), and the navigator’s secret dispatch of a caravel to test Columbus’s theory without him.

Chapter VIII: Departure of Columbus from Portugal, and his Application to other Courts — Columbus’s departure from Portugal in 1485 after the secret-caravel incident. His journey to Genoa to present his plan, then to Venice (both rejected). His decision to try the Spanish court, and his arrival in Spain.

BOOK II — Negotiations with Spain

Chapter I: First Arrival of Columbus in Spain — Columbus’s arrival at the Franciscan monastery of La Rábida near Palos, his meeting with Fray Juan Perez de Marchena, who became his advocate. His first appearance at the court of Ferdinand and Isabella.

Chapter II: Characters of Ferdinand and Isabella — A portrait of the Spanish monarchs: Ferdinand’s political acumen and Isabella’s religious devotion and strong will. Their recent unification of Castile and Aragon and their completion of the Reconquista.

Chapter III: Propositions of Columbus to the Court of Castile — Columbus’s arrival at Cordova in 1486, his presentation to the sovereigns, and the appointment of a commission headed by Hernando de Talavera to examine his proposals.

Chapter IV: Columbus before the Council at Salamanca — The famous academic conference at Salamanca, where Columbus defended his theories before the learned men of Spain. The theological and scientific objections raised, and the ambiguous outcome.

Chapter V: Further Applications at the Court of Castile — Columbus follows the Court in its Campaigns — Columbus’s long years of waiting (1487–1491), following the Spanish court from camp to camp during the war against Granada. His growing frustration, the repeated delays, and his near-abandonment of the project.

Chapter VI: Application to the Duke of Medina Celi — Return to the Convent of La Rábida — Columbus’s brief negotiation with the Duke of Medina Celi, who offered to finance the voyage himself. His return to La Rábida, where Fray Juan Perez interceded with Queen Isabella on his behalf.

Chapter VII: Application to the Court at the time of the Surrender of Granada — Columbus’s recall to court after the fall of Granada in January 1492. The final rejection by the Talavera commission, Columbus’s departure from court, and his interception by Luis de Santangel, who persuaded Isabella to reconsider.

Chapter VIII: Arrangement with the Spanish Sovereigns — The historic agreement at Santa Fe: the Capitulations of April 1492, granting Columbus the titles of Admiral, Viceroy, and Governor, and one-tenth of all discoveries. The financing of the expedition (partly from the Santa Hermandad, partly from Santangel’s loan).

Chapter IX: Preparations for the Expedition at the Port of Palos — Columbus’s return to Palos, the royal order compelling the town to provide two caravels. The reluctance of the seamen, the recruitment of the Pinzon brothers, and the final fitting of the three ships: the Santa Maria, the Pinta, and the Niña.

BOOK III — The First Voyage

Chapter I: Departure of Columbus on his First Voyage — The departure from Palos on August 3, 1492. The course to the Canary Islands, the repair of the Pinta’s rudder, and the final departure from Gomera on September 6.

Chapter II: Continuation of the Voyage — Variation of the Needle — The voyage westward into unknown waters. The discovery of magnetic declination (variation of the compass needle), which alarmed the sailors. Columbus’s skill in managing the crews’ fears.

Chapter III: Continuation of the Voyage — Various Terrors of the Seamen — The growing panic among the crews as weeks passed without sight of land. Fears of contrary winds, the Sargasso Sea, sea monsters, and never being able to return. The mutinous murmurs and Columbus’s attempts to reassure them.

Chapter IV: Continuation of the Voyage — Discovery of Land — The final days: signs of land (birds, floating branches, carved wood). The sighting of land by Rodrigo de Triana on October 12, 1492. The landing on Guanahani (San Salvador).

BOOK IV — The Discovery

Chapter I: First Landing of Columbus in the New World — The ceremonial landing on San Salvador, taking possession for Spain. First contact with the Taino people, their appearance and customs, and Columbus’s impression that he had reached islands off Asia.

Chapter II: Cruise among the Bahama Islands — The voyage through the Bahamas, discovering and naming Santa Maria de la Concepcion, Fernandina, and Isabella. Encounters with the native people and observations of their culture.

Chapter III: Discovery and Coasting of Cuba — The arrival at Cuba on October 28. The expedition’s exploration of the Cuban coast, the search for gold and the Grand Khan of Asia. The sending of an embassy inland.

Chapter IV: Further Coasting of Cuba — Continued exploration of the Cuban coast. Attempts to communicate with the natives and the search for cities of gold. Columbus’s decision to turn east toward what he hoped was Japan.

Chapter V: Search after the supposed Island of Babeque — Desertion of the Pinta — The search for the golden island of Babeque (according to native reports). The desertion of the Pinta under Martin Alonso Pinzon, who sought gold independently.

Chapter VI: Discovery of Hispaniola — The arrival at Hispaniola (December 5). Contact with the Taino chief Guacanagari. Favorable reports of gold in the island’s interior.

Chapter VII: Coasting of Hispaniola — Navigation along the northern coast of Hispaniola. Further trade with natives, acquisition of gold ornaments, and growing confidence that this was a land of great wealth.

Chapter VIII: Shipwreck — The wreck of the Santa Maria on Christmas Eve 1492. The beaching and dismantling of the ship. Guacanagari’s assistance in saving the crew and stores.

Chapter IX: Transactions with the Natives — Columbus’s growing friendship with Guacanagari. Reports of a powerful chief (Caonabo) in the interior. The decision to establish a settlement.

Chapter X: Building of the Fortress of La Navidad — The construction of the fortified settlement of La Navidad from the timbers of the wrecked Santa Maria. The selection of 39 volunteers to remain as the first European colony in the New World.

Chapter XI: Regulation of the Fortress of La Navidad — Departure of Columbus for Spain — Columbus’s instructions to the colonists, his farewell, and the departure of the Niña for Spain on January 4, 1493.

BOOK V — Return and Preparations for the Second Voyage

Chapter I: Coasting towards the Eastern End of Hispaniola — Meeting with Pinzon — Affair with the Natives — The voyage along the coast, the reunion with Pinzon and the Pinta. An armed encounter with natives at the Gulf of Samana.

Chapter II: Return Voyage — Violent Storms — Arrival at the Azores — The Atlantic crossing, a violent storm that nearly sank the Niña, the separation from the Pinta, and the arrival at the island of Santa Maria in the Azores.

Chapter III: Transactions at the Island of St. Mary’s — Portuguese hostility at the Azores, a brief detention of Columbus’s men by the Portuguese governor, and Columbus’s diplomatic resolution of the incident.

Chapter IV: Arrival at Portugal — Visit to the Court — Forced by weather to land at Lisbon. Columbus’s audience with King John II of Portugal, the king’s chagrin at losing the western discovery to Spain, and a near-violent confrontation between the two nations.

Chapter V: Reception of Columbus at Palos — Columbus’s triumphant return to Palos on March 15, 1493. The rejoicing of the townspeople and the arrival of the Pinta shortly after.

Chapter VI: Reception of Columbus by the Spanish Court at Barcelona — Columbus’s journey to Barcelona, his ceremonial reception by Ferdinand and Isabella, and the presentation of the trophies of the New World (gold, exotic plants, parrots, and Taino people).

Chapter VII: Sojourn of Columbus at Barcelona — Attentions paid him by the Sovereigns and Courtiers — Columbus’s weeks of honor at Barcelona, the royal favor, the preparation of his coat of arms, and the jealousy of some courtiers.

Chapter VIII: Papal Bull of Partition — Preparations for a Second Voyage of Columbus — Pope Alexander VI’s bulls (May 1493) dividing the non-Christian world between Spain and Portugal. The massive preparations for a second expedition of 17 ships and 1,500 men.

Chapter IX: Diplomatic Negotiations between the Courts of Spain and Portugal — The diplomatic dispute between Spain and Portugal over the new discoveries. The Portuguese claim based on earlier treaties and the Spanish reliance on the papal bulls.

Chapter X: Further Preparations for the Second Voyage — Character of Alonso de Ojeda — Difference of Columbus with Soria — The continued outfitting of the fleet. The introduction of Alonso de Ojeda, a young nobleman who would become a significant figure. Columbus’s conflicts with Juan de Soria over supplies and personnel.

(Source ends here with the Treaty of Tordesillas settlement, June 1494, and the end of Volume I.)
