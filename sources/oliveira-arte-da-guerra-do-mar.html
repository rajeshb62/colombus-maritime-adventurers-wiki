A Arte da Guerra do Mar — Padre Fernando Oliveira

Bibliographic Information

  • Author: Padre Fernando Oliveira (also written Fernão de Oliveira, Fernand’ Oliveira), c.1507–c.1581, Portuguese Dominican friar, grammarian, navigator, and humanist

About the Author and Edition

Padre Fernando Oliveira was a Portuguese Dominican friar, philologist, and navigator — author of the first printed Portuguese grammar (Gramática da Linguagem Portuguesa, 1536) — whose restless, frankly opinionated life carried him from Lisbon to Castile, Italy, France, and England. The preliminary commentary (by Quirino da Fonseca, after the study of Henrique Lopes de Mendonça) recounts his clandestine departure from the Tagus in the galley armada of the Baron de La Garde, his service among French galleys against English ships off Dover, his sojourn in Protestant England, and his later trouble with the Inquisition. The Arte da Guerra do Mar, printed at Coimbra in 1555 and dedicated to Dom Nuno da Cunha, captain of the galleys of King John III, was chosen to open the Navy Ministry’s Colecção Documentos series as the oldest known treatise on naval strategy and tactics in print: “Tem este livro a destacá-lo o facto de ser o mais antigo tratado de estratégia e táctica naval de que há memória em letra de forma” (the book is distinguished by being the oldest treatise of naval strategy and tactics of which there is record in print).

Summary

The work is divided into two parts. The first part, “da intenção e apercebimento da guerra” (on the purpose and preparation of war at sea), treats the justification, justice, and conduct of war; the office of the Admiral; the shipyards (taracenas) and their provisioning; timber for ships and when to cut it; magazines and their supply; victuals; the men of the sea; the captains of the sea and their authority; the choosing and settling of soldiers; and the exercise of soldiers. The second part, “das frotas armadas, e das batalhas maritimas, e seus ardis” (on armed fleets, maritime battles, and their stratagems), treats ships for the armadas and the number of men for them; provisioning of stores, munitions, and rigging; the time to sail and the change of seasons; signs of storms and variation of the weathers; the winds, their regions and names; necessary advices for navigation; tides, currents, and the sea’s movements; how the armadas set sail; battles at sea and the stratagems needed in them; the place for fighting; the loss of the ships that went with the king of Bélez; the ordnances of war at sea; some general rules of war; and the conclusion of the work. Throughout, Oliveira draws on classical authorities (Vegetius, Pliny, Quintilian, Justin) and on his own sea service to prescribe the practical conduct of a naval war.

Scope and Approach

Oliveira writes a prescriptive and technical treatise rather than a narrative history: he lays down the offices, provisioning, seamanship, and battle order required to equip and fight an armada, mixing classical citation with firsthand knowledge of Portuguese and French galleys and caravels. He opens the Prologue by declaring that no earlier author, so far as he knew, had written any art or documents of war at sea — “Da qual nenhum autor, que eu sayba, escreuco antes dagora arte nem documentos” — the claim that makes the work foundational in the history of naval theory. The Navy Ministry edition frames him as “uma das figuras mais completas da Renascença” in Portugal (one of the most complete figures of the Renaissance in Portugal), restless, eclectic, and ahead of his time in opinion.

Contents

Editorial Front Matter

Nota explicativa — The Navy Ministry explains the Colecção Documentos series, chosen to open with the Arte da Guerra do Mar as the oldest printed treatise of naval strategy and tactics, and sketches Oliveira’s biography (first Portuguese grammar, cleric and scholar, rebel and outspoken).

Comentários preliminares (by Comandante Quirino da Fonseca, from the 1937 edition) — A biographical study of Oliveira: his schooling, his grammar of the Portuguese language, his service as master to the sons of the chronicler of India João de Barros and of other noble houses, his mysterious commission to Italy, his flight with the galleys of the Baron de La Garde, the fight against English ships off Dover, his stay in Protestant England and correspondence with the Count of Castanheira, his return to Lisbon and the bookseller’s shop of João Fernandes, and the suspicion of the Inquisition that his frankness drew upon him.

Prologo (com exordio, e escusa do autor)

Prologue with exordium and the author’s apology — Oliveira addresses the “muyto manifico señor” (Dom Nuno da Cunha), drawing on Quintilian on the art of pleasing hearers, defends the active life of travel against mere idleness, praises the Portuguese for conquering and discovering new lands and spreading the faith by “a guerra do mar,” and declares that no author before him, so far as he knew, had written an art or documents of it; he excuses the novelty and smallness of his present to his patron.

Parte Primeira — “da intenção e apercebimento da guerra no mar”

Capítulo I: Que he necessario fazer guerra (That it is necessary to make war) — Opens on the authority of Plato and the laws of Crete that men should be kept prepared as in continual war; holds that since men disobeyed God they live in natural discord, and that war is sometimes necessary to defend the land.

Capítulo II: De quem pode fazer guerra (Of who may make war) — On the lawful maker of war, the prince and the commonwealth that defends its land and people.

Capítulo III: Que he necessario fazer guerra no mar (That it is necessary to make war at sea) — The necessity of war at sea for a maritime realm, the defence of coasts, and the keeping of the sea as part of the land’s safety.

Capítulo IIII: Qual he guerra justa (Which war is just) — The conditions of a just war, on the authority of the doctors of the Church, defense against injury, and the recovery of what is justly due.

Capítulo V: Da tenção e modo da guerra (Of the intention and manner of war) — The right intent and orderly manner of waging war.

Capítulo VI: Do officio do Almirante (Of the office of the Admiral) — The Admiral’s duty: to have ships ready in the taracenas to answer sudden surprises, and to keep all the navy’s provisions in order.

Capítulo VII: Das taracenas e seu prouimento (Of the shipyards and their provisioning) — The taracenas (royal shipyards) must hold ships made and ready, with cordage, nails, pitch, oakum, and all tackle; on ropes of hemp and of cotton, and on their qualities.

Capítulo VIII: Da Madeyra pera as naos (Of timber for the ships) — Which woods suit ships, differing by the lands in which they grow; on cork (souaro/sobreiro), larch, chestnut, and other timbers, their strength, resistance to rot and to the sea, and how to choose sound trees.

Capítulo IX: De quando se cortaraa a madeyra (Of when the timber shall be cut) — On the season and manner of cutting timber — before the sap and humours rise — so that the wood keeps its strength, and on sparing the cork woods and favouring the growth of pines.

Capítulo X: Dos armazens e seu provimento (Of the magazines and their supply) — The armazéns must be furnished with shot, powder, and artillery, and the men trained in their use, that the Portuguese may defend themselves with equal arms against the Moors; on the arms used on both sides.

Capítulo XI: Das victualhas (Of the victuals) — On provisioning biscuit, wheat, rice, maize, and roots as bread, on beans and pulses (after Pliny), oil, vinegar, candles, and all things needful for an armada.

Capítulo XII: Dos homens do mar (Of the men of the sea) — On mariners, rowers, and their numbers and qualities; on pilots and the perils of bad ones.

Capítulo XIII: Dos capitães do mar e do seu poder (Of the captains of the sea and their power) — The captain as the head of the company of war, “de capite palaura latina” (from the Latin word caput, head); the authority to govern and to punish soldiers without process of court; a captain-major in every fleet obeyed as the prince.

Capítulo XIV: De como deuem ser escolhidos, e assentados os soldados (Of how the soldiers ought to be chosen and enrolled) — On choosing soldiers of good blood, breeding, and honour over worthless and low fellows, that arms defend the land rather than ruin it; on the shame and virtue that win victories.

Capítulo XV: Do exercicio dos soldados (Of the exercise of the soldiers) — On keeping soldiers occupied — making wood and water, gathering stores, embarking artillery — lest idleness breed vice; on the danger of those who only win their living by arms and rail at God.

Parte Segunda — “das frotas armadas, e das batalhas maritimas, e seus ardis”

Capítulo I: Dos nauios pera as armadas (Of the ships for the armadas) — The captains charged with an armada must ask the Admiral or his officers for all things needful: ships suited to the sea and to the war, in kind, quantity, and number; long voyages over the great sea require great ships; on ships of oars and of sail, and on the caravel’s mixing of lateen sail and round hull.

Capítulo II: Do numero da gente pera os nauios (Of the number of men for the ships) — The complement of mariners and ship’s boys (grumetes) for ships of ten, twenty, and thirty tons; the master, pilot, and the company of a galley — pilot (patrão), fifteen mariners, and five bowmen, with the oar-crew; the office of the comitre.

Capítulo III: Da esquipação dos mantimentos, munições e enxerceas (Of the equipping of stores, munitions, and rigging) — The captain provides victuals of every kind with surplus, and arms the ship fittingly; on guns, chambers, and how to keep powder and pieces dry and ready against tempest.

Capítulo IV: Do tempo de nauegar as armadas, e mudança dos tempos (Of the time for the armadas to sail, and the change of seasons) — The captain consults with discreet and experienced friends on the time of departure; on the seasons favourable and dangerous for sailing.

Capítulo V: Dos sinaes das tempestades, e varição dos temporaes (Of the signs of storms and the variation of the weathers) — On reading the sky and stars for weather; the stars that show signs of rain and storm (the erratics, the asses of Cancer with their manger), and the need for humble knowledge among mariners.

Capítulo VI: Dos ventos e suas regiões, e nomes (Of the winds, their regions, and names) — A treatise on the winds: the four principal winds (east, west, north, south) and their regions, their names and properties, and the compendium of their quarters.

Capítulo VII: Dalguns auisos necessarios pa nauegar (Of some necessary advices for navigation) — To know the lands one sails toward and what winds will serve; on “demorar” (the bearing of lands), the height of the pole, and the sun’s declination about the equinoctial line.

Capítulo VIII: Das marees, correntes e aguagens do mar (Of the tides, currents, and the sea’s waters) — On the times of high and low water for entering ports with bars and channels; after Pliny, on the greater tides of the full moon and of summer, and on the head of the waters lagging two or three tides behind the lunations.

Capítulo IX: De como as armadas faram vela (Of how the armadas shall set sail) — The order of making sail: masters, pilots, and mariners aboard from the fleet’s assembly; the captain-major flies the prince’s banner at the mainmast (or on the starboard of the poop for oared ships) that he may be known and followed.

Capítulo X: Das batalhas do mar/e alguns ardis necessarios nellas (Of battles at sea, and some stratagems needed in them) — A terrible thing is the sea battle before human eyes, offering death without refuge amid confusions and anguish; on determining matters of war with moderation and diligence, and on the stratagems and deceits used to seize the occasion.

Capítulo XI: Do lugar pera pelejar (Of the place for fighting) — When combat comes the captain takes the best place; after Vegetius, place commands a great part of victory, and those in an importune place suffer a double labour.

Capítulo XII: De como se perderão os nauios que foram com elrey de Belez (Of how the ships that went with the king of Bélez were lost) — A narrative of the loss of the ships that went with the king of Bélez against the Turks: there was no proportion between the strength of the two sides, and the Portuguese lacked trained gunners (few knew how to fire an arquebus) while the enemy was well furnished.

Capítulo XIII: Das ordenanças da guerra do mar (Of the ordnances of war at sea) — Order in battle avails more than multitude; the ordnances are the nerves of the army and the rest of the captain; the formation of the sea squadrons is simple and straight (or but little curved), since the other forms — wedges, wedges-and-rounds, squares — do not avail at sea save to break and cut.

Capítulo XIV: Dalgumas regras geraes da guerra (Of some general rules of war) — General rules gathered for ready use: wise and faithful industry in the officers, use and dexterity in the men, plenty in provisioning, caution and diligence in action.

Capítulo XV: Da conclusam da obra (Of the conclusion of the work) — The conclusion: great importance of the documents of war at sea, especially in Portugal where the livelihoods of many depend on that sea; a call to prepare for the storms to come, to provision the taracenas and magazines, to exercise men in arms, to keep fleets ready, to know the seasons of navigation, and to be alert.

Relation to Other Sources in This Wiki
