About the Author and Edition

Corbett was the leading English naval historian of the late Victorian and Edwardian period, later associated with official and strategic writing on sea power. This work is not a pure personal life of Drake but a two-volume history of the rise of England as a maritime power, with Drake as the central agent of that rise. The 1917 impression reprints the 1898 text; the Preface to the Second Edition answers J. K. Laughton’s criticism on the development of line-ahead tactics in 1588. An epigraph from Sir Walter Raleigh on trade and command of the sea opens the argument. A portrait of Drake from Buckland Abbey is among the front matter illustrations.

Summary

Volume I carries Drake from Crowndale near Tavistock through Hawkins’s third voyage and San Juan de Ulúa, the early Navy service, Nombre de Dios and the Spanish Main, the war-party years, the circumnavigation (three chapters), knighthood, and a closing study of Elizabeth’s Navy. Volume II opens with the Indies voyage of 1585, San Domingo and Cartagena, operations on the Spanish coast, Drake’s plan of campaign against the Armada, the Channel actions through Portland, the Isle of Wight, and Gravelines, then the Lisbon expedition, Drake’s disgrace, the last voyage, and a Conclusion. Appendices in both volumes collect authorities, documents, and technical notes (birth and parentage; San Juan de Ulúa reports; Armada and Indies voyage authorities; Elizabethan tonnage measurement; etc.). Corbett’s thesis—later a target of twentieth-century revision—is that Drake and the Tudor seamen forged a new naval art and England’s claim to maritime supremacy.

Scope and Approach

Corbett writes operational and institutional naval history: ship types, gunnery, fleet tactics, administration, and the political “war party” around Elizabeth, Burghley, Walsingham, and Leicester, as much as pure biography. He treats the long “neither peace nor war” of Anglo-Spanish relations as the sixteenth century’s distinctive international form. The Preface to the Second Edition defends his reading of 1588 tactics against Laughton. Sugden’s later popular biography explicitly names the “Corbett tradition” of Drake as founder of English naval supremacy as one pole it seeks to move beyond; this page documents that older standard work for the wiki corpus.

Contents

Volume I

Introduction / The Naval Art in the Middle of the Sixteenth Century — Sets the technical and strategic baseline of mid-Tudor sea fighting before Drake’s generation transforms it.

Chapter I: Drake’s Early Years — Birth at Crowndale near Tavistock; the farm’s passage from Tavistock Abbey to the Russells; Devon and Reformation milieu; family ties that lead toward the Hawkins connection and the sea.

Chapter II: John Hawkins — Three streams of national sentiment—religious passion foremost among them—that gather toward rupture with Spain and the House of Burgundy. Hawkins’s career as the hinge of Drake’s early service and of England’s first sustained push into the Atlantic slave and contraband trades.

Chapter III: San Juan de Ulúa — Hawkins’s third voyage as the step that “could never be retraced.” Camden’s report that seafaring men fretted for war; Drake’s and his master’s turn to irreconcilable hostility toward Spain; English and Spanish responsibility weighed in Corbett’s balance.

Chapter IV: Drake’s First Service in the Navy — Sixteenth-century hostilities short of open war; Drake’s early official employment; the legal and diplomatic grey zone of reprisal and “distraint” at sea.

Chapter V: Nombre de Dios — The new stream of daring that Corbett sees entering Elizabeth’s Navy through Drake; the 1572 Panama enterprise; Nombre de Dios as the opening of a distinctive English style of oceanic war.

Chapter VI: The Spanish Main — After the ease of the Nombre de Dios stroke, Drake attempts the capital of the Spanish Main itself; islands of San Bernardo; night entry toward Cartagena; raids and intelligence along the treasure coasts.

Chapter VII: Drake and the War Party — Lost years after Drake empties a Plymouth church until he reappears in Ireland in spring 1575; conjectural movements; return to a political atmosphere eager for peace after the Ridolfi crisis; alignment with the court faction pushing harder measures against Spain.

Chapter VIII: The Voyage of Circumnavigation — Secret aims of Drake’s exalted promoters; Spanish suspicions that the “Alexandria currants” cover story is false; departure and the Atlantic leg of 1577–78.

Chapter IX: The Voyage of Circumnavigation (continued) — Crisis in St. Julian’s Bay; the Doughty tragedy; Drake as a matured, harsher leader; the passage toward the Strait and the Pacific.

Chapter X: The Voyage of Circumnavigation (continued) — The voyage “made”: Golden Hind ballasted with silver; Anton’s question on the return route; Drake’s chart of four ways home (including Moluccas and the Cape); the homeward design.

Chapter XI: Knighthood — England’s year of waiting; Wynter’s return and reception; reports of Magellan’s Strait and of Doughty’s death; Drake’s reappearance and the queen’s recognition—knighthood as official seal on the new oceanic prestige.

Chapter XII: The Navy of Elizabeth — Drake on the Royal Commission into the Navy Office; complaints of mismanagement and dishonesty; Sir Thomas Cotton and other critics; institutional reform as the counterpart to Drake’s personal glory.

Volume I Appendices (selected) — A. Birth, parentage, and early years; B. Authorities for Hawkins’s third voyage, with Spanish official report on San Juan de Ulúa; C. further documentary notes on Drake’s early services (as listed in the volume TOC).

Volume II

Chapter I: The Indies Voyage 1585 — Navy reorganisation not completed before England stands on the brink of war; forces beyond even Elizabeth’s genius for delay; Drake’s commission and the West Indies expedition as the opening of open large-scale oceanic offensive.

Chapter II: San Domingo and Cartagena — Beyond official instructions, Drake pursues the real object of the expedition; plan of operations (with expected timings and plunder) drawn for Burghley’s information; sack and ransom of Santo Domingo and Cartagena as the campaign’s centrepiece.

Chapter III: Operations on the Spanish Coast — Home ovation and the revelation of the Babington Plot; assassination of Elizabeth and Mary Stuart’s cause; Cadiz and the Spanish littoral operations that “singe the King of Spain’s beard” and disrupt Armada preparations.

Chapter IV: Drake’s Plan of Campaign — End of the first stage of the war; criticism of Cartagena’s evacuation set against the brilliance of the preliminary campaigns; Elizabeth’s handling of the next stage; Drake’s strategic design for meeting the Armada at sea.

Chapter V: Attempts to Attack the Armada — The Plymouth fleet as the culmination of the long transition—Howard’s royal great-ships and pinnaces plus a formidable auxiliary squadron; efforts to strike the Armada before or as it completes its concentration.

Chapter VI: The Fleets in Contact — Combined fleet back at Plymouth (12 July); revictualling and readiness; false home reports that the Armada lies scattered; the first real contact and the Channel chase begins.

Chapter VII: Portland and the Isle of Wight — Fleets lying-to off Plymouth; Sidonia succouring injured ships; Howard’s council of war; Recalde and Salvador; Valdés’s foremast lost; fighting about Portland and the Isle of Wight as the Armada still advances largely intact.

Chapter VIII: The Battle of Gravelines — English seamen’s disappointment with mere Channel cannonades; anxiety ashore as the Armada seems to defy the best seamen; fireships, the Flanders shore, and Gravelines as the decisive fleet action; Corbett’s long view that three centuries later can call it one of the decisive military exploits of the world even when contemporaries felt dazed disappointment.

Chapter IX: The Lisbon Expedition — After Gravelines, the Counter-Armada / Portugal voyage; aims under Dom Antonio; Corunna and Lisbon; the campaign that undoes much of Drake’s political capital.

Chapter X: Drake’s Disgrace — The country counts an irreparable loss: Drake’s fall from the pre-eminence won by Armada service and earlier exploits; jealousy of “natural leaders” of the realm in arms; court and public judgment after Lisbon.

Chapter XI: The Last Voyage — Relief of Brest as nearly the last blow to the Spanish cause in France; languid war after Walsingham’s death (1590); Burghley’s cautious control; the final Drake–Hawkins Indies voyage; failure, sickness, and Drake’s death in the Caribbean.

Conclusion — Corbett’s closing assessment of Drake’s place in the rise of England as a maritime power and of the Tudor Navy’s transformation across the reign.

Volume II Appendices (selected) — A. Authorities for the Indies voyage, 1585; B. Authorities for the Armada campaign; C. Elizabethan tonnage measurement; D. Authorities for the Indies voyage, 1595; Index.
