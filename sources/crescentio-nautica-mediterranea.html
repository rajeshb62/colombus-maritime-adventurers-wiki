About the Author and Edition

Bartolomeo Crescentio was an Italian engineer in the service of the Papal States, styling himself “Ingegniero Pontificio.” He dedicated the work to the Illmo et Reverendissimo Signor Cardinale Aldobrandino (Cardinal Aldobrandini), Camerlengo of the Holy Church and governor-general of Ferrara and of the whole Ecclesiastical State. The opening address recounts the work’s slow composition: the Nautica Mediterranea was first given to the press some six years before completion, then long interrupted while the author was “impiegato” in various voyages and affairs, in the service of the Pope and of the Cardinal. Crescentio’s frame is classical — he opens on Plato’s ascent from the species to the genus, on Hero of Alexandria and the marriage of Platonic geometry with the Mechanics of Archimedes — and applies it to the practical geometry of the Mediterranean galley.

Summary

The Nautica Mediterranea is a systematic practical-geometry treatise on the design, building, rigging, and navigation of the Mediterranean war galley and its consorts, arranged in three books. It opens with the choice of place for building the armada, the qualities of timber (elm, oak, poplar, chestnut, larch), and the enumeration of the galley’s parts — from the keel (carena), frames (stamenali), and futtocks through the planking, the ferzi (the diagonal ribs over which the hull is planked), and the fastening system of treenails and iron. The first book carries the running theme of the parts of the galley, their measures and proportions. The second book turns to navigation: the compass (bussola) and its variation, the errors of charts — the Mediterranean and Lisbon charts “abbagliate” (blinded) by the false arrangement of meridians and parallels — the riformation of the rose, the poles and the tropic circles, and the longitudinal problem. The third book takes up the astronomical and practical rules of the mariner: the meridian, the two tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, the declination of the sun, the rising and setting of the stars, and the removal of “il prolisso colo de gl’Aprologhi” (the prolix error of the aphorisms) and of mariners’ errors.

Scope and Approach

Crescentio writes as a practising military engineer, not a historian: the work is prescriptive, technical, and heavily diagrammatic — the OCR pages are dense with the letter-references (A, B, C, D) of geometric figures. He is sharply critical of the current state of Mediterranean navigation, dismissing the errors of the common charts and of the mappi (plane charts) “abbagliate” by a false arrangement of meridians, and reporting the compass-variation rules of Gio. Battista della Porta, “commune amico nostro,” the Neapolitan natural philosopher. He addresses himself to working mariners, stating his intent to be understood by them rather than to dazzle the learned, and in the third book records that he taught a young mariner his rules to test whether his manner of writing was clear enough to be followed.

Front Matter and Dedication

Dedication to Cardinal Aldobrandino — Crescentio addresses the Cardinal, Camerlengo of the Holy Church and governor of Ferrara and the Ecclesiastical State, on the Platonic ascent from the particular to the general as the path by which the Ancients found all the sciences, and applies it to the reduction of the heavens and the measures of the globe to the small sphere of water and earth.

Address to the Reader — Signed by “Bartolomeo Crescentio Ingegniero Pontificio”; recounts that the work was first given to the press six years before, then long interrupted while the author served the Pope and the Cardinal in voyages and affairs; notes the friendship and correspondence of Gio. Battista della Porta.

“Quello che si ha da trattare in questo libro” — A table of the subjects to be treated in the book, framed as the author’s perpetual intent to be useful to others by procuring rules whereby man may preserve himself in safety on land and sea.

Libro Primo (Book One) — The Fabrica (construction) of the Galley

Capitolo Primo: Che luoghi si debbano eleggere per fabricar l’armata navale — Which places should be chosen for building the naval armada; the site must be sufficient for the senale (dockyard), the port, and the maestranza (workmen), with timber and victuals brought to it.

The running text then proceeds through the qualities of timber (olmo elm, quercia oak, faggio beech, chiuppo poplar, tiglio linden), the enumeration of the galley’s parts — carena (keel), impiatori, bracciuoli, bancacci, fodero (planking), the four matere stamenali (frame timbers), cordone, cinta, parme, contevali, contouali, the ferzi and their setting, the corsia (gangway), coperta (deck), tapere, raggioli, gioghi, corde, crocette, calaveme, draganti, baccalari, banchi, battagliuole — and on through the ship’s furniture: catene (chains), chiavi, sartie (shrouds), schiffo, alberi (masts), antenne (yards), gomeni, aguglie, metalame, pulegge (blocks), quarnari; then the making of the tacking and hoisting gear, the varodano (windlass) and the centura, the hoisting of the oar-crew from one galley to another, the parts of the sail, and the measures and proportions of each part of the vessel.

Libro Secondo (Book Two) — Navigation, the Compass, and the Errors of Charts

The second book turns from the fabric to the sailing of the vessel. It treats the compass (bussola) and its variation, the errors of the Mediterranean charts and of the Lisbon charts (“Le Carte di Lisbona sono pure abbagliate come le Mediterranee” — the charts of Lisbon are as blinded as the Mediterranean ones), the false arrangement of meridians and parallels on the mappi (plane charts), the riformatione della rota (reformation of the compass rose), the taking of the hours by night, the Gregorian reform and the need to add ten days to the computation, the poles, the two tropics, and the problem of longitude — recording della Porta’s rule for finding degrees of longitude by sailing under a fixed parallel.

Libro Terzo (Book Three) — Astronomical and Practical Rules of the Mariner

The third book gathers the mariner’s astronomical rules. It treats the meridian and the description of its limb, the two tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, the equinoctial and its cross, the declination of the sun, the rising and setting of the fixed stars by which the mariner reckons, and the correction of the errors of the common aphorisms and of mariners’ practice. Crescentio states his intent to be understood by working mariners, and records teaching a young mariner his rules as a test of their clarity.

Scan Completeness Audit

The digitisation runs continuously from the title page through the dedication, the address to the reader, the table of subjects, and the three books, ending with the last leaves of the third book. The 662 OCR pages are ordered and gapless, but the scan is a degraded optical character recognition of an early-modern Italian book: about 55 pages carry an explicit “low accuracy” banner, and many others are garbled or fragmented (the letters are broken, f and s are confused in the long-s orthography, and diacritics are scattered). Roughly 160,000 words of usable Italian text survive, enough to identify the work, its author, its three-book structure, and its principal subjects, but not enough to recover every chapter heading exactly. This source is usable for broad doctrine and structure; individual passages should be treated with caution and checked against a clean edition before quotation.
