Shipwreck on Jamaica (June 1503 – June 1504)

The Jamaica marooning: ships beached at St. Ann’s Bay (red X), Mendez’s 150-mile canoe route to Hispaniola via Navassa Island (green), Escobar’s reconnoitring trip (red dashed), and the final rescue ship (blue dashed). The Porras mutiny battle was fought at the east end of Jamaica (red star).

Overview

After the Veragua disaster, Columbus’s three remaining caravels — riddled by shipworms and leaking badly — limped toward Hispaniola. On June 23, 1503, he was forced to beach the two surviving ships on the coast of Jamaica. Columbus and his crew of about 120 men were marooned for over a year — a period that included a heroic canoe voyage for rescue, a desperate mutiny, and one of the most famous astronomical ruses in history.

The Beaching

The two ships were so perforated by the teredo navalis (shipworm) that they could not be kept afloat. Columbus ran them aground side by side on the north coast of Jamaica (near present-day St. Ann’s Bay), lashing them together and building cabins on their decks to shelter the crew. The beached ships became a floating fortress. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XV Ch. X]

Arrangements with the Natives

The local cacique, Huareo, and other Jamaican chiefs were initially friendly. Diego Mendez, Columbus’s resourceful and loyal servant, negotiated agreements with the caciques to supply the castaways with food — cassava bread, fish, and fruit — in exchange for trade goods. The system worked for several months, but the drain on local food supplies was severe. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. I]

Diego Mendez’s Canoe Voyage

Mendez proposed the boldest plan of the marooning: to cross the 150-mile open-sea channel from Jamaica to Hispaniola in a native canoe. With a crew of six Spaniards and ten natives, Mendez paddled along the Jamaican coast, crossed the channel to the island of Navassa, and from there reached the western tip of Hispaniola. The journey took four days of desperate paddling under a blazing sun, with only rainwater to drink. Mendez then walked across Hispaniola to San Domingo, a journey of many weeks, to beg Ovando for a rescue ship. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. V]

Ovando’s Delay

Ovando, the governor of Hispaniola, was in no hurry to rescue Columbus. He detained Mendez for months and dispatched only a small caravel under Diego de Escobar to reconnoitre — not to rescue. Escobar appeared off Jamaica, exchanged a few words with Columbus, left a barrel of wine and a side of bacon, and sailed away without taking a single castaway aboard. The message was clear: Ovando was prepared to let Columbus rot. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. IV]

The Mutiny of Porras

Months passed. The castaways’ provisions ran low. Two brothers — Francisco de Porras and Diego de Porras — organized a mutiny. Led by Francisco, the mutineers seized ten canoes and attempted to paddle to Hispaniola. When contrary winds forced them back to Jamaica, they established a separate camp, plundered the natives for food, and turned the local population against Columbus by their violent behaviour. The supply of food to the Admiral’s camp dried up. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. II]

The Lunar Eclipse Stratagem

With starvation imminent, Columbus devised one of his most famous stratagems. Using an astronomical almanac, he knew that a total lunar eclipse was due on February 29, 1504. He summoned the caciques to a meeting on that night and announced that God was displeased with their failure to supply the Spaniards with food. To prove it, he said, the moon would rise that night “inflamed with wrath” — a sign of divine punishment.

As the moon rose in eclipse, reddened by the earth’s shadow, the caciques were terrified. They fell at Columbus’s feet, begging him to intercede with his God. Columbus retired to his cabin, “prayed,” and timed the eclipse’s end. When the moon emerged, he announced that God had been appeased. The food deliveries resumed immediately. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. III]

The Battle on the Beach

The mutineers, even after the eclipse, refused to submit. In May 1504, Bartholomew Columbus led the loyal Spaniards against Porras and his followers in a pitched battle on the beach. Bartholomew fought with desperate courage, personally capturing Francisco de Porras after a hand-to-hand struggle. The mutineers were defeated and taken prisoner. Columbus, showing a clemency remarkable given the circumstances, pardoned them all. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVI Ch. VI]

The Rescue

In June 1504 — over a year after the shipwreck — Ovando finally sent a ship under Diego de Salcedo to rescue Columbus. Columbus, his brother Bartholomew, his son Fernando, and the loyal remnant of the crew were taken aboard and sailed for Hispaniola. Diego Mendez, who had engineered the rescue, returned with them. Columbus arrived at San Domingo in August 1504, his health shattered, his authority gone, his fourth voyage at an end. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. I]

Significance

The shipwreck on Jamaica was the last great ordeal of Columbus’s life. The year of marooning tested his leadership, his seamanship, and his psychological resilience to the breaking point. The lunar eclipse trick became one of the most famous episodes in the Age of Discovery — a demonstration that knowledge could be power in the most desperate of circumstances. The episode also exposed the depth of the enmity between Columbus and the Spanish Crown: Ovando’s deliberate delay in sending rescue was effectively a death sentence that only Columbus’s ingenuity and Mendez’s courage prevented from being carried out.

Key Figures

  • Christopher Columbus — Admiral, leader of the castaways
  • Diego Mendez — Faithful servant, made the heroic canoe voyage for rescue
  • Bartholomew Columbus — Adelantado, suppressed the Porras mutiny
  • Francisco de Porras — Mutineer leader, defeated in battle
  • Diego de Escobar — Ovando’s reconnoitring captain who offered no rescue
  • Nicolas de Ovando — Governor of Hispaniola, who delayed rescue by months

Location

North coast of Jamaica (near present-day St. Ann’s Bay)
