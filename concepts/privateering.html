Privateering in the Age of Discovery

Overview

Privateering — state-sanctioned piracy — was a defining feature of maritime conflict in the Age of Discovery. Privately owned ships, authorized by government commissions called letters of marque, attacked and plundered enemy shipping during wartime or undeclared conflict. Privateering blurred the line between legitimate commerce, naval warfare, and outright piracy.

The practice existed across all major maritime powers. French corsairs preyed on Spanish shipping from the 1520s, operating from Caribbean bases and Atlantic ports. Portuguese corsários targeted Muslim shipping in the Indian Ocean. English privateers under Elizabeth I turned it into a systematic instrument of economic warfare against Spain. The Spanish themselves licensed guarda costas (coast guards) that operated as de facto privateers. In the Mediterranean, the centuries-old corsair tradition continued between Christian and Muslim states. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

But privateering reached its peak in the Elizabethan era (1558-1603), when it became a cornerstone of English maritime strategy — simultaneously waging economic warfare against Spain and enriching the queen, her courtiers, and the seamen who risked their lives in the hazardous waters of the Spanish Main. Francis Drake was the most famous and successful Elizabethan privateer. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Pre-Elizabethan Privateering

French Corsairs in the Caribbean (1520s-1550s)

The French were the first European power to challenge Spain’s monopoly in the Caribbean. From the 1520s, French corsairs operating from bases in the Antilles and from French Atlantic ports attacked Spanish treasure ships and settlements. Unlike the English who followed, the French operated without any pretence of state authorisation — they were pirates in Spanish eyes. The Spanish responded by fortifying their major ports (Havana, Cartagena, San Juan) and establishing the convoy system for the treasure fleets. The French corsair tradition established the tactical patterns — small, fast ships, night attacks, alliances with local native groups — that English privateers would later adopt and refine. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Hawkins Family: From Trade to Privateering

John Hawkins of Plymouth was the crucial transitional figure between legitimate merchant and state-sanctioned privateer. On his three slave-trading voyages to the Caribbean (1562-1563, 1564-1565, 1567-1569), Hawkins operated in a legal grey area — the Spanish prohibited foreign trade with their colonies, but Hawkins insisted he was a peaceful merchant, not a pirate. His third voyage ended in disaster at San Juan de Ulúa (1568), where a Spanish attack under cover of truce destroyed most of his fleet. The young Francis Drake, commanding the 50-ton Judith, escaped with Hawkins but never forgave the Spanish.

The San Juan de Ulúa disaster transformed English privateering. Before 1568, English expeditions to the Spanish Caribbean were primarily trading ventures — they carried English goods and attempted to sell them to Spanish colonists. After 1568, the trading gave way to raiding. The English had learned that the Spanish could not be trusted to trade honestly; the only language they understood was force. Drake’s lifelong war on Spanish shipping was born in the flames of San Juan de Ulúa. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Portuguese Privateering in the Indian Ocean

While the English privateered in the Caribbean, the Portuguese had developed their own form of state-sanctioned maritime violence in the Indian Ocean. The distinction is important: Portuguese corsários were not independent operators like Drake but instruments of the Estado da Índia. The cartaz system — requiring all Indian Ocean ships to carry Portuguese passes — was enforced by Portuguese warships that acted as de facto privateers, seizing any vessel without a pass. Unlike the English system, where privateers operated for private profit with royal approval, Portuguese “privateering” was a direct arm of state policy, integrated into the bureaucratic structure of the Estado. ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

The difference reflects the divergent maritime strategies of the two powers. Portugal, with a small population but a centralised state, built a controlled, monopolistic empire based on fortified trading posts and enforced licences. England, with a weak crown but a strong merchant class, outsourced maritime warfare to private enterprise. English privateering was capitalism by other means; Portuguese state-privateering was imperialism by administrative fiat. When the English East India Company was founded in 1600, it combined elements of both traditions — the Portuguese model of chartered monopoly with the English model of private investment and profit-sharing.

Elizabethan Privateering

Overview

Privateering was state-sanctioned piracy — the use of privately owned ships authorized by government commission to attack and plunder enemy shipping during wartime or undeclared conflict. In the Elizabethan era, privateering became a cornerstone of English maritime strategy, simultaneously waging economic warfare against Spain and enriching the queen, her courtiers, and the seamen who risked their lives in the hazardous waters of the Spanish Main. Francis Drake was the most famous and successful Elizabethan privateer. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Legal Framework

Letters of Marque

Privateers operated under a legal instrument known as a letter of marque (or letters of marque and reprisal). These documents, issued by the Crown, authorized the holder to attack and seize the ships and goods of a specified enemy nation. Without such a commission, attacks on shipping constituted piracy, for which the penalty was death. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

As John Sugden notes, “Privateers were authorized by governments to raid enemy shipping during war-time, and they were the common recourse of nations whose formal navies were weak.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Fine Line Between Privateering and Piracy

In practice, the line between privateering and piracy was thin. The same man could be a privateer on one voyage and a pirate on the next, depending on whether he held a valid commission and was attacking authorized targets. Drake himself operated on both sides of this line at different points in his career.

His early voyages to the Caribbean in 1570-1571 were essentially piratical: “Drake was bent upon piracy. There was not a thread of authority for his raid, nor a commission from his government that would have made him a privateer.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

By the time of his circumnavigation (1577-1580), Drake was acting with the queen’s secret sanction — making him, in effect, a privateer: “If this interpretation is correct, Drake was in effect a privateer, a licensed private man-of-war, an instrument of government policy, rather than a pirate, but with the difference that war had not been declared.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Queen Elizabeth’s Role

Queen Elizabeth I was deeply involved in privateering ventures. She invested her own money in expeditions, including Drake’s circumnavigation, to which she contributed 1,000 crowns. She shared in the profits when voyages succeeded and took a share of captured treasure. This gave her a direct financial stake in the plunder of Spanish shipping. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

However, Elizabeth was also cautious. She often sought to maintain plausible deniability, issuing secret verbal instructions rather than formal commissions. When Drake returned from his circumnavigation laden with Spanish treasure, she was initially uncertain how to respond — ordering the treasure stored at the Tower of London, but then relenting after Drake’s backers (including Walsingham, Leicester, and Hatton) refused to sign the order. After a six-hour private interview with Drake, she resolved to protect him and his treasure. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Spanish Main

The Prime Hunting Ground

The Caribbean — known to the Spanish as the Spanish Main (Tierra Firme) — was the primary theatre of English privateering. It was the conduit through which the wealth of the New World flowed to Spain. The key targets included:

  • Nombre de Dios: A town on the Isthmus of Panama that served as the entrepot for silver shipped from Peru. Silver from the mines of Potosi was carried by mule train across the isthmus from Panama to Nombre de Dios, where it was loaded onto the Spanish flota (treasure fleet). Diego Polo, a burgher of Cartagena, lamented that Nombre de Dios lived in fear and boasted little protection — “The most frequented road of this district is that from Nombre de Dios to Panama.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • Cartagena: The most important town on the Spanish Main, a major port on the Caribbean coast of modern Colombia. It served as the winter anchorage for the flota and was a center of trade, shipbuilding, and defense. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • The treasure fleets: The annual flotas carried vast quantities of silver, gold, emeralds, pearls, and other valuables from the Americas to Spain. These were the ultimate prizes for privateers.

Spanish Defenses

Spain’s defenses in the Caribbean were inadequate for the scale of territory they had to protect. The region was administered by the Audiencia of Santo Domingo, but warships were few. Oared galleons, though fast and maneuverable, drew too much water to work close inshore — precisely where French and later English privateers operated. As Sugden notes, “There were also too few Spanish warships to discharge the enormous responsibility of defending an area as large.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Drake as Privateer

Early Caribbean Raids

Drake’s first privateering (and piratical) ventures in the Caribbean began in 1570-1571. Until his arrival, “no Englishman is positively known to have entered the Caribbean intent on plunder.” Drake’s unusual genius marked these early expeditions. He used small, shallow-draft vessels that could operate in waters inaccessible to Spanish warships, and he gathered intelligence from escaped African slaves and local informants. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Attack on Nombre de Dios (1572)

Drake’s most famous early exploit was his attack on Nombre de Dios in 1572. With 73 men in pinnaces, he launched a night assault on the town, catching the Spanish off guard. However, Drake himself was wounded in the leg, and the attack had to be abandoned without securing the treasure. Despite the failure, the raid demonstrated Drake’s boldness and made his name feared along the Spanish Main. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Circumnavigation as Privateering Venture

Drake’s circumnavigation of 1577-1580 was, at its core, a privateering voyage. While it included elements of exploration (such as the claim of Nova Albion in California) and diplomacy (the trade agreement with Sultan Babu of Ternate), its primary purpose was to raid Spanish shipping on the undefended Pacific coast of South America. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The voyage’s greatest prize was the capture of the treasure ship Nuestra Senora de la Concepcion (nicknamed the Cacafuego), which yielded an immense haul of silver and gold. The total treasure brought back by the Golden Hind made Drake and his investors rich and contributed significantly to the English treasury. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Later Privateering Ventures

Drake continued to mix privateering with national service. His 1585-1586 expedition captured Santo Domingo and Cartagena. His 1587 raid on Cadiz was a pre-emptive strike against the Spanish Armada but also included instructions “to intercept heavily laden homeward-bound ships from the West and East Indies” — the privateering component of his commission. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Economic Significance

Privateering was both a form of economic warfare against Spain and a major source of national wealth. The profits:

  • Funded the English treasury, particularly in the decades before England had a standing navy capable of projecting power
  • Made fortunes for investors, including the queen, courtiers like Walsingham and Leicester, and merchants
  • Created a class of experienced, aggressive seamen who would form the backbone of England’s naval power
  • Drained Spain of the silver that financed its European wars

As Sugden observes, the view that Drake was “a great national hero” and the view that he was “primarily a pirate” have coexisted uneasily in the historical record. The fact is that Drake operated within the ambiguous framework of Elizabethan privateering — neither fully legitimate warfare nor simple piracy, but a system that suited the needs of a relatively weak Protestant kingdom confronting a mighty Catholic empire. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
