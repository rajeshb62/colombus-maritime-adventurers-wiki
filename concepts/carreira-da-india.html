Carreira da Índia (The India Run)

The regular sea route from Portugal to India around the Cape of Good Hope, established by Vasco da Gama’s first voyage (1497–1499). For over a century and a half, this was the lifeline of Portugal’s Eastern empire — an annual convoy system that carried men, goods, and royal authority between Lisbon and the Estado da Índia.

Route and Waypoints

The Carreira da Índia followed a well-defined sequence of waypoints, refined through experience across decades of voyages:

  1. Lisbon — Departure from the Tagus estuary, typically in March or April to catch favourable winds
  2. Canary Islands — First provisioning stop, ~700 miles southwest of Lisbon
  3. Cape Verde — Last European-controlled port of call; final fresh water and supplies before the Atlantic crossing
  4. South Atlantic crossing — Ships sailed southwest toward the Brazilian coast, exploiting the South Atlantic gyre; this volta do mar (return-through-the-sea) technique kept them clear of the doldrums and caught the westerlies
  5. Brazil coast — Often sighted near Cape São Roque or Bahia; occasional provisioning stops for fresh water and Brazilwood
  6. Cape of Good Hope — The treacherous southern tip of Africa, notorious for storms and heavy seas
  7. Mozambique Channel — Passage between the African mainland and Madagascar; the channel was the most perilous section of the route due to reefs, currents, and the potential for shipwreck
  8. Malindi — Friendly port on the East African coast where Gama secured an Arab pilot for the Indian Ocean crossing
  9. Indian Ocean crossing — With the monsoon winds, ships sailed directly east to the Malabar Coast of India
  10. Calicut / Goa / Cochin — The destination ports on India’s southwestern coast, where spices were loaded for the return voyage

The return route reversed this sequence but with critical differences: ships left India in December–January to catch the northeast monsoon, rounded the Cape of Good Hope between May and August, and reached Lisbon between June and September.^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The India Armadas: Annual Convoy System

The Carreira da Índia soon evolved from isolated voyages into a regular annual convoy system known as the India Armadas. Each year, a fleet of ships — typically four to six, sometimes as many as fifteen — departed Lisbon together for mutual protection and navigational support. The system was administered by the Casa da Índia (India House) in Lisbon, which regulated shipping, collected customs, and maintained the route’s charts and pilots.

The voyage took 6–8 months each way, meaning a round trip consumed at least a year and often longer. Ships that missed the monsoon window in India had to wait a full year for favourable winds, extending voyages to two years or more.^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Mortality and Risk

The Carreira da Índia was one of the most dangerous shipping routes in history. Between 1497 and 1650, approximately 900 ships sailed the route; of these, a significant fraction were lost to:

  • Scurvy — The greatest killer. Crews subsisted on salt meat, ship’s biscuit, and limited fresh provisions. On long voyages, vitamin C deficiency caused debilitating illness and death. Mortality rates of 30–50% per voyage were not uncommon.
  • Storms — The Cape of Good Hope, in particular, earned its name only after the fact; Bartolomeu Dias originally called it the Cape of Storms (Cabo das Tormentas). Winter storms in the South Atlantic and Indian Ocean could sink entire fleets.
  • Shipwreck on reefs — The Mozambique Channel, with its poorly charted shoals and coral reefs, claimed many ships.
  • Piracy and enemy action — English and Dutch privateers attacked returning ships in the 17th century, particularly after the union of the Spanish and Portuguese crowns (1580–1640).

Subrahmanyam notes that the high mortality shaped the social character of the India service: men who undertook the voyage were seen as gambling with their lives for the chance at fortune, and the Estado da Índia was perpetually short of experienced personnel.^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Significance

The Carreira da Índia was the backbone of Portugal’s Asian empire. It carried:

  • Outward: Soldiers, administrators, missionaries, trade goods (copper, silver, coral, cloth), and royal orders
  • Inward: Pepper, cinnamon, cloves, nutmeg, ginger, silk, porcelain, precious stones, and spices — the cargo that made Portugal one of the wealthiest nations in Europe

The route remained in use, in declining form, well into the 17th century, until the Dutch and English East India Companies captured the Cape and disrupted Portuguese shipping. The last regular India Armada sailed in the mid-1600s, but the route itself was used by Portuguese vessels into the 18th century.^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]
