Cartography in the Age of Discovery

The transformation of world cartography between 1488 and the early 17th century, as empirical knowledge brought back by Portuguese, Spanish, Flemish, and English navigators shattered the Ptolemaic model of the world and established modern cartographic conventions. This page documents the cartographic record preserved in Ravenstein’s Appendix G to the Hakluyt Society edition of the Roteiro of Vasco da Gama’s first voyage, and traces later developments in Flemish and English cartography through the Elizabethan era.

Introduction

Before Vasco da Gama’s voyage of 1497-1499, European cartography rested on two foundations: the geography of Claudius Ptolemy, compiled in Alexandria in the second century AD, and the practical portolan charts used by Mediterranean mariners. Ptolemy’s Geography, rediscovered in the West in the early fifteenth century, provided a mathematical framework of latitude and longitude but also propagated major errors — most influentially, the idea that the Indian Ocean was a closed sea, landlocked by an unknown southern continent connecting Africa to Southeast Asia. The portolan charts, by contrast, were empirically accurate for the Mediterranean and Black Sea coastlines but extended only as far south as Cape Bojador on the African coast. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Portuguese discoveries in the fifteenth century gradually extended the known coastline of Africa southward. Prince Henry the Navigator’s captains pushed past Cape Bojador in 1434, reaching the Cape Verde Islands by 1456 and the Gold Coast by 1471. Diogo Cão explored the Congo River and the coast of present-day Angola in the 1480s, planting padrões (stone pillars bearing the Portuguese royal arms) to mark his farthest points. But it was Bartolomeu Dias’s rounding of the Cape of Good Hope in 1488 that shattered the Ptolemaic barrier and demonstrated that Africa could be circumnavigated. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

Gama’s voyage completed what Dias had begun. The Roteiro — the ship’s journal kept by the expedition — recorded latitudes, place-names, bearings, and distances along the entire route from Lisbon around the Cape, up the East African coast, across the Indian Ocean to Calicut, and back. This empirical data, combined with information from Arab pilots and Indian Ocean merchants, rapidly transformed the European world map. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Maps Illustrated in Ravenstein’s Appendix G

Ravenstein’s Appendix G (37,882 characters of the original text) examines seven contemporary maps and one compiled list of place-names that together document how the world map changed under the impact of Gama’s voyage. None of the actual sailing charts prepared by Gama’s pilots survive — the appendix works from compilations that, although contemporary, also incorporate materials from other navigators. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 203-214]

A key difficulty noted by Ravenstein is the small scale of all available charts, which forced compilers to select only a subset of the names found on the larger charts at their disposal. A further difficulty arises from the “crabbed characters employed by the map draughtsmen of the early part of the sixteenth century: a difficulty all the more serious when these illegible characters had to be reproduced by Italians having no knowledge of the language of the documents they used, or the meaning of the uncouth names which they were called upon to copy.” ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 204]

Henricus Martellus Germanus (c. 1489)

Henricus Martellus Germanus was an Italian cartographer working in Florence. His world map of 1489 is one of many in a manuscript called Insularium illustratum, now in the British Museum (Add. MS. 15760). It is described by Ravenstein as “very roughly drawn and without a scale,” dated to 1489 — almost immediately after the return of Bartolomeu Dias in December 1488. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 204]

The map is fundamentally a pre-Gama world picture. For the Indian Ocean, it follows Ptolemy’s closed-sea model. The African coast beyond the Cape is very incorrectly drawn and contains only six names:

  • Golfo dentro delle serre (False Bay, within the mountain ranges)
  • Rio della vacche (Gouritz River, the “River of Cows”)
  • Cavo dalhado (talhado) (Seal Point, “Cut Cape”)
  • Golfo de Pastori (St. Francis Bay, “Gulf of Shepherds”)
  • Padrom de S. George (erroneously given as Saint George instead of São Gregório, the padrão planted by Dias)
  • Ilha de fonte (instead of Ilha do Infante, Dias’s farthest point)

The map thus records only what Dias had discovered but renders it inaccurately. Its primary value is as the earliest cartographic document showing the coast beyond the Cape, however imperfectly. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 204-205; also referenced in footnotes at pp. 192-193]

Juan de la Cosa (1500)

Compiled by Juan de la Cosa, the famous pilot of Christopher Columbus, in 1500. This is the earliest known European world map to show the Americas — it depicts the coastline of the New World from the discoveries of Columbus and the Pinzon brothers. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 205]

Ravenstein notes that Cosa was “fairly well informed of the discoveries made by his own countrymen, but knew apparently but little about those of the Portuguese.” The map correctly shows the coast explored by Vicente Yanez Pinzon (who returned to Spain on September 30, 1500) to the west of the Rostro Hermoso (Cabo de Agostinho). However, Santa Cruz, discovered by Cabral in April 1500, is incorrectly indicated, despite Gaspar de Lemos having brought news of the discovery to Portugal three months before Pinzon’s return. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 205-206]

Two groups of imaginary islands appear in the southern Atlantic: “thebas, yslas tibras etiopicas yn mare oceanum austral” (latitude 1° 40’ S) and “Y. tausens, yslas tausens montises etiopicus oceanas” (latitude 15° S). Ravenstein warns that these could be mistaken for the islands actually discovered by João da Nova in 1501-1502, though they are purely products of the mapmaker’s imagination. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 205]

Of Gama’s expedition, Cosa seems to have been very ill-informed. Among the many uncouth and incomprehensible names inserted along the East African coast, not one can be traced to Gama’s voyage. Even such well-known places as Sofala, Quiloa (Kilwa), Moçambique (Mozambique), and Mombaça (Mombasa) cannot be identified. Zanzibar and Madagascar lie far out in the Indian Ocean. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 206]

The coastline of the Indian Ocean on Cosa’s map is Ptolemaic — there is no hint of the peninsular shape of India, making the map inferior in this respect to the Catalan Atlas of more than a century earlier. The only indication that Gama had visited these seas is the name “Calicut” placed on the south coast of Caramania (Kerman, in modern Iran), with a legend to the east of it: “tierra descubierto por el rey dom Manuel rey de Portugal.” ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 206; a fine facsimile of the map was published in Madrid in 1892 by Antonio Canovas Vallego and Prof. Traynor]

Dr. Hamy’s Chart (c. 1502)

An anonymous Portuguese chart purchased at a sale in London and now in the possession of Dr. Hamy, who published a description with facsimiles in his Etudes historiques et geographiques (Paris, 1896). The author is unknown. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 206]

The chart records the discoveries of Vicente Yanez Pinzon, Pedro Alvares Cabral, Sancho de Toar, and Gaspar Corte-Real, as well as the expedition King Manuel sent to Brazil in 1501 (accompanied by Amerigo Vespucci), which explored the American coast as far as the Rio de Cananea, latitude 25° 45’ S. The author knows nothing of the discoveries of Joao da Nova, who returned to Lisbon on September 11, 1502, allowing the map to be confidently dated to 1502, as Dr. Hamy does. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 206-207; cites A. Galvano, The Discoveries of the World, Hakluyt Society, 1862, p. 98, and The Letters of Vespucci, trans. Sir C. R. Markham, 1894]

The most curious feature of this map is its double equator. The equator for the western half of the map is the “new” equator, to which the recent Spanish and Portuguese discoveries are referred. The equator for the eastern half lies four degrees to the north and is taken from Ptolemy. The outline of the Indian Ocean is Ptolemy’s, as is the nomenclature, with a few exceptions. However, in the southeast, the author has broken through Ptolemy’s encircling barrier and opened a way from the Indicum Mare to an outer ocean, where room has been found for Seilam (Ceylon), Iava (Java), and far Quinsai (Hangzhou). The eastern edge of his Oekumene lies 205° east of Lisbon (196° E of Greenwich), carrying the map almost to Hawaii. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 207]

Within the Indian Ocean, the only original features are a peninsular India projecting from Ptolemy’s old coastline to the west of Taprobana, with a town called “Colochuti,” and the islands of Madagascar and Tangibar lying far out at sea in latitude 20° S. The only other modern name in this wide area is “Malacha” (Malacca), placed in the Aurea Chersonesus. Along the African coast, the nomenclature is fairly full and taken from original sources, but the spelling is corrupt and the letters frequently illegible. A legend to the south of Mozambique reads “questo avemo visto” (“this we have seen”), pointing to the use of an original sailing chart. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 207-208]

The Cantino Planisphere (1502)

The Cantino Planisphere is the most important early map of Portuguese discoveries. It was commissioned by Alberto Cantino, the correspondent of Hercules d’Este, Duke of Ferrara, who caused it to be “specially designed for his patron, at Lisbon, and for which he paid twelve golden ducats.” The map was begun after Corte-Real’s return (October 11, 1501) and completed before November 1502. The manuscript is deposited in the Biblioteca Estense at Modena. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 208]

The Cantino chart represents Portuguese cartographic knowledge at its most comprehensive and secret — maps of this kind were state secrets, and its procurement by Cantino was effectively an act of industrial espionage. The map was smuggled out of Portugal to Ferrara and was unknown to the Portuguese court until after it had left the country. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 208-209; see also Harrisse, Les Cortereals, Paris, 1883, and Sir Clements R. Markham’s Journal of Christopher Columbus, London, 1893]

The outline of Africa on the Cantino chart is “wonderfully correct, considering the age of the chart, and the broad face presented by that continent to the south is brought out most satisfactorily.” However, several names along the south coast are illegible even on the original, due to the coloured Table Mountain. Along the east coast, there is a paucity of names. Ravenstein notes that the padrões and Portuguese flags have been located somewhat at hazard. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 208]

The chart shows:

  • The complete Gama route to India, including Calicut
  • The African coastline with unprecedented accuracy, from the Gulf of Guinea to Mogadishu
  • The coast of Brazil, discovered by Cabral in 1500
  • The Line of Tordesillas demarcation, dividing the Atlantic between Spanish and Portuguese spheres
  • The Indian coast and Further India, largely based on native information

While the African coast is taken exclusively from recent Portuguese sources, the coasts of India and Further India are based on native information. This is proved by some of the legends: at Chatigam (Chittagong), the map reads “esta em xi pulgados a o norte” — these “pulgados” or inches are the “isbas” of the Mohit, a mode of expressing latitude peculiar to the Indian Ocean. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 209]

The Cantino chart is the most detailed contemporary representation of the Portuguese Estado da India in formation, showing the network of trading ports, entrepots, and strategic points that Portugal was then in the process of acquiring or contesting.

The Chart of the “Mohit”

The “Mohit” (or Muhit, meaning “Ocean” or “Encyclopaedia of the Sea”) is the Arab nautical atlas compiled by Admiral Sidi Ali ben Hosein, completed in 1554. Although it postdates the other maps in this appendix, it embodies local navigational knowledge accumulated over centuries and is not indebted to Portuguese charts for its superiority. Ravenstein reproduces Dr. Tomaschek’s elaborate reconstruction of a chart in accordance with the Mohit’s data. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 209-210; see Die topographischen Capitel des Indischen Seespiegels Mohit, von Dr. M. Bittner, Vienna, 1897]

The Mohit represents Indian Ocean navigational knowledge from the Islamic maritime tradition — the other side of the encounter. Where Portuguese charts depicted the ocean from the perspective of European arrivals sailing around Africa, the Mohit embodied generations of Arab, Persian, and Indian pilotage based on:

  • Monsoon wind patterns, whose seasonal reversal governed all Indian Ocean navigation
  • Star altitudes measured with the kamal, a simple wooden device for measuring the altitude of the Pole Star to determine latitude
  • Coastal rhumb lines connecting the major ports of East Africa, Arabia, India, and Southeast Asia
  • The system of “isbas” or finger-widths of the Pole Star altitude, the unit used in the Cantino chart’s legend for Chittagong

The Mohit’s chart includes place-names from the entire Indian Ocean rim: from Sofala and Mombasa on the African coast to Hormuz, Diu, Calicut, Ceylon, Malacca, and the Spice Islands. It extends eastward to China (Gor, Ainam), Java (Jawa), and the Moluccas (Wak Wak). The system of latitudes expressed in isbas (finger-breadths of the Pole Star) provided a practical navigational grid that Arab pilots had used for generations. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 209]

Ravenstein notes that even Ptolemy seems to have been in possession of some of these Indian sailing charts, and suggests that the monstrous size of Ptolemy’s Taprobana (Ceylon) is due to his having mistaken the horizontal lines crossing these charts for parallels drawn at intervals of a degree. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 209-210, footnote]

The Mohit’s data was used by the Arab pilots who guided Portuguese ships across the Indian Ocean — most famously, the anonymous pilot who took Gama’s fleet from Malindi to Calicut in 1498. The incorporation of this indigenous knowledge system into European cartography is one of the most significant but least visible aspects of the cartographic revolution of the period.

Canerio (c. 1504)

The chart of Nicolas de Canerio, a Genoese cartographer, is dated to approximately 1502-1504 — before the results of Joao da Nova’s voyage had become available. The manuscript is in the Hydrographic Office at Paris. It was described by Prof. L. Gallois in the Bulletin of the Geographical Society of Lyons (1890). ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 210-211]

The map shows that it is “almost wholly based upon the materials previously utilised by Cantino’s draughtsman, although more detailed in outline and with a more ample nomenclature in some places.” The shape of Africa, however, is far more correctly given on Cantino’s chart than on Canerio’s. Africa north of Mozambique measures 28° across on Canerio’s chart and 25° on Cantino’s, the actual breadth being 26° — Cantino is closer to the truth. The technical workmanship of the Cantino chart is also of a superior character. The legends on both maps were evidently taken from the same source, but those on the Cantino chart appear to be more numerous and in some cases fuller. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 210]

Canerio drew very largely on native information, but “he has not ventured to disassociate himself altogether from Ptolemy.” He made some use of Marco Polo, though he puts names taken from him — such as Murfulu, Var, and Coilu — in the wrong places. The island in the middle of the Indian Ocean, labelled “Y. rana,” is not one of the Mascarenhas as might be supposed but the “Illa Iaua” of the Catalan map — that is, Java. The legend accompanying it reads: “In this island is much benjoim, and silk and porcelain.” Still further south, three islands represent the Mascarenhas, then known by Malabari names. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 211]

Ravenstein provides the full text of the legends from Canerio’s chart (and a few from Cantino’s not found in Canerio’s), with translations. These include the description of Calicut as “the most noble city discovered by the most illustrious prince Dom Manuel, King of Portugal,” with lists of the spices, precious stones, and merchandise to be found there. The legendary description of Taprobana (Ceylon) notes it as “the largest island in the world, rich in gold, silver, pearls, precious stones, rubies, all kinds of spices, silks and brocades.” ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 212-214]

List of Place-Names

Ravenstein’s compilation of place-names covers the coast from the Cape of Good Hope to Malindi, with principal names beyond that point also included. The authorities are referred to by Arabic numerals:

  1. Henricus Martellus Germanus
  2. Juan de la Cosa
  3. Dr. Hamy’s Chart
  4. Cantino Chart
  5. Canerio
  6. Strassburg Ptolemy (1513)
  7. Duarte Pacheco Pereira (Esmeraldo de Situ Orbis, 1505)

Place-names with an asterisk are mentioned in the Roteiro itself. The dates in the first column are those of saints after whom localities were named. The list also incorporates names from Duarte Pacheco Pereira’s Esmeraldo de Situ Orbis, written in 1505. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 214-225]

The list tracks how toponyms evolved across different maps. For example, the Cape of Good Hope appears as:

  • 1: Cavo desperanza (Martellus)
  • 2: C. de boa esperança (Cosa)
  • 3: Cabo di bona speranza (Hamy)
  • 4: Cabo de boa esperança (Cantino)
  • 5 and 6: Cabo di bona speranza (Canerio and Strassburg Ptolemy)

The process of naming — sometimes corrupting, sometimes refining — is visible at every step. False Bay appears as “Golfo dnt delle serre” in Martellus, “Praia sabio (pablo?)” in Hamy, and “abaia” in Cantino. The list documents the progressive consolidation of geographic knowledge as names were transmitted, miscopied, corrected, and standardised across successive maps from 1489 to 1505. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 214-225]

The Transformation of Cartography (1488-1504)

The seventeen years between Dias’s rounding of the Cape of Good Hope (1488) and the completion of the Cantino Planisphere (1502) witnessed the most rapid transformation of the European world map since Ptolemy. In 1488, the southern limit of the known world was Dias’s padrão at the Rio do Infante. By 1504, European cartographers had a remarkably accurate outline of Africa, the coast of Brazil, the Indian peninsula, and the sea route through the Indian Ocean to the Spice Islands beyond. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The End of Ptolemy’s Closed Indian Ocean

The most fundamental change was empirical: Gama’s voyage confirmed that the Indian Ocean was open to the Atlantic, as Dias’s rounding had implied but not proven. Ptolemy’s concept of a closed sea, landlocked by a southern continent connecting Africa to Southeast Asia, was definitively refuted. On the Cantino and Canerio charts, the Indian Ocean opens to the south, and the sea route around Africa to India is clearly delineated. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 204-211]

Yet the Ptolemaic framework did not disappear overnight. Dr. Hamy’s chart still uses Ptolemy’s equator for the eastern hemisphere. Canerio has not “ventured to disassociate himself altogether from Ptolemy.” The Strassburg Ptolemy of 1513, based on Portuguese sources from 1502, publishes the new knowledge within the framework of a Ptolemaic atlas — the old and new worlds literally bound together in the same volume. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 207, 211-212]

Portuguese Policy of Cartographic Secrecy

Portuguese maps were state secrets. The crown prohibited the export of detailed charts and navigational data, fearing that Portugal’s hard-won geographic knowledge would benefit its European rivals. This policy of cartographic silence (the Portuguese crown’s deliberate suppression of navigational data) meant that the most accurate maps were locked away in the Casa da India in Lisbon and never published. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The Cantino Planisphere is the supreme example of how this secrecy was breached. Alberto Cantino, as the Duke of Ferrara’s agent in Lisbon, bribed Portuguese officials or cartographers to produce a copy of the most up-to-date official map and smuggled it to Italy. The twelve golden ducats he paid — a substantial sum — purchased not just a piece of parchment but the collective navigational knowledge of a decade of Portuguese exploration. The map reached Ferrara before the Portuguese crown knew it had left the country. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, p. 208]

From Portolan Charts to World Planispheres

The cartography of the Age of Discovery also saw a transformation in the form and function of maps. The medieval portolan chart was a practical navigational instrument: it showed coastlines, harbours, compass rhumb lines, and distances; it was designed for the pilot’s use at sea. The maps that illustrate Gama’s voyage — especially the Cantino and Canerio planispheres — belong to a different genre. They are world maps designed for patrons and princes, political documents as much as navigational aids. They display the extent of Portuguese dominion, the Line of Tordesillas, the flags and padrões marking claimed territories, and the trade goods available in each port. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 208-214]

The transition from the practical portolan to the political planisphere is visible across the seven maps in Ravenstein’s appendix. Martellus Germanus’s 1489 map, with its rough drawing and only six names beyond the Cape, belongs to the world of manuscript portolans. The Cantino Planisphere, with its legends listing cinnamon, pepper, cloves, sandalwood, rubies, silk, and porcelain at each Indian port, is a document of imperial ambition as much as geographic knowledge. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 204-214]

Ravenstein notes that placing the same charts side by side “almost looks as if they were not merely based upon the same original authorities, but had actually been slavishly copied one from the other, or from some common prototype.” Yet on closer examination this proves unsustainable: the latitudes and longitudes assigned to the leading points differ very considerably, showing that each compiler exercised independent judgment in reconciling discordant materials. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 211-212]

The Role of Native Knowledge

A theme that runs through Ravenstein’s appendix is the extent to which Portuguese cartographers relied on indigenous Indian Ocean navigational knowledge. The Cantino and Canerio charts both use the “isba” system (Arab finger-breadths of the Pole Star) for Indian latitudes. The Mohit represents an entire parallel cartographic tradition — the Islamic maritime science of the Indian Ocean — that was centuries old when the Portuguese arrived. Arab pilots who guided Portuguese ships, the anonymous pilot at Malindi being the most famous, brought this tradition into direct contact with European cartography. The result was a synthesis: Portuguese outline and coastal detail overlaid on Indian Ocean navigational grids developed by Arab and Gujarati pilots. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix G, pp. 209-210]

Later Cartography: Flemish and English Developments (1560-1600)

Gerard Mercator (1512-1594)

The Flemish geographer and cartographer. His world map of 1569 used the Mercator projection — a revolutionary cylindrical projection that preserved angles and bearings, making it invaluable for navigation. English navigators including Drake likely used Mercator’s maps. His atlas was published posthumously. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Abraham Ortelius (1527-1598)

Flemish cartographer who created the Theatrum Orbis Terrarum (1570), the first modern atlas — a bound collection of uniform maps. Ortelius corresponded with Mercator and was the royal cartographer to Philip II. His world map showed general understanding of the Americas and Pacific. Drake is known to have carried Ortelius-type maps. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Nicola Van Sype’s Map of Drake’s Voyage

A contemporary engraved map (c. 1583) showing Drake’s circumnavigation route around the world. It depicts the Golden Hind’s track across the Atlantic, through the Strait of Magellan, up the Pacific coast, across to the Moluccas, and around the Cape of Good Hope. One of the first widely circulated maps to show the English circumnavigation. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Cartography of Drake’s Voyage

Drake’s voyage contributed new geographic data: confirmation that the Strait of Magellan connected the Atlantic and Pacific (earlier maps had it as a closed bay), the extent of the Pacific, the location of the Spice Islands from an English perspective, and the California coast (Nova Albion). Hakluyt’s Principal Navigations (1589) and subsequent editions helped disseminate this geographic knowledge. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

English vs. Portuguese Cartographic Traditions

The Portuguese maintained a policy of cartographic secrecy (the Cantino chart was smuggled out). English cartography in Drake’s era was more open — the publication of atlases and voyage accounts made geographic knowledge widely available. This shift from secret portolan charts to printed atlases marks a fundamental change in how geographic knowledge was produced and shared. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
