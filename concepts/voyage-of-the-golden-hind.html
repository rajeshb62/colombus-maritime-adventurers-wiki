Voyage of the Golden Hind (1577-1580)

Drake’s circumnavigation route (orange). Plymouth → Strait of Magellan → Pacific raids (Valparaiso, Callao, Cacafuego) → Nova Albion (California) → Ternate (Moluccas) → Celebes → Cape of Good Hope → Plymouth. The outward route is shown solid, with key ports and raid sites marked.

Overview

Francis Drake’s circumnavigation of the globe (1577-1580) was the first English voyage to sail around the world and only the second complete circumnavigation in history, following the Magellan-Elcano expedition (1519-1522). It was a voyage of exploration, diplomacy, and — above all — privateering, combining the discovery of new lands with the systematic plunder of Spanish shipping along the Pacific coast of the Americas. Drake returned to England with immense treasure, was knighted by Queen Elizabeth I, and became a national hero. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Planning and Preparation

The Strategic Context

The voyage was conceived in the context of rising tensions between England and Spain. Philip II’s Spain controlled vast territories in the Americas and claimed exclusive access to the Pacific Ocean. Elizabeth I, while formally at peace with Spain, was increasingly willing to challenge Spanish monopolies by supporting English adventurers. Drake’s voyage was a deliberate provocation — a strike at the heart of Spain’s undefended Pacific empire. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Backers and Secrecy

The expedition was financed by a syndicate of investors including prominent figures at Elizabeth’s court:

  • Sir Francis Walsingham (the queen’s principal secretary)
  • Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester
  • Sir Christopher Hatton (the lord chancellor)
  • The queen herself, who contributed 1,000 crowns

No formal commission was issued; the queen’s sanction was given verbally. The voyage was shrouded in secrecy. A fictitious cover story was circulated that the ships were bound for Alexandria and Constantinople on a trading voyage. Even the mariners were recruited on that basis. “These devices seem to have worked, because it was not until after Drake had sailed that Spanish suspicions were aroused; even then none guessed his true destination.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Fleet

The Pelican (later Golden Hind)

Drake’s flagship was the Pelican, built in 1576 at Plymouth. She was later renamed the Golden Hind in August 1578 as the fleet entered the Strait of Magellan. The new name honoured Sir Christopher Hatton, whose family crest featured a golden hind (a female deer). ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Golden Hind was a small ship by modern standards — about 150 tons burden and approximately 80 feet from stem to sternpost. She carried an armament of eighteen brass and iron guns and was described as a good sailer with a sturdy hull, sheathed with extra planking to protect against rot. She was designed to carry up to eighty men. Despite her modest size, she would go on to become one of the most famous ships in maritime history, her portrait eventually gracing the British halfpenny. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Other Vessels

The expedition originally consisted of five ships:

  • Pelican (flagship, later Golden Hind) — ~150 tons, commanded by Drake
  • Elizabeth — ~80 tons, commanded by Captain John Winter
  • Swan — a flyboat, commanded by John Chester
  • Marigold — a pinnace, commanded by Captain John Thomas
  • Christopher — a small ship, commanded by Tom Moone

The fleet was later supplemented by a captured Portuguese ship. The company included mariners, artisans (coopers, carpenters, a blacksmith), and a complement of soldiers. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Voyage

Departure from Plymouth

The expedition made its first start in November 1577 but was forced back by bad weather. On 13 December 1577, Drake departed Plymouth for the second time. The fleet ran south-westerly along the coast of north-west Africa, quickly making it clear that the Mediterranean was not their destination. Some discontent arose among the crew, who had been told they were bound for Alexandria. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Atlantic Crossing

After calling at the Cape Verde Islands for provisions (obtained by force from the Portuguese), the fleet crossed the Atlantic and made landfall on the coast of Brazil. From there, they worked their way south along the South American coast toward the Strait of Magellan. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Strait of Magellan (August-September 1578)

The Cape of Virgins, marking the entrance to the Magellan Strait, was sighted on 20 August 1578. Drake held a ceremony, saluting the queen, conducting a religious service, and rechristening his flagship the Golden Hind. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The passage through the strait took only 14 days — remarkably fast for such a treacherous waterway. The Strait of Magellan was so dangerous that it had fallen into disuse by the Spanish. No European had ever been south of Cape Horn; the geography before Drake’s voyage simply did not know of it. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Storms and Dispersion

Immediately after clearing the strait and entering the Pacific, the fleet was struck by ferocious storms that scattered the ships. The Marigold went down with all hands. The Elizabeth, under Captain John Winter, turned back and eventually returned to England through the Strait of Magellan. Only the Golden Hind continued alone up the Pacific coast of South America. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Raiding the Pacific Coast

For the first time, an English ship was operating in the Pacific, which the Spanish considered their private lake. The undefended Spanish settlements were taken completely by surprise:

  • Valparaiso: Drake raided the port, capturing a ship laden with wine and gold.
  • Callao: The port of Lima, the capital of Spanish Peru. Drake entered the harbour and cut the cables of the ships anchored there, though he found little treasure.
  • Capture of the Cacafuego: Off the coast of Ecuador, Drake captured the treasure ship Nuestra Senora de la Concepcion, nicknamed the Cacafuego (“Spitfire”). She had left Callao for Panama richly laden with silver. The prize yielded an immense haul — the richest single capture of Drake’s career. The Spaniards irreverently rechristened her the Cacafuego; Sugden notes that from a prize Drake “learned that he was three days behind her,” but he soon caught up and “sent the Nuestra Senora de la Concepcion on her way” — emptied of her treasure. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Nova Albion (California)

After completing his raiding along the South American coast, Drake sailed north to avoid further Spanish pursuit and to search for a passage back to the Atlantic (the fabled Northwest Passage). He explored the coast of present-day California, which he claimed for England under the name Nova Albion (“New England”), “in respect of the white banks and cliffs, which lie” — a reference to the white cliffs of Dover. He erected a post with a plaque claiming the territory for Queen Elizabeth. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The English spent several weeks in the area, careening the Golden Hind and trading with the local Native American population. The specific location of Nova Albion was kept secret to prevent the Spanish from finding it, and the exact site (generally believed to be Drake’s Bay, north of San Francisco) remains debated. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Crossing the Pacific

From California, Drake struck westward across the vast Pacific Ocean. His navigational knowledge was limited for this region — “for many regions he had no guidance” — yet he successfully navigated to the Moluccas (the Spice Islands) in the East Indies. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Moluccas and Ternate

In the Moluccas, Drake made contact with Sultan Babu of Ternate, one of the most powerful rulers in the region. The sultan had no love for the Portuguese, who had driven him from his islands of Ternate, Motir, and Maquiam and were entrenched on neighbouring Tidore. Babu therefore welcomed the English as potential allies against the Portuguese. A trade agreement was reached, opening the possibility of English access to the valuable spice trade. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Drake was invited to the fortress of Ternate at the port of Talangam, where he was “well received and provided with certain supplies.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Grounding in the Celebes Sea

After leaving Ternate, Drake sailed westward, planning to pass around Celebes (modern Sulawesi) into the Celebes Sea and Makassar Strait, where “the greatest peril of the whole voyage” awaited. The Golden Hind grounded on a reef in the Celebes Sea. The ship was stuck fast, and the situation appeared desperate. “Ahead the greatest peril of the whole voyage” threatened, “the warm green seas that washed the east coast of Celebes, its ragged and unknown reefs.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Through a combination of skill, luck, and determination, the crew managed to free the ship by lightening her (throwing cargo overboard) and waiting for the tide. The Golden Hind survived, though it was a near-disaster. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Return Journey

The Golden Hind crossed the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and called at Sierra Leone on the west coast of Africa for provisions. From there, she sailed north across the Atlantic toward England. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Arrival at Plymouth

On 26 September 1580, fishermen working their nets in the English Channel sighted a small, heavily laden ship making for Plymouth Sound. As she passed, a man aboard hailed the fishermen with a strange question: “Is the queen alive?”

She was alive and in good health. The Golden Hind sailed home.

Drake had been away for almost three years. He had plundered the King of Spain, and his hold was full of treasure. His first concern was whether Elizabeth still reigned, for if the Catholic Mary Queen of Scots had succeeded to the throne, “his head sat loosely on his shoulders.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Aftermath

The Treasure

The treasure brought back by the Golden Hind was immense. The cargo of the Nuestra Senora de la Concepcion alone was valued at a fortune. The total haul made Drake and his investors rich and significantly boosted the English treasury. The treasure was eventually stored at the Tower of London, but only after a political struggle between Drake’s supporters (Walsingham, Leicester, Hatton) and those who favoured restitution to Spain. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Knighthood

Queen Elizabeth interviewed Drake privately for over six hours aboard the Golden Hind. She heard “stirring stories that the queen did not fail to appreciate, great tales of adventure, of the Elizabeth Islands and Nova Albion, of the capture of the treasure ship and the trade agreement with Ternate, tales the like of which none had brought her before.” On 4 April 1581, Elizabeth knighted Sir Francis Drake aboard the Golden Hind, making him the second Englishman to circumnavigate the globe. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Historical Significance

  • First English circumnavigation and second overall after Magellan-Elcano
  • Extended English knowledge of geography, particularly the Pacific and the Spice Islands
  • Established the viability of English long-distance voyages
  • Demonstrated the vulnerability of Spain’s Pacific possessions
  • Laid groundwork for future English claims in North America (Nova Albion)
  • Initiated English contact with the Spice Islands and the Moluccas
  • Made Drake a national hero and a symbol of English maritime ambition ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
