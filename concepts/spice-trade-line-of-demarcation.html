The Spice Trade and the Line of Demarcation

The value of spices in 16th-century Europe, the Venetian/Arab monopoly, the fall of Constantinople, Portugal’s Cape Route, the papal division of the world, Magellan’s westward end-run around Portuguese control, and the treaties that finally settled the Moluccas dispute. Drawn primarily from Bergreen’s Over the Edge of the World (Prologue and Chapter I).

The Value of Spices in 16th-Century Europe

Spices were the most precious commodity of the Age of Discovery. Bergreen likens their economic role in the 16th century to that of oil in the modern era: they drove the world’s economy, influenced global politics, and became inextricably intertwined with exploration, conquest, and imperialism. The mere mention of their names — white and black pepper, myrrh, frankincense, nutmeg, cinnamon, cassia, mace, and cloves — evoked the wonders of the Orient.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The economic stakes were enormous. A sailor who returned from the Spice Islands with even a small sack of cloves or nutmeg could sell it for enough to buy a small house and live off the proceeds for the rest of his life. A captain could expect not only vast riches and fame, but titles to pass on to his heirs and foreign lands to rule. Successful expeditions could yield profits of 250–400 percent.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The European climate could not sustain these tropical spices. The Iberian peninsula during the 16th century was in the grip of the Little Ice Age — too cold and too dry to cultivate cinnamon, cloves, or pepper. An Indonesian ruler was said to have boasted to a trader who wanted to grow spices in Europe: “You may be able to take our plants, but you will never be able to take our rain.”^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The Venetian/Arab Monopoly

Arab merchants traded in spices across land routes reaching across Asia and became adept at boosting prices by concealing the origins of cinnamon, pepper, cloves, and nutmeg. They maintained a virtual monopoly by insisting these precious items came from Africa, when in fact they grew in India, China, and especially throughout Southeast Asia. Europeans came to believe that spices came from Africa, when they only changed hands there. To protect their monopoly, Arab merchants invented monsters and myths to conceal the ordinary process of harvesting spices, making it sound impossibly dangerous to acquire them.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The spice trade was central to Arab life. Muhammad himself belonged to a family of prominent merchants and traded in myrrh and frankincense. Arabs developed sophisticated methods of extracting essential oils from aromatic spices for medical and therapeutic purposes, formulating elixirs and syrups including julab (from which the word “julap” derives). During the Middle Ages, Arab knowledge of spices spread across western Europe, where apothecaries developed a brisk trade in concoctions made from cloves, pepper, nutmeg, and mace.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Under the traditional system, spices reached Europe by slow, costly, and indirect routes over land and sea — across China and the Indian Ocean, through the Middle East and Persian Gulf. Merchants received them in Italy or southern France and shipped them overland to their final destination. Spices passed through as many as twelve different hands, each transaction driving the price higher. They were the ultimate cash crop.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The Fall of Constantinople (1453)

The global spice trade underwent an upheaval in 1453, when Constantinople fell to the Ottoman Turks. The time-honoured overland spice routes between Asia and Europe were severed. This disruption opened up new economic possibilities for any European nation able to master the seas. For those willing to assume the risks, the rewards of an oceanic spice trade — combined with control over the world’s economy — were irresistible.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Columbus’s Search for Spices (1492–1504)

While Columbus’s voyages are remembered for the discovery of the New World, Columbus himself believed to his dying day that he had reached the eastern edge of Asia — and that the Spice Islands lay just beyond the lands he had found. The search for spices was the explicit economic motive of all four of his voyages, and its repeated failure was one of his greatest disappointments.

The Promise of the First Voyage

Columbus’s contract with Ferdinand and Isabella (the Capitulations of Santa Fe, April 1492) specifically empowered him to “discover and acquire” islands and mainland in the Ocean Sea, with the explicit expectation of finding spices, gold, and precious stones. On his first voyage, every native report of gold was eagerly interpreted; every new island was scanned for cinnamon, pepper, and other spices. ^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt]

When Columbus reached Cuba in October 1492, he sent an embassy inland specifically to find the Grand Khan of Asia and the spice markets of the Orient. The embassy found a village of forty huts and a population that fled in terror — no spices, no gold, no Grand Khan. ^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt]

The False Spices

Throughout the first voyage, Columbus and his men collected bark, berries, and leaves they believed were valuable spices. Irving records that Columbus identified “cinnamon” from the bark of a local tree (likely Canella winterana or a similar species native to the Caribbean, unrelated to true Asian cinnamon). He found “pepper” in the form of allspice (Pimenta dioica) and chili peppers — pungent but commercially valueless in the European spice market. ^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt]

On the second voyage, Columbus brought seeds and cuttings of plants he believed were nutmeg and ginger. None survived the crossing. The Caribbean simply did not produce the high-value spices of the Indian Ocean. The only tropical products Columbus could ship back in quantity — cassava bread, sweet potatoes, cotton, and dyewood — were too bulky and low-value to pay the costs of a transatlantic voyage. ^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt]

Why Columbus Failed to Find Spices

The fundamental problem was geographical. Columbus had not reached Asia — he had stumbled upon a New World whose spice profile was entirely different from that of the Indian Ocean. The Caribbean pepper (Capsicum) was novel but competed with black pepper only as a curiosity. The “cinnamon” was a local substitute, not the true Cinnamomum of Ceylon. The profitable spice trade ran from the Moluccas (cloves, nutmeg), Ceylon (cinnamon), and the Malabar Coast (pepper) — all of which lay on the opposite side of the world from Columbus’s discoveries.

Vasco da Gama’s success in reaching Calicut in 1498 made this abundantly clear. While Columbus returned to Spain in chains in 1500, his claims of reaching Asia increasingly disbelieved, Gama’s pepper-laden ships were arriving in Lisbon to enormous profits. Columbus never accepted this. On his fourth voyage (1502–1504), he still believed he was approaching the Spice Islands from the east — a geographical delusion that motivated his desperate search for a strait through Central America.

Columbus’s Spice Legacy

Columbus brought back no significant spice cargo in any of his four voyages. The gold and pearls he found were real, and the Taíno people he enslaved made a political point, but the commercial driver of his expeditions — the spice trade — remained wholly out of reach. It was the Portuguese who reaped what Columbus had sown, sailing the route Columbus’s navigational techniques had helped pioneer, but reaching the real spice sources that Columbus’s geography had so fundamentally mislocated.

Portugal’s Cape Route (Vasco da Gama, 1498)

Portugal was the first European nation to exploit the sea for spices and the global empire that went with them. The quest began as early as 1419, when Prince Henry the Navigator established his court at Sagres, an outcropping of rock at the southern edge of Portugal. He attracted navigators, shipwrights, astronomers, pilots, cosmographers, and cartographers — both Christians and Jews — who designed the caravel, a small manoeuvrable vessel with triangular lateen sails borrowed from Arab vessels. By 1434, Gil Eannes had sailed past Cape Bojador, opening the African coast to exploration.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa (the Cape of Good Hope). Ten years later, Vasco da Gama retraced Dias’s route, reached Mozambique, and sailed east to establish an ocean route to India. King Manuel I of Portugal anointed himself “Lord of Guinea and of the navigation and commerce of Ethiopia, Arabia, Persia, and India.” Other European monarchs disparaged him as “The Grocer King.”^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Gama’s Impact on the Spice Trade

Vasco da Gama’s arrival in Calicut on May 20, 1498, broke the Venetian-Arab monopoly on the spice trade that had endured for centuries. Calicut was the hub of the pepper trade on the Malabar Coast — a bustling emporium where Arab, Gujarati, and Chinese merchants exchanged goods from across Asia. The Portuguese, under Gama and subsequent commanders, systematically redirected the flow of spices around Africa, bypassing the Red Sea and Levant routes that had funnelled Asian spices through Arab middlemen to Venice and the Mediterranean markets.^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The economic consequences were profound. Pepper (pimenta), cinnamon, ginger, cloves, and nutmeg — previously subject to the markups of twelve or more intermediaries between the Moluccas and a European kitchen — could now be shipped directly from India to Lisbon. Portuguese carracks arriving in the Tagus estuary with their holds full of spices could realise profits of 250–400%. This fundamentally shifted the economic centre of gravity from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic. Venice’s share of the spice trade collapsed from virtual monopoly to a fraction of its former volume within decades; Lisbon replaced Alexandria and Venice as the spice capital of Europe.^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Pope Alexander VI’s Inter Caetera (1493) and the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)

On June 7, 1494, Pope Alexander VI — a Spaniard born Rodrigo de Borja, of the Borgia family — divided the world in half, bestowing the western portion on Spain and the eastern on Portugal. Responding to appeals from King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella (the “Catholic Monarchs”), the pope issued papal bulls establishing a line of demarcation between Spanish and Portuguese territories. The line extended from the North Pole to the South Pole, located one hundred leagues (about four hundred miles) west of the Cape Verde Islands. Spain received exclusive rights to lands west of the line; Portugal was to keep to the east.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Rather than settling disputes, this arrangement touched off a furious race between the two nations to claim new lands and control trade routes. The bickering continued as diplomats convened at Tordesillas in northwestern Spain. The Portuguese prevailed on the Spanish to move the line 270 leagues further west, so that it now lay 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands (approximately 46° 30’ W by modern reckoning). This placed the boundary in the middle of the Atlantic, roughly halfway between the Cape Verde Islands and Hispaniola, and — critically — allowed Portugal to claim the newly discovered land of Brazil. Pope Julius II confirmed the changes in 1506.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The treaty created more problems than it solved. Cosmologists could not determine longitude — nor would they for another two hundred years — so the line’s location was impossible to fix. The treaty failed to specify whether the line extended around the entire globe or bisected just the Western Hemisphere. Maps of 1494 mixed geography with mythology, adding phantom continents while neglecting real ones. Bergreen describes the Treaty of Tordesillas as “not even a line drawn in the sand; it was written in water.”^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan’s Proposal: A Westward Spanish End-Run

A generation after Columbus, King Charles I of Spain (later Holy Roman Emperor Charles V) resumed the quest to establish a Spanish global empire. Ferdinand Magellan, a Portuguese nobleman who had been humiliated and rejected by King Manuel I of Portugal, offered himself and his plan to the Spanish crown.

Magellan’s proposal was elegant in its simplicity: if the Portuguese controlled the eastern route around Africa to the Spice Islands, Spain could reach them by sailing westward, finding a passage through or around South America. This would place the Moluccas within the Spanish hemisphere as defined by the Treaty of Tordesillas — or so Magellan argued. He brought letters from his friend Francisco Serrao, a Portuguese explorer who had reached Ternate in the Moluccas in 1512 and whose letters placed the Spice Islands far to the east of their true position, squarely within the Spanish sphere. Magellan displayed a globe or map depicting his planned route, invoking seven years of service in Portugal’s eastern empire.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan’s conception of the world was fatally inaccurate. Inspired by Ptolemy, he underestimated the size of the Pacific Ocean; his estimated route came to only half the actual distance. Had he comprehended the true size of the Pacific, its currents, storms, and reefs, it is unlikely he would have dared to mount the expedition.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The March 1518 Contract with Charles V

On March 22, 1518, King Charles I offered Magellan and his partner, the cosmographer Ruy Faleiro, a contract “regarding the discovery of the Spice Islands.” The terms were remarkable:

  • A ten-year exclusive on voyages to the Spice Islands (though Charles did not honour this promise, dispatching a follow-up expedition only six years later)
  • One-twentieth of all lands and islands discovered
  • The title of Lieutenant and Governor of said lands and islands, inheritable by their heirs in freehold
  • Five ships: two of 130 tons, two of 90 tons, and one of 60 tons, supplied for two years
  • Magellan’s salary of 50,000 maravedis plus 8,000 monthly (paid directly to his wife Beatriz by royal order)

King Charles enjoined Magellan to respect Portugal’s territorial rights under the Treaty of Tordesillas, but in practice the young king intended to bend the treaty to Spain’s advantage by insisting the Spice Islands lay within the Spanish hemisphere — a point impossible to either prove or disprove.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The total expedition cost was 8,751,125 maravedis, of which the king’s share was 6,454,209. The financier Cristobal de Haro, representing the Fugger banking dynasty of Augsburg, advanced much of the money at 14% interest, expecting a profit of 250%.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The 1529 Treaty of Saragossa

After Magellan’s death at Mactan and the return of the Victoria (the only ship of five to complete the circumnavigation), King Charles launched a series of follow-up expeditions to the Moluccas — the second Armada de Molucca under Loaysa and Elcano (1525), a third under Sebastian Cabot, and a fourth under Hernando Cortes from Mexico. All met with disaster. The second armada lost four of five ships; only 8 of 450 men returned to Spain. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Desperately short of cash from fighting the French and with the Fugger dynasty refusing further backing, King Charles could no longer fund expeditions. He turned to diplomacy, inviting Portugal to join a commission and asking the Vatican to arbitrate. Ultimately, in 1529, Charles borrowed 350,000 ducats from King Joao III of Portugal, pledging as security the Moluccas and all islands lying to the east. Both nations signed the Treaty of Saragossa, ending the epic struggle for control of the spice trade. Just seven years after Magellan’s voyage, facing bankruptcy, Charles gave up the Spice Islands and returned them to Portugal. “In matters of empire,” Bergreen observes, “everything had its price.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Gama’s Spice Trading: From the Velho Journal

The Velho journal provides the only first-hand account of the spices Gama encountered and the specific terms of trade.

The First Voyage: Samples, Not Cargo

Gama’s first voyage returned with relatively modest quantities of pepper and cinnamon — enough to prove the route existed but not enough to transform the Portuguese economy. The journal records that the real prize was the route itself: the Calicut spices — pepper, ginger, cinnamon, cloves, and nutmeg — were all available in abundance, and the prices were a fraction of Venetian market rates. The return cargo was largely samples brought to demonstrate the commercial potential: the spices Gama loaded, stored in the holds of the worm-eaten São Gabriel and Berrio, sold for sixty times the cost of the expedition when they reached Lisbon. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Spice Prices at Alexandria

Appendix C of the Hakluyt edition reproduces a price list from Alexandria, preserved in the Velho journal, showing the Mediterranean prices that the Portuguese aimed to undercut. The list includes the values of pepper, ginger, cinnamon, cloves, and other spices at the great Levantine emporium. These prices — the result of the twelve-hand chain of markups — were the benchmark against which Portuguese direct shipping would be measured. The price of pepper at Alexandria was roughly ten times the price at Calicut; the profit margin for direct shipping was enormous. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt, Appendix C]

The Second Voyage: Volume and Violence

On the second voyage (1502-1503), Gama returned with a heavily armed fleet of twenty ships. The objective was not merely to trade but to enforce. Gama bombarded Calicut, seized merchant vessels, and established factories at Cochin and Cannanore. The spices loaded on the second voyage were measured in tons rather than samples — pepper, cinnamon, and ginger filled the holds of the returning fleet. Subrahmanyam notes that the second voyage was the first time the Portuguese demonstrated they could move spices in volumes sufficient to break the Venetian monopoly, rather than simply proving the route existed. ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Portuguese Monopoly Enforcement: The Cartaz System

After Gama’s voyages, Portugal moved quickly to control not just the spice trade at its sources but the entire shipping network of the Indian Ocean. The instrument of this control was the cartaz system.

How the Cartaz Worked

Any ship trading in the Indian Ocean — Asian, Arab, or African — required a Portuguese safe-conduct pass (cartaz) proving that the vessel had paid the required duties and was carrying only permitted goods. Ships without a cartaz were subject to seizure, their cargoes confiscated, and their crews often killed or enslaved. The Portuguese navy patrolled the key chokepoints: the strait of Hormuz (controlling the Persian Gulf route), the coast of Malabar (the pepper ports), and the Strait of Malacca (the gateway to the Spice Islands). ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The cartaz system was enforced by a network of fortified trading posts (feitorias) — at Cochin, Cannanore, Goa, Malacca, and Hormuz — each armed with artillery and garrisoned with Portuguese soldiers. The Estado da Índia, as this network was known, was the first European colonial empire in Asia: a maritime empire of controlled ports and enforced trade routes, not territories.

The Carreira da Índia

The Carreira da Índia (India Run) was the annual convoy system that carried spices from India to Portugal. From 1503, Portuguese carracks sailed in convoy, protected by armed escorts, following the route Gama had pioneered: from Lisbon around the Cape of Good Hope, up the East African coast, and across the Indian Ocean to Cochin or Goa. The return voyage carried the year’s spice crop — pepper, cinnamon, ginger, and cloves — to Lisbon, where the Casa da Índia auctioned the cargo at fixed prices.

The convoy system was necessary because the spice route was vulnerable not only to storms and shipwreck — which claimed an estimated 30% of ships on the India run — but also to attack by rival European powers. The Carreira was the logistical backbone of the Portuguese spice monopoly, and it operated for over a century with remarkable consistency. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The Economic Impact

The direct shipping of spices eliminated twelve intermediaries and their cumulative markups. Lisbon replaced Venice as the spice capital of Europe. The price of pepper in European markets fell by more than half within a decade of Gama’s first voyage. The Portuguese crown’s share of the spice trade — collected through duties at the Casa da Índia — became the largest single source of royal revenue, funding Portuguese operations in Asia, Africa, and Brazil. ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

The English Entry: Drake at Ternate (1579)

Nearly a century after the Portuguese breakthrough, an English ship finally reached the Spice Islands. Francis Drake, on his circumnavigation of 1577–1580, arrived at the Moluccas in November 1579 — the first Englishman to reach the source of the spice trade.

The Ternate Alliance

Drake anchored at Ternate, one of the two great clove-producing islands of the Moluccas (the other being Tidore). The Sultan of Ternate, Sultan Babu, was a bitter enemy of the Portuguese, who had established themselves on neighbouring Tidore and were attempting to monopolise the clove trade. Babu welcomed the English as potential allies. Drake negotiated a trade agreement: the English would receive cloves and other spices in exchange for protection against the Portuguese. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The First English Spice Cargo

The Golden Hind loaded six tons of cloves at Ternate — the first English spice cargo from the East Indies. Drake also took on samples of nutmeg, mace, and other spices. The cloves alone were worth a fortune; they were carried across the Indian Ocean, around the Cape of Good Hope, and back to Plymouth as part of the most valuable cargo ever brought to England. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Significance

Drake’s trade agreement at Ternate was the first breach in the Portuguese monopoly on the Spice Islands trade. It demonstrated that the Portuguese could not defend their monopoly against a determined European rival. The cloves of Ternate, brought to London and sold at enormous profit, proved that England could enter the spice trade directly — a lesson that would drive the formation of the East India Company two decades later. The agreement also gave England an ally in the Moluccas, a foothold in the most lucrative trade of the age.

But Drake’s success was not sustained. His hope of establishing regular English trade with Ternate was cut short by the Spanish Armada and the Anglo-Spanish war. It would be the Dutch — not the English — who would ultimately break the Portuguese monopoly in the East Indies, establishing the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in 1602. Drake’s voyage proved the route existed; the Dutch proved it could be commercially exploited.

Pigafetta’s First-Hand Descriptions of the Spices and Spice Trade

Pigafetta’s journal, the only surviving eyewitness account of the expedition, provides the first detailed European descriptions of the spice plants themselves — their appearance, cultivation, harvest, and trade value. His observations at Tidor in November–December 1521 are the earliest recorded European descriptions of these commodities at their source.

Cloves at Tidor

Pigafetta went ashore specifically to observe the clove harvest: “That same day, I went ashore to see how the clove grows. The clove tree is tall and as thick as a man’s body or thereabout. Its branches spread out somewhat widely in the middle, but at the top they have the shape of a summit. Its leaves resemble those of the laurel, and the bark is olive coloured. The cloves grow at the end of the twigs, ten or twenty in a cluster. Those trees have generally more cloves on one side than on the other, according to the weather conditions. When the cloves sprout they are white, when ripe, red, and when dried, black.” He recorded that cloves are gathered twice per year, at Christmas and midsummer, and that each individual possesses his own trees “although he does not cultivate them.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Nutmeg and Mace

Pigafetta described nutmeg trees growing alongside the cloves: “The tree resembles our walnut tree, and has leaves like it. When the nut is gathered it is as large as a small quince, with the same sort of down, and it is of the same color. Its first rind is as thick as the green rind of our walnut. Under that there is a thin layer, under which is found the mace, which is a brilliant red and is wrapped about the rind of the nut, and within that is the nutmeg.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Ginger

“Ginger is found throughout those islands: we ate it green like bread. Ginger is not a tree, but a small plant that puts forth from the ground certain shoots a span in length that resemble reeds, and whose leaves resemble those of the reed, except that they are narrower. Those shoots are worthless, but the roots form the ginger, and it is not so strong green as dry. Those people dry it in lime, for otherwise it would not keep.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

The Sultan’s Palace and the Trade at Tidor

Pigafetta described the court of the Sultan of Tidore, who maintained a large house with two hundred women and a like number of female servants. The king ate alone or with his chief wife “in a high place like a gallery.” Trade was brisk: “for ten cubits of red cloth of very good quality, they gave us one bahar of cloves, which is equivalent to four quintals and six pounds (one quintal is one hundred pounds).” Other exchange rates included fifteen hatchets for a bahar, thirty-five glass drinking cups for a bahar, forty caps for a bahar, and one quintal of bronze for a bahar. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Loading of the Victoria

The Victoria loaded its precious cargo of cloves at Tidore. The ship was so heavily laden that the king of Batjan’s gift of ten bahars of cloves could not be taken — “the ships could not carry them as they were so heavily laden.” Before departure, the crew had to unload sixty quintals of cloves, fearing the ship would “break open.” On December 21, 1521, the Victoria departed with “forty-seven men and thirteen Indians,” its holds full of the spices that would prove the expedition’s commercial success and establish the value of the Spice Islands trade. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]
