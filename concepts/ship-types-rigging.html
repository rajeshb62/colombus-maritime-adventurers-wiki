Ship Types, Rigging, and Construction

The vessels, rigging, and construction practices of Columbus’s four voyages as described in Irving’s History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus. This page covers caravel and carrack/nao design, lateen versus square rig, construction materials and tonnage, shipworm damage, careening and field repair, and the remarkable dugout canoes of the Caribbean.

Caravel Design

The caravel was a small, highly manoeuvrable vessel developed by Portuguese shipbuilders in the 15th century under Prince Henry the Navigator, refined through decades of coastwise exploration along Africa. Irving recounts that the caravel’s design was crucial to the Age of Discovery: its shallow draught allowed it to enter rivers and harbours inaccessible to larger ships, and its rigging options gave it exceptional tactical flexibility.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book I, Ch. III]

Two of the three ships on Columbus’s first voyage — the Pinta and the Niña — were caravels. The Pinta was carvel-built and square-rigged on the fore and main masts with a lateen mizzen, making her the fastest of the three vessels. The Niña (originally named the Santa Clara) started the voyage as a caravela latina — lateen-rigged on all three masts.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book II, Ch. IX]

Lateen Rig: Windward Ability

The lateen (or latine) sail, a triangular sail set on a long yard at a sharp angle to the mast, gave the caravel its signature capability: it could sail closer to the wind than square-rigged vessels. This windward ability was essential for coastal exploration, allowing caravels to tack upwind along African coastlines and navigate narrow channels with shifting winds. Portuguese explorers had relied on this feature for decades before Columbus.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book I, Ch. III]

The lateen rig, however, had disadvantages. It required a larger crew to handle the long yard — the Niña carried about 20 men, smaller than the Pinta’s 26 — and its performance on long downwind passages was inferior to a square rig. The yard itself was heavy and cumbersome to shift when tacking.

Square Rig: Downwind Speed

Square sails — rectangular sails set on yards perpendicular to the keel — excel at running before the wind. They catch the following breeze efficiently and require less manual handling than lateen sails on long ocean crossings. On the Atlantic crossing, where the northeast trade winds blow steadily from astern, square rigs offered superior speed and required less constant attention from the crew.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I]

The Niña Re-rigged at the Canary Islands

The most instructive example of rigging flexibility is the Niña’s conversion. When the fleet reached the Canary Islands in August–September 1492, Columbus ordered the Niña’s rig altered from lateen to square:

“The latine sails of the Nina were also altered into square sails, that she might work more steadily and securely, and be able to keep company with the other vessels.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I, p. 5774]

This re-rigging transformed the Niña from a caravela latina (all lateen) to a caravela redonda (square on fore and main masts, lateen on mizzen). The change improved her performance on the open Atlantic crossing, where steady following winds made the lateen rig less advantageous. After the wreck of the Santa Maria, Columbus transferred his flag to the Niña for the perilous return voyage, and she proved remarkably seaworthy, weathering a violent storm off the Azores that nearly sank her.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I, p. 5771-5777; Book V, Ch. II]

Carrack / Nao Design

The carrack (Spanish nao, meaning simply “ship”) was a larger, heavier vessel built primarily for cargo and long-distance voyaging. The Santa Maria — Columbus’s flagship on the first voyage — was a nao of approximately 100–120 tons burden. Irving notes that on September 11, 1492, when about 150 leagues west of Ferro, the expedition encountered a fragment of a ship’s mast that “appeared to have belonged to a vessel of about a hundred and twenty tons burden” — close to the Santa Maria’s own size.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. II, p. 5905]

Hull Shape and Freeboard

The carrack was distinguished from the caravel by its rounder hull — a fuller, more barrel-shaped cross-section that increased cargo capacity at the expense of speed. Where the caravel was lean and swift, the carrack was broad and capacious. The Santa Maria had a deeper draught and substantially higher freeboard (the distance between the waterline and the deck), which made her drier in heavy seas but also slower and less manoeuvrable. The carrack’s higher sides made it harder for native canoes to board or attack — an advantage in the Caribbean, where the Santa Maria’s decks stood well above the water.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. VIII–X]

Forecastle and Aftercastle

Carracks were built with prominent forecastles and aftercastles (or sterncastles) — elevated superstructures at the bow and stern. The aftercastle housed the captain’s cabin and the steering platform; the forecastle provided a defensive fighting platform and shelter for the crew. These castles gave the carrack its characteristic silhouette — a high, bluff bow and an even higher stern — and contributed to its structural strength, but also made it heavy in the helm and susceptible to weather helm (a tendency to turn into the wind).^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I]

Square Rig on Fore and Main, Lateen Mizzen

The Santa Maria was rigged with square sails on the fore and main masts, a lateen sail on the mizzen mast, and a bowsprit with a small square sail (the spritsail). This was the standard rig for a large oceangoing vessel of the period: the two square sails provided the driving force before the wind, while the lateen mizzen helped balance the helm and improved manoeuvrability. The bowsprit sail added a small increment of speed and helped the ship stay before the wind. Irving describes her as a decked ship with heavy, sluggish handling — a characteristic that contributed directly to her wreck on Christmas Eve 1492 off the coast of Hispaniola.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I; Book IV, Ch. VIII]

The caravel’s shallower draught allowed it to enter rivers and harbours inaccessible to the larger carrack — a critical advantage when exploring the shallow coasts and river mouths of the New World. The carrack’s higher freeboard and greater cargo capacity made it better suited as a flagship and supply vessel, but its deeper draught and sluggish handling made it more vulnerable to grounding — which proved fatal for the Santa Maria.

Tonnage

The ships of Columbus’s first voyage were strikingly small by modern standards. Irving remarks on their small size as a mark of Columbus’s audacity: “To traverse this vast Atlantic, with three small barks, the largest of which did not exceed the size of an ordinary river craft of the present day.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I]

All three vessels were approximately 100 tons burden, though the Santa Maria may have been slightly larger at 100–120 tons. This measure of tonnage referred to the ship’s cargo-carrying capacity — the number of tuns (large casks of wine) the vessel could stow in its hold. The Niña was the smallest, at roughly 100 tons; the Pinta was similar; the Santa Maria was the largest at perhaps 120 tons. The ships measured roughly 50–70 feet (15–21 metres) in length.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I, II]

Construction Materials

Oak and Pine

European shipbuilders of the 15th century relied primarily on two woods: oak for the hull’s structural elements (frames, keel, planking) and pine for masts and spars. Oak was tough, durable, and resistant to rot when properly seasoned; it formed the backbone of the ship’s skeleton. Pine was lighter, tall, and straight-grained, ideal for the long, single-piece masts that carried the sails. Irving mentions that the ships were caulked with oakum (fibres picked from old ropes, impregnated with tar or pitch) to seal the seams between planks — a process that could be done carelessly or thoroughly, affecting the ship’s seaworthiness. At Palos, the caulkers employed on the vessels “did their work in a careless and imperfect manner” and absconded when ordered to do it over again.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book II, Ch. IX, p. 5424-5428]

New World Timbers

During the second voyage, Columbus’s men discovered in Cuba tall pines that Irving records as “enough to make masts for the finest ships” and “noble oaks” — a finding that suggested the New World’s vast shipbuilding potential.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. X (Cuba exploration), p. 8689-8692]

Worm-Eaten Ships of the Fourth Voyage (Teredo Navalis)

The fourth voyage (1502–1504) was Columbus’s most disastrous in material terms. The vessels were severely damaged by the teredo or shipworm (Teredo navalis), a wood-boring marine bivalve mollusc that infests tropical waters. The Caribbean Sea, with its warm temperatures, provided ideal conditions for the teredo, which could render a ship’s hull riddled and unseaworthy within months.

Irving records of one expedition that “his vessels being nearly destroyed by the teredo, which abounds in those seas, he had great difficulty in reaching Xaragua in Hispaniola, where he lost his two caravels.”^[source: irving_columbus_v3.txt, Book XIV, Ch. II, p. 4129-4132]

By June 1503, the two remaining caravels of the fourth voyage were in such a state that they could no longer keep the sea. The crews laboured constantly at the pumps, and even baled water out with buckets and kettles, but the leaks continually gained. Columbus made desperately for Jamaica, running the ships ashore at Port San Gloria (Don Christopher’s Cove) before they could founder in deep water:

“His ships, reduced to mere wrecks, could no longer keep the sea, and were ready to sink even in port. He ordered them, therefore, to be run aground, within a bow-shot of the shore, and fastened together, side by side. They soon filled with water to the decks. Thatched cabins were then erected at the prow and stern for the accommodation of the crews, and the wreck was placed in the best possible state of defence. Thus castled in the sea.”^[source: irving_columbus_v3.txt, Book XV, Ch. X, p. 8276-8284]

The teredo’s relentless destruction was a constant threat to wooden ships in warm waters. Its larvae burrow into exposed timber, growing into long, worm-like adults that honeycomb the wood with tunnels, weakening the structure until the hull can no longer hold water. The only remedy was frequent careening and sheathing — a practice that would later evolve into copper-bottoming in the late 18th century.

Careening and Field Repair

Careening was the standard method for repairing hull damage at sea. A ship was deliberately beached on a suitable shore at high tide, then tipped onto its side by means of cables and tackle so that the crew could:

  • Scrape the hull clean of barnacles, seaweed, and marine growth
  • Caulk seams with oakum (fibres picked from old rope) and hot pitch or tar
  • Replace or patch worm-damaged planking
  • Inspect and repair the rudder and sternpost

Irving describes careening during the second voyage while Columbus’s squadron explored Cuba:

“While awaiting the return of his ambassadors, the Admiral ordered the ships to be careened and repaired.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. IV, p. 8200-8201]

On making a fire to heat the tar for careening, the seamen found that the wood they burned sent forth a powerful odour and, on examining it, declared that it was mastic — a valuable fragrant resin.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. IV, p. 8255-8258]

Field repair was a constant necessity on all four voyages. The Pinta’s rudder, damaged in heavy weather, was temporarily repaired at sea and then replaced at the Canary Islands with a new one built on the spot after no replacement vessel could be found there.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. I, p. 5767-5773]

At the mouth of the Rio Belen (Bethlehem) on the coast of Veragua (modern Panama) during the fourth voyage, Columbus and his crew conducted field repairs and built a small settlement with houses of wood thatched with palm leaves. One caravel was abandoned there as unseaworthy after being stranded, decayed, and rotting in the river.^[source: irving_columbus_v3.txt, Book XV, Ch. VI, VIII–IX]

The ultimate repair-in-place was the beaching at San Gloria on Jamaica, described above, where the two wrecked caravels were converted into a habitable fortress that Columbus and his crew inhabited for nearly a year until rescue arrived in June 1504.^[source: irving_columbus_v3.txt, Book XV, Ch. X, p. 8265-8288; Book XVI]

The 96-Foot Dugout Canoe Measured in Jamaica

During the second voyage (1494), Columbus explored the island of Jamaica. Irving, drawing on the accounts of the Cura de los Palacios and the Historia del Almirante, records the remarkable indigenous watercraft encountered there:

“Their canoes were better constructed, being ornamented with carving and painting at the bow and stern. Many were of great size, though formed from the trunks of single trees; often from a species of the mahogany. Columbus measured one, which was ninety-six feet long, and eight broad, hollowed out of one of those magnificent trees, which rise like verdant towers amidst the rich forests of the tropics. Every cacique prided himself on possessing a large canoe of the kind, which he seemed to regard as his ship of state.”^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book IV (Cuba/Jamaica), p. 477-490]

This dugout canoe — 96 feet (29 metres) long and 8 feet (2.4 metres) wide, carved from a single mahogany trunk — dwarfs the ships of Columbus’s own fleet. The Niña, by comparison, was only about 50–55 feet long. The canoe could carry a large number of warriors and was clearly intended as a vessel of prestige and war, not mere transport. Irving notes the apparent paradox: the warlike Jamaicans had a more formidable maritime capability than the peaceful people of Puerto Rico, who “possessed very few canoes.”^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, p. 492-500]

This passage is one of the few places in Irving where the technological achievement of indigenous peoples is explicitly measured and compared to European standards — and the comparison favours the natives.

Gama’s Fleet: The First India Armada (1497)

Vasco da Gama’s first voyage employed a mixed fleet of four vessels, designed to combine the strengths of different ship types for the unprecedented challenge of the India voyage. The fleet departed Lisbon on July 8, 1497.

Carracks (Naus): São Gabriel and São Rafael

The two carracks — São Gabriel (flagship) and São Rafael — were the workhorses of the fleet. Portuguese carracks of this period, called naus (from Latin navis, “ship”), were larger, round-hulled vessels built specifically for long ocean voyages. Key design features included:

  • Hull: Broad and capacious, with a high rounded stern and pronounced bow. The round hull provided ample cargo space for provisions (enough for three years) and trade goods, at the cost of speed.
  • Rigging: Square-rigged on the foremast and mainmast, with a lateen sail on the mizzen mast. This was the standard oceangoing rig: the square sails drove the ship before the prevailing winds, while the lateen mizzen helped balance the helm and improved manoeuvrability in variable winds.
  • Castles: High forecastle and aftercastle (sterncastle), providing accommodation, a steering platform, and defensive positions.
  • Tonnage: The São Gabriel was approximately 100–120 tons burden; the São Rafael was somewhat smaller at about 100 tons.

The carrack’s deep draught and heavy construction made it seaworthy in the Southern Ocean storms that Gama encountered around the Cape of Good Hope.^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

Caravel: Berrio

The Berrio (sometimes called São Miguel) was a caravel of approximately 50 tons — half the size of the carracks. Caravels were smaller, faster, and more manoeuvrable than carracks, with:

  • Lateen rig: Triangular sails on two or three masts, enabling the caravel to sail closer to the wind than square-rigged vessels. This made the Berrio ideal for coastal exploration, entering harbours and navigating shallow waters.
  • Shallow draught: The caravel’s shallow hull allowed it to approach coasts and river mouths that the deeper-draught carracks could not reach.
  • Speed: The Berrio was the fastest vessel in the fleet, frequently used for scouting and dispatch duties.

The caravel’s smaller size meant it carried less cargo and fewer provisions, but its speed and windward ability made it an essential component of the mixed fleet. Nicolau Coelho, the Berrio’s captain, was the first to return to Lisbon (July 10, 1499), bringing the news of the voyage’s success.^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Storeship

The fourth vessel was a purpose-built storeship (a nau or larger transport) carrying additional provisions and trade goods. It was not intended to complete the voyage. After the fleet rounded the Cape of Good Hope, the storeship was stripped of its remaining supplies and burned at Mossel Bay on the South African coast, its crew distributed among the other three vessels. This was standard Portuguese practice on long voyages: a storeship accompanied the fleet through the most dangerous passage, then was sacrificed to reduce the number of hulls that needed to be maintained and manned.^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

Tactical Rationale of the Mixed Fleet

Da Gama’s fleet design reflected a sophisticated tactical understanding. The mixed composition offered several advantages:

  1. Redundancy: If one ship was lost, the others could continue. This proved critical when the São Rafael became too leaky to continue and was scuttled near Malindi on the return voyage.
  2. Complementary capabilities: The carracks provided cargo capacity and seakeeping; the caravel provided speed and inshore access.
  3. Mutual support: In unknown waters, ships could scout ahead, stand by for rescue, and share navigational information.

Subrahmanyam notes that the fleet composition was not accidental but derived from Portuguese experience in earlier West African voyages, where the caravel’s inshore capability and the carrack’s ocean-going capacity had been tested separately. Da Gama was the first commander to integrate both types in a single deep-ocean expedition.^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

The English Galleon (c. 1550-1600)

Design Evolution

The galleon evolved from the carrack in the mid-16th century, with a longer, lower, and more streamlined hull. English shipwrights (influenced by John Hawkins as Treasurer of the Navy) lowered the forecastle, reducing windage and improving stability. The length-to-beam ratio increased from about 3:1 (carrack) toward 4:1 (galleon), making faster, more weatherly ships. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Rigging

Galleons carried a square rig on fore and main masts, with a lateen mizzen. They typically had a spritsail under the bowsprit. The rig was similar to the carrack but with refinements — larger main sails, smaller topsails, and improved running rigging. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Armament

Unlike carracks which carried bombards on the forecastle and poop, galleons mounted broadside batteries of standardized iron cannon along the lower and upper decks. The Revenge (470 tons) carried culverins and demi-culverins that could fire through gunports cut in the hull — a design innovation that enabled the English broadside tactic. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Key Examples

Golden Hind (ex-Pelican) — Drake’s circumnavigation ship. About 100-150 tons, 18 guns. Originally a merchant galleon, converted for the voyage. Renamed at Port St. Julian in honor of Sir Christopher Hatton’s crest. ~100 feet length overall. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Revenge — A purpose-built warship galleon, launched 1577, 470 tons, 40+ guns. Drake’s Armada flagship. Considered the finest English ship of her era. Lower and longer than Spanish equivalents, with much heavier broadside armament. Later became legendary for Grenville’s last stand at Flores (1591). ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Victory — John Hawkins’s flagship at the Armada. Built in 1560s, rebuilt to Hawkins’s specifications. Carried a heavy broadside and was one of the largest English ships at 800+ tons. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Hawkins’ Shipbuilding Reforms

As Treasurer of the Navy (1578-1595), John Hawkins transformed English ship design: introduced the race-built galleon (longer, lower, faster), standardized gun calibers, improved the placement of gunports, and oversaw the construction of the fleet that defeated the Armada. His reforms gave England a decisive technological edge over Spain. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
