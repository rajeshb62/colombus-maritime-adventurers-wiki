Spanish Armada (1588)

The Armada campaign: outward route from Lisbon (red), retreat around Scotland and Ireland (dashed red), and the English fleet’s path (blue). Key engagements at Portland Bill, Calais fireship attack, and Gravelines.

Overview

The Spanish Armada of 1588 (Grande y Felicísima Armada) was King Philip II of Spain’s attempted invasion of England. It was the largest naval expedition launched against England in the sixteenth century and resulted in a decisive strategic defeat for Spain, marking the beginning of English naval dominance in European waters. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Historical Context

Causes of the Conflict

Tensions between Spain and England had been escalating for decades before the Armada sailed. Several factors combined to bring the two powers to open war:

  • English privateering: English seamen, led most famously by Francis Drake, had been raiding Spanish shipping and settlements in the Caribbean and along the Spanish Main since the 1570s. These attacks were often conducted with the tacit approval of Queen Elizabeth I. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • English support for the Dutch Revolt: Elizabeth provided financial and military aid to the Protestant Dutch rebels fighting against Spanish rule in the Netherlands. Philip regarded this as direct interference in his sovereign territory. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • Execution of Mary Queen of Scots (1587): The execution of the Catholic claimant to the English throne removed any possibility of a peaceful Catholic succession. Philip, who had been Mary’s former brother-in-law, now put forward his own claim to the English crown, arguing that restoring Catholicism to England required a rightful Catholic king. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • Religious division: The Reformation had created an impassable religious divide. Philip saw the Armada as a crusade to restore Catholicism in England, and received papal support for the enterprise. Pope Sixtus V, though privately anxious that Drake might defeat the Armada, nonetheless backed the venture. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Philip’s Plan

Philip’s strategy called for the Armada to sail from Spain to the English Channel, pick up the army of the Duke of Parma in Flanders (the Spanish commander in the Netherlands), and escort the invasion barges across to Kent. The plan required precise coordination between the fleet and the army — coordination that would prove impossible to achieve. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Armada Force

Spanish Command

The Armada was commanded by Don Alonso Perez de Guzman, the seventh Duke of Medina Sidonia. A grandee and an able administrator, he was an inexperienced naval commander who had initially begged Philip to excuse him from the command. He was supported by experienced naval officers such as Juan Martinez de Recalde and Diego Flores de Valdes. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Composition

The Armada that left Lisbon consisted of approximately 151 ships. After repairs at Corunna, it sailed for England with around 130 vessels. The fleet included:

  • Purpose-built galleons of the Spanish navy
  • Armed merchantmen hired or commandeered from Spain, Italy, and the Baltic
  • Mediterranean galleasses (oar-and-sail hybrid warships)
  • Hulks and supply vessels

The total complement was about 30,000 men, including sailors, soldiers, and officers. The flagship was the San Martin. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

English Preparations

English Command

The English fleet was commanded by Charles, Lord Howard of Effingham, the Lord Admiral. Francis Drake served as vice-admiral, commanding from his flagship, the Revenge. Other notable English captains included John Hawkins, Martin Frobisher, and Lord Henry Seymour. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

English Strategy

Drake consistently argued for a pre-emptive strike against Spain, advocating that the best defence was attack. He had already demonstrated the effectiveness of this approach with his 1587 raid on Cadiz, where he had “singed the King of Spain’s beard” by destroying ships and supplies being assembled for the Armada. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

From September 1587 onward, Drake pressed his views at court, winning converts including Sir Francis Walsingham and the Earl of Leicester. Drake served on Burghley’s war council, helping coordinate English defences alongside soldiers such as Sir John Norris and Sir Roger Williams. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

English Fleet

The English fleet comprised around 140 ships at its peak strength, including the queen’s warships and many armed merchant vessels. The English ships were generally smaller, faster, and more manoeuvrable than their Spanish counterparts — qualities that would prove decisive. The English also had the advantage of better-trained gun crews and superior long-range gunnery. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Campaign

Departure and Delay

The Armada left Lisbon on 18 May 1588 under Medina Sidonia’s flag aboard the San Martin. Almost immediately, contrary winds drove the fleet south and scattered it. Only a few ships reached the rendezvous off the Scillies. Medina Sidonia gathered most of his fleet into Corunna, where they spent a month refitting, replacing spoiled provisions, and discharging the sick. Despite his own doubts, Philip insisted the Armada sail, and Medina Sidonia eventually led approximately 130 ships from Corunna on 12 July. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The First Engagement — Plymouth (21 July)

The Armada was sighted off the Lizard on 19 July. The English fleet, caught at Plymouth, scrambled to put to sea. On the morning of 21 July, with the wind veering to west-north-west, Howard and Drake found themselves to windward of the Armada. The royal standard flew from Medina Sidonia’s flagship, and the Armada formed into its battle formation — a defensive crescent.

The English, having little more than fifty ships available, attacked. However, they showed their inexperience in open-water gunnery, firing from too great a distance to inflict serious damage. Howard later admitted: “We durst not adventure to put in among them, their fleet being so strong.” ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Battle of Portland Bill (23-24 July)

The action off Portland Bill was the most serious engagement yet. The English attacked vigorously, and at one point Martin Frobisher in the Triumph found himself isolated and under heavy attack from multiple Spanish ships. Drake, who had led about fifty ships out to sea, suddenly threw himself into the battle, launching a vigorous assault on the Armada’s southern flank “so sharply that they were all forced to give way and bear room.” Howard came forward with the wind behind him, and the English pummelled Medina Sidonia’s San Martin for an hour, riddling her with some five hundred shot before the Spaniards slipped away to leeward. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Fireships at Calais (Night of 28-29 July)

The Armada anchored in the neutral French port of Calais on 27 July. Medina Sidonia waited for word from Parma, whose army was supposed to rendezvous with the fleet. The replies were crushing — Parma would not be ready for several days.

At midnight on 28 July, the English launched their most devastating attack. Eight ships of 90 to 200 tons were prepared as fireships, their rudders lashed fast, their sails set, loaded with pitch, tar, faggots, and ordnance. In the high wind, they ran straight into the Spanish anchorage, sputtering into flames and occasionally erupting in explosions. The Spanish, remembering the Dutch “hell-burners” of Antwerp that had destroyed part of Parma’s bridge in 1585, panicked. Captains cut their cables and scattered to sea, leaving their anchors behind. Only Medina Sidonia’s San Martin, Recalde’s San Juan, and three Portuguese galleons preserved their anchors and held position. The fireships themselves destroyed no Spanish vessels, but they broke the Armada’s formation — something the English fleet had failed to do in four battles. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Battle of Gravelines (29 July)

At dawn on 29 July, the decisive battle began. The English now closed to point-blank range, pouring shot into the scattered Spanish ships. Drake’s Revenge led the attack, followed by the squadrons of Hawkins, Frobisher, and Seymour. The Spanish fought desperately, forming a ragged half-moon formation, but the English pounded them mercilessly.

The San Felipe and San Mateo were so badly damaged that they fell behind and were cut off. One Portuguese galleon sank after being hammered by three English ships in succession. The San Martin herself took two hundred shot on her starboard side, many of her guns were dismounted, and the sea leaked through holes below her waterline. At one point Medina Sidonia despaired: “Senor Oquendo, what shall we do? We are lost!”

The Armada lost at least four ships at Gravelines, with as many as 1,500 men killed, drowned, or captured, and some 800 wounded. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Retreat

The wind shifted to the south-west, enabling the crippled Spanish ships to bear north-eastwards, away from the shoals, the English, and Parma. Medina Sidonia decided to sail north around Scotland and return to Spain via the Atlantic. It was a disastrous route. Storms off the coasts of Scotland and Ireland destroyed many ships, driving them onto rocky shores. Only about half the original fleet — perhaps 60-70 ships — eventually made it back to Spanish ports. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Drake’s Role

Drake’s contribution to the Armada campaign was substantial:

  • Vice-admiral of the English fleet, commanding from the Revenge
  • Key strategic advisor who consistently argued for an aggressive, offensive approach
  • Led the fireship attack at Calais, which broke the Spanish formation
  • Commanded his squadron through all the Channel battles, notably at Portland Bill and Gravelines
  • Captured the Rosario, a Spanish galleon carrying treasure, early in the campaign
  • Served on Burghley’s war council coordinating English defences ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Aftermath and Significance

The defeat of the Spanish Armada had far-reaching consequences:

  • It marked the beginning of English naval dominance and the decline of Spain’s unchallenged maritime supremacy
  • The battles in the Channel represented the first major naval battle emphasizing sails and guns rather than oars and boarding
  • It demonstrated the effectiveness of the English approach — smaller, more manoeuvrable ships with superior gunnery
  • England’s confidence and national pride surged, while Spain’s prestige suffered a severe blow
  • The war between England and Spain continued for another sixteen years, but the threat of invasion was effectively ended ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
