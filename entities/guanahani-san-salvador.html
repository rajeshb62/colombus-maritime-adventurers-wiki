Guanahani / San Salvador

Guanahani (the Taíno/Lucayan name) — renamed San Salvador by Christopher Columbus on October 12, 1492 — was the first island of the New World where Columbus set foot during his first voyage across the Atlantic. The landfall ended 33 days of sailing westward from the Canary Islands and initiated sustained European contact with the Americas. The island is part of the Bahama archipelago (the Lucayan Islands, called “Gucayos” or “Lucayos” by Irving), which stretches southeast to northwest from the coast of Florida to Hispaniola, covering the northern coast of Cuba.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 247]

The Landfall (October 12, 1492)

According to Irving’s account, drawn from Columbus’s journal as preserved by Las Casas:

On the night of October 11, Columbus thought he saw a light in the distance — “a glimmering light” like a wax candle rising and falling — which he pointed out to Pedro Gutierrez, a gentleman of the King’s bedchamber. Gutierrez also saw it, as did Rodrigo Sanchez of Segovia, though the light was so faint and uncertain that others dismissed it as an illusion. At two o’clock on the morning of October 12, the Pinta, sailing ahead, fired a signal gun: land was in sight. The lookout was Rodrigo de Triana, a seaman of that vessel.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book III, Ch. IV]

At dawn, Columbus beheld a level and beautiful island several leagues in extent, of great freshness and verdure, covered with trees “like a continual orchard.” Though everything appeared in the wild luxuriance of untamed nature, the island was clearly populous: inhabitants emerged from the woods and ran to the shore from all parts, standing and gazing at the ships in astonishment. They were perfectly naked, and from their attitudes and gestures appeared lost in wonder at the strange vessels hovering on their coast.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 236-237]

Relevant primary-source guide: irving-columbus, Book IV, Chapter I.

Columbus’s Naming

Columbus gave the island the name San Salvador (“Holy Saviour”), reflecting his deep religious piety and his understanding of the providential nature of the discovery. He performed this naming as part of the formal ceremony of taking possession (see below). At the time, Columbus believed he had arrived at an island off the coast of Asia, somewhere in the “Chinese Sea” near the opulent regions described by Marco Polo — Cipango (Japan) and Cathay (China). This misidentification colored all his subsequent interpretations of what he encountered.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 237, 241-242]

The native name, as recorded by Columbus and preserved in his journal, was Guanahani (spelled Guanahanè by Irving).^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 247]

The Ceremonial Taking of Possession

Columbus’s landing was conducted as a formal act of state, meticulously staged to establish a legal claim under the authority of the Spanish Crown:

  1. The approach: Columbus made signal for the ships to cast anchor and the boats to be manned and armed. He entered his own boat, richly attired in scarlet, bearing the royal standard. Martin Alonso Pinzon and Vicente Yañez Pinzon followed in their boats, each bearing the banner of the enterprise — a green cross with the letters F and I (Fernando and Isabel) surmounted by crowns.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 236]

  2. Thanksgiving on landing: No sooner did Columbus land than he threw himself on his knees, kissed the earth, and returned thanks to God with tears of joy. His example was followed by the rest, whose hearts overflowed with gratitude.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 237]

  3. Solemn possession: Columbus rose, drew his sword, displayed the royal standard, and assembled around him the two Pinzon captains, Rodrigo de Escobedo (notary of the armament), Rodrigo Sanchez, and all who had landed. He then took solemn possession in the name of the Catholic Sovereigns Ferdinand and Isabella, giving the island the name San Salvador. Having complied with the requisite forms and ceremonies, he called upon all present to take the oath of obedience to him as Admiral and Viceroy representing the persons of the Sovereigns.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 237]

  4. Emotions of the crew: The crew, who had recently considered themselves “devoted men hurrying forward to destruction,” now gave themselves up to unbounded joy. They thronged around the Admiral — some embraced him, others kissed his hands. Even those who had been most mutinous and turbulent during the voyage became the most devoted and enthusiastic.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 237-238]

Irving includes a note that a form of prayer — “Domine Deus aeterne et omnipotens, sacro tuo verbo caelum, et terram, et mare creasti…” — was later composed by Padre Claudio Clemente and, by order of the Castilian sovereigns, was used by Balboa, Cortes, and Pizarro in their discoveries.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 237, footnote]

The Taíno (Lucayan) Inhabitants

The inhabitants of Guanahani were Taíno people of the Lucayan branch, who inhabited the Bahama archipelago. Irving describes them in detail based on Columbus’s journal:

  • Appearance: Of tawny or copper complexion, entirely without beards, with straight coarse hair (not crisped like African peoples under the same latitude). They cut their hair short above the ears but left some locks falling on the shoulders. Their features, though obscured and disfigured by paint, were agreeable; they had lofty foreheads and remarkably fine eyes. They were of moderate stature and well-shaped; most appeared under thirty years of age. Only one female was present with the initial landing party — quite young, naked, and beautifully formed.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 240-241]

  • Body painting: They painted themselves with a variety of colours — some confined to the face, nose, or around the eyes; others covering the whole body, giving a wild and fantastic appearance.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 241]

  • Disposition: Friendly, gentle, simple, and artless. They approached the Spaniards with great awe, frequently prostrating themselves on the earth and making signs of adoration. They universally believed the white men had come from the sky — an idea Irving notes was “universally entertained by the inhabitants of the New World.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 239-240]

  • Arms and tools: Their only weapons were lances hardened at the end by fire, or pointed with a flint, or the tooth or bone of a fish. They had no iron and did not understand its properties — when a drawn sword was presented to them, they unguardedly took it by the edge.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 241-242]

  • Canoes and navigation: They used light barks called canoes, formed from a single hollowed tree, capable of holding from one to forty or fifty people. These they managed dexterously with paddles; if overturned, they righted them with great facility.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 243]

  • Subsistence: They cultivated yuca (cassava), a large root from which they made cassava bread — a thin broad cake that hardened and could be kept for long periods, requiring steeping in water before eating. They had abundance of cotton, which they spun into yarn and traded in large balls. They also kept domesticated parrots.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 242-244]

  • Trade goods: Columbus distributed coloured caps, glass beads, hawks’-bells, and other trifles (typical of Portuguese trade on the African gold coast). The natives received these as inestimable gifts, hanging beads around their necks. They eagerly exchanged fragments of gold worn as nose ornaments for glass beads and hawks’-bells.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 242-245]

Columbus, believing he had reached Asia, called the natives Indians (Indios) — a name that was universally adopted and extended to all aboriginals of the New World.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 241-242]

Subsequent Exploration of the Island

On October 14, the Admiral set off at daybreak with the ship’s boats to reconnoitre the island, directing his course to the northeast. The coast was surrounded by a reef of rocks within which there was depth of water and a sufficient harbour, though the entrance was very narrow. The island appeared well-wooded with streams of water and a large lake in the centre. Passing two or three villages, the inhabitants — men and women — ran to the shores, prostrating themselves and lifting up their hands and eyes, either giving thanks to heaven or worshipping the Spaniards as supernatural beings. Many swam after the boats or followed in canoes. The expedition came to a small peninsula where they could have fortified a position, though Columbus judged no fortification necessary given the gentleness of the people.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, pp. 247-249]

Columbus took seven natives from Guanahani to serve as interpreters and guides for the further voyage. These Lucayan interpreters later accompanied him to Cuba and Hispaniola. Irving notes that during subsequent voyages, Columbus refrained from touching at islands near Guanahani because his Indian interpreters, “who were from the island of Guanahani, only eight leagues from that of Isabella, might desert, the poor savages keeping a wistful eye in the direction of their home.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book VIII, Ch. III]

Modern Identification Debates

The precise island at which Columbus first made landfall has been a matter of scholarly debate since at least the early nineteenth century. Irving himself notes the dispute:

“The island where Columbus had thus, for the first time, set his foot upon the New World, was called by the natives, Guanahane. It still retains the name of San Salvador, which he gave to it, though called by the English Cat Island. The light which he had seen the evening previous to his making land, may have been on Watling’s Island, which lies a few leagues to the east.”^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 247]

Irving adds that “some dispute having recently arisen as to the island on which Columbus first landed,” referring readers to the “Illustrations” section of his work, Article “First Landing of Columbus,” for a full discussion.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 247, footnote]

Key candidates in the modern debate include:

CandidateDescription
Watlings Island (officially renamed San Salvador Island in 1926 by the Bahamian government)The most widely accepted candidate among 20th-century scholars. Fits Columbus’s log distances and descriptions. The light Columbus saw on October 11 may have been on this island.
Cat Island (the original English name for what was then called San Salvador)Irving identifies this as the English name for San Salvador itself. However, the light sighting suggests a separate island to the east.
Samana CayProposed by National Geographic (1986) based on computer analysis of Columbus’s log by Joseph Judge. Less accepted by subsequent scholarship.
Plana Cays / Mayaguana / other Bahamian islandsVarious other candidates have been proposed, but none have achieved consensus.

The official position of the Bahamian government is that Watlings Island was renamed San Salvador Island in 1926 to recognize its historical significance as Columbus’s first landfall, though the scholarly debate continues.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book IV, Ch. I, p. 247]

Key Facts

  • Native name: Guanahani (recorded as Guanahane by Irving)
  • Columbus’s name: San Salvador (given October 12, 1492)
  • Modern name: San Salvador Island, Bahamas (formerly Watlings Island, 1926 renaming); also historically called Cat Island by English mariners
  • Coordinates: Approximately 24°N, 74°W (San Salvador Island, Bahamas)
  • Archipelago: Lucayan Islands (Bahamas), called “Gucayos” by the Spanish
  • Date of discovery: October 12, 1492 (Julian calendar)
  • Original inhabitants: Taíno people of the Lucayan branch
  • First European to sight land: Rodrigo de Triana (lookout on the Pinta)
  • Notary of the landing: Rodrigo de Escobedo
  • Interpreters taken: Seven Lucayan natives who accompanied Columbus on the remainder of the first voyage
  • Distance from Hispaniola: approximately 8 leagues from Isabella (Hispaniola’s northeastern coast) ^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt, Book VIII, Ch. III]

Relationship to Other Entities

  • Christopher Columbus — The explorer who made landfall at Guanahani and renamed it San Salvador, commencing the first voyage
  • Martin Alonso Pinzon — Captain of the Pinta, who commanded the lead ship that first sighted land (via lookout Rodrigo de Triana)
  • Ferdinand and Isabella — The Catholic Monarchs in whose name Columbus took possession of the island
  • Caonabo — The cacique of Maguana on Hispaniola (a different branch of the Taíno people), who led native resistance against the Spanish
