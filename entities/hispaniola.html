Hispaniola: the island where Columbus founded the first European settlements, fought the Battle in the Vega Real, was arrested by Bobadilla, and where Ovando’s massacre at Xaragua ended organized Taíno resistance.

Hispaniola

Hispaniola (known to its indigenous Taino inhabitants as Hayti or Ayiti, meaning “mountainous land”) is the second-largest island of the Greater Antilles, discovered by Christopher Columbus on December 5, 1492, during his first voyage. It was the site of the first sustained European settlement in the Americas and the theatre of the earliest conflicts between Europeans and Native Americans. The island served as the administrative and economic heart of the Spanish Indies for the first three decades after discovery, and its native population was devastated within a generation by war, forced labour, and introduced disease. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. VI]

Discovery (Book IV, Chapter VI)

On December 5, 1492, after leaving the coast of Cuba and sailing east-northeast in search of the reported golden island of Babeque, Columbus’s remaining two ships — the Niña and the wrecked Santa Maria — sighted a large landmass on the horizon. From a distance the island appeared vast, mountainous, and densely wooded. Columbus named it La Isla Espanola (the Spanish Island), later Latinized as Hispaniola. The natives whom Columbus encountered called their land Hayti. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. VI]

Columbus anchored in a harbour he named Puerto de la Concepcion, near the territory of the cacique Guacanagari, who came aboard the Niña bearing gifts of gold ornaments, cotton, and cassava bread. The admiral was struck by the beauty of the island — its towering mountains, broad valleys, and rivers abundant with gold dust in their sands. Guacanagari gave Columbus reports of rich gold deposits in the interior, particularly in a mountainous region the natives called Cibao. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. VI–VII]

The fleet spent the next two weeks coasting the northern shore of Hispaniola, trading with the natives and confirming the presence of gold. On Christmas Eve 1492, the Santa Maria ran aground on a reef and was wrecked beyond salvage off the coast near present-day Limonade, Haiti. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. VIII]

Taino Name and Caciquates

At the time of Columbus’s arrival, the island was called Hayti (variously rendered as Ayiti, Bohio, or Quizqueia) by its Taino inhabitants. It was divided into five principal caciquates (chiefdoms), each ruled by a cacique who exercised authority over a defined territory and its villages. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII]

The five caciquates were:

  • Marien — The northwestern region, ruled by the friendly cacique Guacanagari, who assisted Columbus after the wreck of the Santa Maria. Marien was the first native domain contacted by the Europeans.
  • Maguana — The central mountainous region, encompassing the gold mines of Cibao. Ruled by the formidable Carib cacique Caonabo, the most powerful and warlike of the Taino chieftains, who led the destruction of La Navidad and organized the first organized resistance against Spanish encroachment. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. III]
  • Higuey — The easternmost province, a fertile and populous region that later offered stiff resistance to the Spanish under the cacique Cotabanama during Ovando’s governorship. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. III]
  • Maguá — The northeastern region, ruled by the cacique Guarionex, who initially submitted to Spanish tribute demands but later led two insurrections in the Vega Real before being captured by Bartholomew Columbus. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book XI Ch. II]
  • Xaragua — The southwestern region, centred on the fertile plain of present-day Leogane, Haiti. Ruled by the female cacique Anacaona (sister of the former cacique Behechio), Xaragua was the wealthiest and most populous of the Taino domains, renowned for its agricultural abundance and the grace and hospitality of its people. Xaragua became the refuge of Francisco Roldan’s rebels and later the site of Ovando’s infamous massacre. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. I] ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. II]

Settlement of La Navidad

After the wreck of the Santa Maria on Christmas Eve 1492, Columbus decided to establish the first European settlement in the New World using the timbers of the ruined ship. With the assistance of Guacanagari, the Spaniards built a fortified compound on the northern coast, near present-day Bord de Mer de Limonade, Haiti. Columbus named it La Navidad (The Nativity) in commemoration of the Christmas event. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. X]

Columbus selected 39 volunteers to remain as the garrison — men who, Irving notes, were eager to be released from the discipline of the ships and who anticipated easy wealth from the gold of the interior. Columbus left them with supplies, arms, and instructions to treat the natives with respect and to build a stockade. He also left one of his ships’ boats for coastal communication. On January 4, 1493, Columbus departed for Spain aboard the Niña, expecting to return within the year to a thriving settlement. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. XI]

When Columbus returned on his second voyage in November 1493, he found La Navidad utterly destroyed. The fortress had been burned to the ground, and the corpses of the 39 colonists lay among the ruins. The settlement had fallen into disorder soon after Columbus’s departure: the colonists quarrelled among themselves, abused the hospitality of the natives, and provoked the wrath of Caonabo, who led a surprise assault that annihilated the entire garrison. Only Guacanagari remained loyal to the Spaniards, claiming to have been wounded in their defence. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. III] ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. IV]

Founding of Isabella (Isabella)

With La Navidad destroyed, Columbus selected a new site for the capital of the Spanish colony on the northern coast of Hispaniola, near the mouth of the Yagui River in present-day Puerto Plata province, Dominican Republic. The settlement was named Isabella (La Isabela) in honour of Queen Isabella of Castile. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII]

Construction began in January 1494. Isabella was the first planned European city in the Americas, laid out with a central plaza, a church, a storehouse, a municipal hall, and stone buildings. The fleet of 17 ships and 1,500 men that Columbus had brought on the second voyage provided the labour to construct the town. However, the colony was immediately stricken by disease — the Europeans, unaccustomed to the tropical climate, fell victim to fevers and dysentery. Famine set in as provisions spoiled or were exhausted. Within months, Isabella became a place of suffering, and Columbus’s authority was challenged by the insubordination of Pedro Margarite and the complaints of disaffected colonists. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. I]

Despite its hardships, Isabella served as the base for Columbus’s expeditions into the interior — including the exploration of the Cibao gold mines and the campaign against Caonabo — and remained the capital of Hispaniola until the founding of Santo Domingo on the southern coast in 1496.

The Mines of Cibao

The mountainous interior of Hispaniola, known to the Taino as Cibao, was the principal source of gold on the island. Native reports of rich alluvial gold in the Cibao rivers had been a primary motive for Columbus’s interest in the island during his first voyage. After establishing Isabella, Columbus personally led an expedition into the Cibao mountains in early 1494, confirming the presence of gold in quantity. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. VI]

To secure the gold region, Columbus ordered the construction of the fortress of Santo Tomas in the heart of Cibao, garrisoned by fifty men under the command of Alonso de Ojeda. This fortress, built within the domain of Caonabo, provoked the cacique’s siege and the subsequent campaign of native resistance. Later, the even richer mines of Hayna were discovered in the southern part of the island, further fuelling Spanish expectations of limitless wealth. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. X]

Columbus imposed a tribute system on the native population: every Indian over the age of 14 was required to deliver a hawk’s bell full of gold dust every three months, or in lieu of gold, an arroba (25 pounds) of cotton. This levy proved impossible to collect, inflicting severe hardship on the native population and sparking the rebellion of Guarionex in the Vega Real. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. VII]

The Vega Real

The Vega Real (Royal Valley) was the great fertile plain in the north-central part of Hispaniola, running east-west between the Cordillera Septentrional and the Cordillera Central mountains. It was the agricultural heartland of the Taino population, especially the caciquate of Maguá under Guarionex, and was famed for its remarkable fertility and dense settlement. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. VI]

In March 1495, provoked by the cruelty of the Spaniards and the imposition of the gold tribute, the cacique Guarionex assembled a large army of native warriors and attacked the Spanish settlements in the Vega Real. Columbus, with his brother Bartholomew Columbus and Alonso de Ojeda, marched into the valley at the head of 200 infantry, 20 cavalry, and a number of war-dogs. The Battle of the Vega Real was the first large-scale European-Indian engagement in the New World. The Spanish cavalry, firearms, and attack dogs routed the native forces, who had never before faced horses or heard gunfire. Thousands of Taino were killed for the loss of a few Spaniards. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. VI]

The victory was followed by the formal imposition of tribute on the caciques of the Vega, a system that deepened native misery and led to further insurrections under Guarionex (second rebellion, 1497) and continued resistance throughout the interior. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book XI Ch. II]

The Rebellion of Roldan

The most serious internal challenge to Spanish authority in early Hispaniola was the revolt of Francisco Roldan, the alcalde mayor (chief magistrate) of the colony. During Columbus’s absence on the third voyage (1498), Roldan assembled a following of discontented colonists who resented the strict governance of Bartholomew Columbus, the adelantado. Roldan withdrew to the province of Xaragua, where he established a rival government with the backing of the female cacique Anacaona, living licentiously among the Taino and recruiting defectors from passing ships. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. I]

When Columbus returned to San Domingo on August 30, 1498 — exhausted and ill with gout and ophthalmia — he found the colony in ruins: famine had set in, cultivation was abandoned, native villages were depopulated, and Roldan’s rebels controlled the richest province with impunity. Unable to suppress the rebellion by force, Columbus was forced to negotiate. Roldan was reinstated as alcalde mayor and received extensive grants of land and native labour (encomiendas) for himself and his principal followers — a humiliating concession that set a dangerous precedent by rewarding armed insurrection. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. III–V]

The rebellion fatally undermined Columbus’s authority. The rebels’ accusations — carried to Spain by every departing ship — painted Columbus and his brothers as oppressive foreigners, fuelling the campaign at court that led to the dispatch of Francisco de Bobadilla. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. V]

Bobadilla’s Administration

In 1500, the Spanish sovereigns dispatched Francisco de Bobadilla as royal commissioner with full powers to investigate Columbus’s conduct and, if necessary, assume the government of Hispaniola. Bobadilla arrived at San Domingo (the new capital on the southern coast, founded in 1496 and later renamed Santo Domingo) and, without any preliminary investigation, publicly read his commission, seized the fortress, and broadcast accusations against Columbus. He invited depositions — drawing testimony from Roldan’s former rebels — and ordered the arrest of Christopher Columbus, his brother Bartholomew, and his younger brother Diego. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XIII Ch. I–II]

Columbus was placed in chains and shipped to Spain. During the voyage, he famously refused to have the irons removed, declaring that he would wear them until the sovereigns ordered their removal. Bobadilla’s administration was marked by corruption, self-enrichment, and the further exploitation of the native population. He was himself superseded within two years by Nicolas de Ovando, who arrived in 1502 with a massive fleet of 30 ships and 2,500 settlers. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XIII Ch. IV] ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XIV Ch. III]

Ovando’s Massacre at Xaragua

Nicolas de Ovando, the third governor of Hispaniola, embarked on a systematic policy of subjugation and exploitation of the native population. He introduced the repartimiento system, which divided Taino villages among Spanish settlers as sources of forced labour in the mines and fields. The native population suffered catastrophic decline under this system. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. I]

The most notorious event of Ovando’s governorship was the massacre at Xaragua in 1503. Suspecting the female cacique Anacaona of plotting rebellion — she had previously allied with Francisco Roldan — Ovando led a military expedition to Xaragua under the pretext of a friendly visit. The Spanish staged a grand festival, inviting the native nobles to participate in games and entertainments. At a prearranged signal, the Spanish fell upon the unarmed Taino nobility and massacred them. Anacaona was captured, subjected to a mock trial, and publicly executed. The province of Xaragua was devastated, and its surviving population was dispersed into the encomienda system. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. II]

The massacre at Xaragua was followed by the war against the eastern province of Higuey, where the cacique Cotabanama led a desperate resistance. The Spanish, employing cavalry, dogs, and brutal tactics, systematically subdued the province. By 1504, organized indigenous resistance on Hispaniola was effectively crushed. The native population, estimated at several hundred thousand at the time of Columbus’s arrival, had been reduced to a fraction of its former number through war, forced labour, introduced diseases, and outright slaughter. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. III–IV]

Significance

Hispaniola was the crucible of European colonization in the New World. The patterns established there — the encomienda and repartimiento systems, the use of horses and dogs in warfare, the imposition of gold tributes, the treatment of native populations as resources to be exploited — became templates for Spanish colonial administration throughout the Americas. The destruction of the Taino civilization of Hispaniola within a generation of European contact stands as the first and most complete genocide of the colonial era, and the island’s subsequent history — as the home of the first colonial capital (Santo Domingo), as the birthplace of African slavery in the Americas (with the introduction of enslaved Africans as early as 1503), and as the site of the first sustained European-native conflicts — established precedents that would echo across the hemisphere for centuries. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XVII Ch. I]
