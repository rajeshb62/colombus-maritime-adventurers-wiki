Vasco da Gama

Vasco da Gama (c. 1460–1524) was a Portuguese explorer and the first European to reach India by sea, commanding the first voyage that linked Europe and Asia by ocean route around Africa. He was a Knight of the Order of Santiago, later appointed Viceroy of Portuguese India. His three voyages established the Portuguese maritime empire in the Indian Ocean and opened the spice trade to Portugal. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt] ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart] ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Background and Family

Born around 1460 in Sines, Portugal, to Estevão da Gama and Isabel Sodré. His father Estevão had been a commander of a fortress and a knight of the household of Infante Fernando. Vasco had two brothers: Paulo da Gama, who captained the São Rafael on the first voyage and died on the return journey, and Aires da Gama. He married Catarina de Ataíde and had several sons, including Estevão, Paulo, and Francisco. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart] ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

The First Voyage (1497–1499)

Preparation and Departure from Lisbon

King Manuel I of Portugal, the first of that name, despatched four vessels to make discoveries and go in search of spices. Vasco da Gama was appointed captain-major of the fleet. His brother Paulo da Gama commanded the São Rafael; Nicolau Coelho commanded the Berrio; and a fourth storeship carried supplies under Gonçalo Nunes. The flagship was the São Gabriel. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Vasco da Gama and his officers spent the night before departure in prayer at a chapel in Restello (the suburb of Restelo, four miles below the Lisbon Arsenal), which had been built by Henry the Navigator. On Saturday, July 8, 1497, the fleet left Restello. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Fleet and Crew

The fleet carried approximately 150-170 men. Hart describes the crew composition in The Sea Road to the Indies: seamen, soldiers, gunners, trumpeters, a surgeon, and rope-makers. Each vessel carried twenty guns — heavier pieces on clumsy forked bases, lighter guns with swivel mounts, bombards, and one-pounder matchlocks. Crew carried personal weapons. Stone balls for cannon were still in use. Powder and shot were stored in the hold. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

The Journal was written by an anonymous crew member who served on board the São Rafael. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Cape Verde Islands (July 8-27, 1497)

On July 15 the fleet sighted the Canaries and passed to the lee of Lançarote. During fog on the night of July 16, Paulo da Gama lost sight of the captain-major’s ship. The fleet became separated. Following instructions, they made sail for the Cape Verde Islands. On July 22 they sighted Ilha do Sal and discovered three vessels — the storeship and those of Coelho and Bartholomeu Dias (who was sailing with them as far as the Mine). They rejoined, and the crew expressed their joy by firing off bombards and sounding trumpets. On Thursday, July 27, they arrived at the island of São Thiago (Santiago) and anchored in the Bay of Santa Maria, where they took on meat, water, and wood, and repaired their yards. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The South Atlantic Crossing: The Volta do Mar (August 3 - November 4, 1497)

On Thursday, August 3, the fleet left São Thiago in an easterly direction. This was the beginning of the volta do mar — the deliberate westward sweep that would carry the ships down into the South Atlantic to catch the westerly winds and avoid the contrary currents and winds of the African coast. It was a route pioneered by earlier Portuguese navigators like Bartolomeu Dias.

On August 18, about 200 leagues from São Thiago, the captain-major’s main yard broke. They lay to under foresail and lower mainsail for two days and a night. The passage from São Thiago to the Bay of St. Helena covered approximately 1,170 leagues (about 4,290 miles), taking 95 days at an average daily progress of 12 leagues (45 miles). ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On October 27 (the eve of St. Simon and Jude) they saw many whales, quoquas, and seals. On Wednesday, November 1 (All Saints’ Day), they perceived many indications of land — gulf-weed, birds, and other signs. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

St. Helena Bay: First Landfall in Southern Africa (November 4-16, 1497)

On Saturday, November 4, a couple of hours before daybreak, they had soundings in 110 fathoms, and at nine o’clock sighted land. They saluted the captain-major by firing bombards and dressing ships with flags. On November 7 they returned to the land, finding a low coast with a broad bay. Pero d’Alenquer (who had doubled the Cape with Bartolomeu Dias) took soundings and found the bay clean and sheltered against all winds except from the N.W. They named it Santa Helena (St. Helena Bay). ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On Wednesday, November 8, they cast anchor and remained eight days, cleaning ships, mending sails, and taking in wood. They encountered Hottentot (Khoikhoi) inhabitants — tawny-coloured people dressed in skins, armed with olive-wood poles tipped with fire-hardened horn, and accompanied by dogs resembling those of Portugal. A notable incident occurred when Fernão Velloso, eager to see how the natives lived, accompanied them to their houses. When he returned shouting, the crew went to fetch him unarmed, and the natives threw assegais, wounding the captain-major and three or four others. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Rounding the Cape of Good Hope (November 16-22, 1497)

At daybreak on Thursday, November 16, having careened ships and taken in wood, they set sail. Unable to determine their position relative to the Cape, they stood out toward the S.S.W. On Saturday, November 18, they beheld the Cape. They made repeated attempts to round it, but the wind blew from the S.S.W. while the Cape juts out toward the S.W. — forcing them to stand out to sea and return repeatedly. Finally, on Wednesday, November 22, at noon, with the wind astern, they succeeded in doubling the Cape of Good Hope and ran along the coast. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Mossel Bay — (Angra de São Braz) (November 25 - December 7, 1497)

Late on Saturday, November 25 (St. Catherine’s Day), they entered the Angra de São Braz (Mossel Bay). They remained thirteen days, breaking up the storeship and transferring her contents to the other vessels. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

About ninety Hottentots appeared on December 1. Unlike when Bartolomeu Dias had visited (when the natives fled), these approached cautiously, having apparently heard from the people at St. Helena Bay that the Portuguese meant no harm. On December 2, about two hundred natives came with a dozen oxen and cows and four or five sheep, playing on flutes and dancing. The Portuguese bought a black ox for three bracelets and dined on it. The captain-major ordered two bombards fired from the poop of the longboat when tensions arose — the first discharge scattered the natives who had been threatening. On an offshore island they found seals, including huge ones with large tusks, and counted 3,000 of them, firing bombards among them for amusement. They erected a cross and a pillar (padrão) here, though the natives tore them down before the fleet departed. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Natal Coast (December 8-25, 1497)

Leaving São Braz on December 8, the fleet encountered a great storm on December 12 (eve of Santa Lucia), losing sight of Coelho’s Berrio before reuniting at night with signal lights. They passed beyond the furthest point reached by Bartolomeu Dias on December 16. The ships were then driven back sixty leagues by the powerful Agulhas current to the Ilhéo da Cruz (Cross Island), but a strong stern-wind restored their progress. By Christmas Day, December 25, they had discovered seventy leagues of coast beyond Dias’s furthest. They named the land Natal (meaning “Christmas” in Portuguese). ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Bantu Coast — Terra da Boa Gente and Rio do Cobre (January 11-16, 1498)

On Thursday, January 11, 1498, they discovered a small river and anchored near the coast. The inhabitants were tall Bantu people, hospitable and welcoming. Their chief was given a jacket, red pantaloons, a Moorish cap, and a bracelet, and in turn offered everything his country could provide. The crew stayed five days, taking in water conveyed to the boats by the villagers. The country was named Terra da Boa Gente (“Land of Good People”) and the river Rio do Cobre (“Copper River”) because of the copper ornaments worn by the people. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Rio dos Bons Signaes (January 24 - February 24, 1498)

On Monday, January 22, they discovered a low coast thickly wooded with tall trees and the broad mouth of a river. The Berrio entered first on January 24, and the other vessels followed on the 25th. While at this river, two gentlemen of the country visited — one wearing a cap of green satin — and a young man with them had seen large ships before, which the Portuguese took as hopeful signs that they were approaching India. They spent thirty-two days here taking in water, careening the ships, and repairing the mast of the São Rafael. Many men fell ill with scurvy — their feet and hands swelling, gums growing over teeth. Paulo da Gama visited the sick night and day, distributing medicines he had brought for his own use. They erected a pillar here, calling it the padrão of St. Raphael. The river they named Rio dos Bons Signaes (“River of Good Signs” or “River of Good Tokens”). ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Mozambique (March 2-29, 1498)

On Thursday, March 1, they sighted islands and the mainland. On Friday, March 2, Nicolau Coelho mistook the channel and ran aground on a bank near Mozambique Island. Local boats — playing anafils (Moorish trumpets) — welcomed them. The inhabitants were Mohammedans (Swahili/Arabs), of ruddy complexion, dressed in fine linen and cotton. They found four Moorish vessels in port laden with gold, silver, cloves, pepper, ginger, pearls, jewels, and rubies. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The local Sultan — whom the Portuguese called a “king” — visited the ships. After being given presents, he provided two pilots. But when the Portuguese attempted to leave on March 10, the pilots fled. A conflict erupted when armed boats from the island confronted them; Paulo da Gama fired bombards and the Berrio pursued. The Moors planned to seize and kill the Portuguese, seeing them as Christian interlopers, but the pilot later revealed the plot. Forced by contrary winds to return to Mozambique for water, the Portuguese were denied access to the watering place. The captain-major ordered three bombards fired to clear the beach, and they took water by force. On March 24 they bombarded the town itself, killing two men. Paulo da Gama captured an almadia laden with fine cotton-stuffs, a glazed jar of butter, glass phials of scented water, books of the Law, and other goods. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Mombasa (April 7-13, 1498)

On Saturday, April 7 (the eve of Palm Sunday), they cast anchor off Mombasa. The city was prepared for them — numerous vessels dressed in flags. News of their actions at Mozambique had preceded them. The Portuguese anchored with caution, believing there were Christians living alongside the Moors in the city (a persistent misconception). At midnight on the night of their arrival, a zavra with a hundred armed men approached, attempting to board the captain-major’s vessel but were refused entry. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On Palm Sunday (April 8), the King of Mombasa sent a sheep, oranges, lemons, sugar-cane, and a ring as a pledge of safety. The Portuguese sent two men to the king, who received them hospitably. But on Tuesday, April 10, when attempting to enter the port, the two Mozambique pilots jumped overboard and escaped. Suspecting treachery, the captain-major “questioned” two Moors from Mozambique by dropping boiling oil on their skin. They confessed that orders had been given to capture the Portuguese once inside the port, to avenge what had been done at Mozambique. One of the tortured Moors threw himself into the sea despite bound hands. During the night, swimmers approached the Berrio attempting to cut her cable, but were discovered and fled. After this treachery, the fleet remained two more days before departing. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Malindi (April 14-24, 1498)

Leaving Mombasa, the fleet captured a boat on April 14 containing seventeen men, gold, silver, and provisions. The prisoners told them that at Malindi there were four vessels belonging to Christians from India. On Easter Sunday, April 15, they cast anchor off Malindi. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The King of Malindi proved friendly, sending sheep and spices (cloves, cumin, ginger, nutmeg, pepper). On April 18, the king and captain-major met in a boat halfway between ship and shore. The king wore a robe of damask trimmed with green satin, seated on cushioned chairs of bronze beneath a crimson satin sunshade. Bombards fired a salute as he toured the ships. He left his son as a hostage on the ship and took two Portuguese to see his palace. The Indians from the four vessels at Malindi prostrated themselves before a picture of Our Lady brought on board Paulo da Gama’s ship, bringing offerings of cloves and pepper. The king provided a Gujarati pilot (often identified as Malema Cana, a name meaning “master Kanaka”) to guide them across the Indian Ocean. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Indian Ocean Crossing (April 24 - May 20, 1498)

On Tuesday, April 24, they left Malindi with the new pilot. On Sunday, April 29, they once again saw the North Star, which they had not seen for a long time. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On Friday, May 18, after having seen no land for twenty-three days, they sighted lofty mountains — having sailed no less than 600 leagues before the wind. The pilot identified Mount Eli (near Cananor, a conspicuous hill 16 miles north of Cananor). Due to heavy rain and thunderstorms on May 19, the pilot was unable to identify the exact location. On Sunday, May 20, 1498, they found themselves close to the mountains above Calicut. The pilot told them that this was the country they desired to go to. They anchored two leagues from the city of Calicut that night. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Arrival at Calicut and Negotiations with the Zamorin (May 20 - August 29, 1498)

On May 21, the captain-major sent one of the convicts ashore to Calicut. He was taken to two Moors from Tunis who spoke Castilian and Genoese. The first greeting was: “May the Devil take thee! What brought you hither?” When told they had come in search of Christians and spices, the Moors replied: “Why does not the King of Castile, the King of France, or the Signoria of Venice send hither?” ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Zamorin was fifteen leagues away but proceeded to Calicut on hearing of the Portuguese arrival. On Monday, May 28, da Gama went to meet the Zamorin with thirteen men, in their best attire, with bombards in their boats and trumpets and flags. He was carried in a palanquin through the city. They passed through a large Hindu temple (which they mistook for a Christian church), with a bronze pillar surmounted by a bird (a cock — the symbol of the war-god Subraumainar). The king — reclining on a couch covered with green velvet in a small court — received da Gama. The captain-major delivered his message: King Manuel of Portugal, lord of many countries and great wealth, desired to be the Zamorin’s friend and brother. The Zamorin welcomed him and promised to send ambassadors to Portugal. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The presents da Gama offered (twelve pieces of lambel, four scarlet hoods, hats, coral, wash-hand basins, sugar, oil, and honey) were scorned by the Zamorin’s Moorish factor as beneath the dignity of a king — poorer gifts than the meanest merchant from Mecca would bring. Relations soured further as established Muslim merchants (from Mecca, Cambay, and elsewhere) resented Portuguese intrusion into the spice trade, telling the Zamorin that the Portuguese were thieves who would ruin his country. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Diogo Dias was sent to the king as factor with merchandise — and was detained, along with other Portuguese, while the Zamorin demanded 600 xerafins (£223). Da Gama seized eighteen hostages in response. After tense negotiations — including the Zamorin writing a letter on a palm leaf with an iron pen — the hostages and merchandise were partially exchanged. The pillar (padrão) of St. Gabriel was given to the Zamorin’s men to be erected. On Wednesday, August 29, 1498, the captain-major and the other captains agreed to depart, resolving to take several Indian prisoners with them. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Return Journey (August 30, 1498 - July/August 1499)

Sailing from Calicut, the fleet was pursued by seventy boats before a thunderstorm scattered the pursuers. They stopped at the Anjediva Islands (September 20 - October 5), careening the ships and taking on water and wood. They met a mysterious figure — a Jewish renegade who spoke Venetian — later known as Gaspar da Gama, who had come to investigate the strange ships. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The crossing of the Arabian Sea took three months less three days (October 5 to January 2, 1499). Calms and foul winds plagued them. Scurvy ravaged the crews once more — thirty men died of their gums growing over their teeth and legs swelling, leaving only seven or eight men able to navigate each ship. The captains held council and agreed that if a favourable wind did not come they would have to return to India. God in his mercy sent a wind that carried them within sight of land in six days. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

They made landfall on January 2, 1499 — sighting Magadoxo (Mogadishu) on the Somali coast, a large town with multi-storied houses. Continuing south, they reached Malindi on January 7, where the friendly king supplied oranges and other fruit for the sick, gave a tusk of ivory for the King of Portugal, and erected a pillar. They remained five days resting. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On January 13 they anchored at the shoals of St. Raphael (near Mtangata), and burnt the São Rafael as they lacked the crew to sail all three vessels. The figurehead of the São Rafael — carved in oak, about 24 inches high — was saved and later became a family heirloom. After fifteen days, the two remaining ships (São Gabriel and Berrio) continued south, stopping at Zanzibar, São Jorge Island, and Mossel Bay (March 3). They doubled the Cape of Good Hope on March 20 and ran before favourable winds for twenty-seven days. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

On April 25 they had soundings of 35 fathoms near the shoals of the Rio Grande (near Sierra Leone). Here the Journal ends abruptly. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

Nicolau Coelho and Vasco da Gama were separated in a storm. Coelho continued in the Berrio, arriving at Cascaes (below Lisbon) on July 10, 1499 — a voyage of seventy-six days from the African coast. Vasco da Gama, having waited a day for his consort, proceeded to São Thiago, then chartered a caravel to take his dying brother Paulo to Terceira in the Azores. D. Paulo da Gama died a day after landing at Angra on Terceira and was buried in the church of the Monastery of São Francisco. Vasco delayed his triumphal return to attend his brother’s burial. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The exact date of Vasco da Gama’s return to Lisbon is uncertain: Barros, Goes, and Resende state August 29, 1499; Castanheda gives September 8; a manuscript in the Torre do Tombo records September 18. His triumphal entry likely took place around September 8. From the departure of July 8, 1497, to Coelho’s return on July 10, 1499, the voyage had lasted 732 days. Of this, 316 days were spent on the outward passage, 102 at Calicut and vicinity, and 314 on the homeward passage. Only 55 of the original 150-170 crew are thought to have survived. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt] ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

Navigation and Distances

The total outward passage from Lisbon to Calicut covered approximately 3,584 Portuguese leagues (12,108 nautical miles) at an average daily run of 58 miles. The homeward passage covered 3,417 leagues (11,550 miles) at an average of 40 miles per day. ^[source: velho_gama_journal.txt]

The Second Voyage (1502–1503)

King Manuel I dispatched da Gama on a second voyage with a heavily armed fleet to intimidate the rulers of the Malabar Coast and secure Portuguese trading interests. Da Gama attacked Calicut, bombarded the port, and seized merchant vessels. He established trading factories at Cochin and Cannanore, left a naval squadron to patrol the Indian Ocean, and returned to Portugal in 1503. His actions on this voyage cemented his reputation as harsh and ruthless. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart] ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Later Years and Third Voyage

After his second voyage, da Gama retired to private life in Évora, managing his estates and maintaining his position in the Order of Santiago. He was involved in advising the crown on Indian affairs but was not initially chosen to lead further expeditions. In 1524, King John III appointed him Viceroy of India and sent him on a third voyage to restore order in the Portuguese settlements. Da Gama arrived in India in September 1524 but fell ill shortly after. He died in Cochin on Christmas Eve, December 24, 1524. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart] ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]

Burial and Tomb

Da Gama was initially buried in Cochin. In 1539, his remains were brought back to Portugal and interred in the Church of Our Lady of the Relics (Igreja do Convento do Carmo) in Vidigueira, in a magnificent tomb. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart]

Character and Reputation

Vasco da Gama was a skilled mariner and determined commander who could be arrogant in later life. He was harsh in his treatment of adversaries, particularly Muslim merchants and rulers who opposed Portuguese interests. Hart describes him as becoming increasingly imperious and intolerant of opposition. Subrahmanyam’s study examines both his career and the legends that grew around him. ^[source: sea-road-to-the-indies-hart] ^[source: career-legend-vasco-da-gama-subrahmanyam]
