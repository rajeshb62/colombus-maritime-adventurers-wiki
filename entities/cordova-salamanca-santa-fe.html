Cordova, Salamanca, and Santa Fe — The Seven-Year Negotiation

Three locations mark the arc of Christopher Columbus’s seven-year struggle (1486–1492) to win Spanish royal sponsorship for his westward voyage: Cordova, where he first appeared before Ferdinand and Isabella; Salamanca, where a learned council debated his proposals inconclusively; and Santa Fe, the royal encampment outside Granada where the historic Capitulations were signed in April 1492. Together they trace the trajectory from initial audience through academic scrutiny to final triumph.

Cordova — First Appearance at Court (1486)

Historical Context

Cordova (Córdoba) was, in the late fifteenth century, one of the principal cities of Andalusia and a frequent residence of the Castilian court. The city, situated on the Guadalquivir River, had been a major centre of Moorish Spain (the capital of the Caliphate of Córdoba in the tenth century) and retained a rich intellectual and architectural heritage. By 1486, Ferdinand and Isabella were holding court there while conducting the long campaign of the Reconquista against the Emirate of Granada to the south.

Columbus’s Arrival

Columbus arrived at Cordova in early 1486, armed with letters of introduction from Fray Juan Perez de Marchena of La Rábida. He had spent the preceding months in Spain making contacts and had already gained some influential allies, including the Duke of Medina Sidonia and the Duke of Medina Celi, though neither had proved willing to finance the expedition personally.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. III]

At Cordova, Columbus secured an introduction to Hernando de Talavera, the Queen’s confessor and a figure of considerable influence at court. Through Talavera’s mediation, Columbus was granted his first audience with Ferdinand and Isabella. Irving records that the sovereigns listened to Columbus’s proposals with interest but, given the preoccupations of the Granada War, declined to commit immediately. Instead, they appointed a commission — headed by Talavera himself — to examine the feasibility and orthodoxy of Columbus’s plan. This commission would eventually convene at Salamanca.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. III]

Commission of Inquiry

The Talavera commission included not only theologians and clergy but also cosmographers, astronomers, and mariners — a deliberate effort to examine Columbus’s proposals from multiple angles. Columbus remained at Cordova for some months, pressing his case and awaiting the commission’s deliberations. The city thus became the first Spanish location where Columbus’s project received serious, if inconclusive, official consideration.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. III]

Salamanca — The Council of Inquiry (1486–1487)

The University and the Council

Salamanca, home to one of Europe’s most prestigious universities (founded c. 1134), was the natural venue for a learned inquiry into Columbus’s geographical theories. The University of Salamanca was a bastion of Thomistic theology, Aristotelian natural philosophy, and Ptolemaic cosmology — the intellectual framework against which Columbus’s proposals would be measured.

The council convened by Talavera met at the College of San Esteban in Salamanca, likely in late 1486 or early 1487. It brought together the leading theologians, astronomers, and cosmographers of Spain to examine Columbus’s plan to reach Asia by sailing west. The proceedings are among the most famous episodes in the Columbus story, though the exact details remain historically debated. Irving’s account, drawn principally from Fernando Columbus’s biography and Navarrete’s documents, presents the council as a pivotal moment of intellectual confrontation.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

The Intellectual Debate

The objections raised before the council fell into three broad categories:

  1. Theological objections — The most frequently cited (and most famously exaggerated in later tradition) concerned the impossibility of inhabited lands beyond the known world. Some theologians argued that if people lived on the other side of the globe, they could not have descended from Adam, as they would be cut off from the rest of humanity by impassable oceans — a violation of the Biblical account of human unity. Others cited St. Augustine’s dictum that no inhabited lands existed in the southern hemisphere, or invoked the authority of Lactantius and other Church Fathers who dismissed the idea of antipodes. Irving notes that these theological arguments were not as uniformly dismissive as legend suggests; several of the scholars were genuinely curious about Columbus’s evidence.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

  2. Cosmographical objections — The learned assembly argued from the inherited authority of Ptolemy, Strabo, and Aristotle. The circumference of the earth, they pointed out (following Ptolemy’s underestimated circumference, which Columbus also accepted), would make the westward voyage to Asia far longer than Columbus calculated — though ironically, Columbus’s own estimate was much too short. The spherical shape of the earth itself was not seriously disputed; this was a settled matter among educated Europeans. What was disputed was the distance and the likely impossibility of sustaining a voyage of that magnitude.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

  3. Practical objections — The council also raised more mundane concerns: the enormous cost, the length of time required, the certain suffering of crews undertaking such a voyage, and the unreliability of the winds and currents in the unknown ocean. These practical objections weighed heavily on a court already strained by the expenses of the Granada War.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

Columbus’s Defence

Columbus, who had educated himself in cosmography and whose personal library included works by Pierre d’Ailly, Marco Polo, and others, defended his proposals with arguments drawn from Scripture, classical geography, and the testimonies of mariners. He cited passages from the Apocrypha (the Book of Esdras, which suggested the earth was six-parts land to one-part water) and pointed to natural phenomena (fragments of carved wood, unusual vegetation, and bodies of unknown peoples washed ashore on Atlantic beaches). He also invoked the authority of Seneca’s Medea, which prophesied that a time would come when the ocean would loosen its chains and a great land would be discovered.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

The Outcome

The council at Salamanca did not issue a definitive rejection. Irving describes the outcome as inconclusive: the theologians and cosmographers were sceptical but not unanimous, and the commission’s report was deferred. This ambiguity — neither approval nor outright denial — became a pattern that would frustrate Columbus for years. The delay suited the Crown, which was reluctant to commit resources during wartime but also unwilling to lose the opportunity if the project proved viable.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. IV]

The Salamanca council nonetheless became, in later tradition, the emblematic example of narrow-minded scholasticism rejecting visionary ideas — a stereotype that Irving’s more nuanced account partly challenges and partly reinforces.

The Interim Years (1487–1491)

Between the Salamanca council and the signing of the Capitulations, Columbus spent nearly five years in a state of strategic limbo. He followed the Spanish court from camp to camp during the Granada campaign — to Malaga, to Baza, to Seville — repeatedly pressing his case and repeatedly being told to wait. The Crown paid him occasional stipends (records show sums disbursed in 1487, 1488, and 1489 to keep him from abandoning the cause), and he maintained contact with sympathetic figures such as the Duke of Medina Celi and the friars of La Rábida. He also sent his brother Bartholomew Columbus to England and France to test interest at those courts — a hedge against continued Spanish procrastination.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. V-VI]

During this period, Columbus’s personal fortunes declined. His wife Felipa Perestrello died, and he took Beatriz Enriquez de Arana as his companion; their son Fernando (later his biographer) was born in 1488. Columbus struggled financially and relied on the charity of the monastery at La Rábida and occasional payments from the Crown. His patience was repeatedly tested by the Talavera commission, which continued to deliberate without reaching a conclusion.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. V]

Santa Fe — The Capitulations (1492)

The Encampment

Santa Fe (Spanish for “Holy Faith”) was not a permanent town but a military encampment built by Ferdinand and Isabella’s army during the final siege of Granada. Located about two leagues (roughly 8 km) from the city walls, it was constructed in 1491 as a fortified base camp — effectively a planned city laid out in a grid pattern with proper streets, barracks, and a church, intended to impress the besieged Moors with the permanence and determination of the Christian forces. The name Santa Fe was chosen to express the religious character of the campaign.

After the surrender of Granada on January 2, 1492, the court remained at Santa Fe to conduct the business of the newly unified kingdom. It was here, in the weeks following the triumphant conclusion of the Reconquista, that the final act of Columbus’s seven-year negotiation took place.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. VII]

The Final Crisis

Columbus had been recalled to court after Fray Juan Perez’s intervention, and he arrived at Santa Fe in time to witness the surrender of Granada. He presented himself to the sovereigns with renewed hope, expecting that the end of the war would finally free them to consider his proposals. The Talavera commission, however, delivered its final adverse report: Columbus’s demands (the titles of Admiral, Viceroy, and Governor, with hereditary rights and one-tenth of all revenues) were judged excessive, and his project was deemed too uncertain to warrant such lavish rewards. Ferdinand, ever cautious, was inclined to let the matter drop.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. VII]

Columbus, in despair, left Santa Fe and began his journey toward France, intending to lay his proposals before King Charles VIII. He got as far as the bridge of Pinos near Granada when he was overtaken by a messenger — Luis de Santangel had intervened.

The Intervention of Luis de Santangel

Luis de Santangel, the keeper of the royal privy purse (escribano de racion) of Aragon, was a converted Jew (converso) who had long been sympathetic to Columbus’s enterprise. After hearing of the Talavera commission’s rejection, Santangel sought an immediate audience with Queen Isabella. He argued passionately that the cost of the expedition was modest — roughly the equivalent of a few days’ expenses for the court — compared to the immense potential reward: new lands, new subjects for the Christian faith, and a western route to the wealth of Asia. He even offered to lend the Crown the necessary funds from his own resources.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. VII]

Isabella, moved by Santangel’s appeal and perhaps stung by the thought that Columbus’s offer might enrich France instead, reversed her decision. She declared that the enterprise should go forward, even if she had to pawn her jewels to finance it. A messenger was dispatched posthaste; Columbus was intercepted and brought back to Santa Fe.

The Capitulations of Santa Fe (April 17, 1492)

The agreement, known as the Capitulations of Santa Fe, was signed on April 17, 1492, at the royal encampment. It was a formal contract between the Crown of Castile and Christopher Columbus, drawn up by the royal secretary Juan de Coloma. The principal terms were:

  • Admiral of the Ocean Sea — Columbus was granted the title of Admiral over all the seas, islands, and continents he might discover, with the same powers and privileges enjoyed by the High Admiral of Castile. The title was made hereditary in perpetuity.
  • Viceroy and Governor-General — Columbus was appointed Viceroy and Governor-General of all lands he discovered or acquired, with authority to appoint subordinate officials and administer justice.
  • One-tenth of all wealth — Columbus was entitled to retain one-tenth of all precious metals, spices, pearls, gems, and other merchandise produced or traded within his jurisdiction, after deducting costs.
  • One-eighth share — Columbus was permitted to contribute one-eighth of the cost of any future trading expedition and in return to receive one-eighth of the profits.
  • Appellate jurisdiction — Columbus was granted the right to hear and decide commercial disputes arising from his discoveries.

The Crown, for its part, retained ultimate sovereignty over all discovered lands and the right to approve or veto Columbus’s appointments. The agreement was signed at Santa Fe and countersigned by Ferdinand and Isabella at Granada later the same month.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. VIII]

Financing

The expedition was financed through a combination of sources. The Santa Hermandad (the Castilian holy brotherhood, a paramilitary police force) contributed a loan of approximately 1,140,000 maravedis. Luis de Santangel himself arranged a further loan of 1,400,000 maravedis. Columbus himself contributed about one-eighth of the total cost, raised from various sources including Italian merchants in Spain. The total cost of the expedition is estimated at roughly 2,000,000 maravedis — a modest sum compared to the Crown’s annual revenues, but a significant personal risk for Santangel.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book II Ch. VIII]

Aftermath

From Santa Fe, Columbus departed for Palos in late April or early May 1492 to prepare his fleet. The Capitulations represented the culmination of nearly seven years of negotiation, and their signing at Santa Fe — a temporary encampment built for war — symbolised the transformation of Spanish energy from the centuries-long Reconquista into the new project of Atlantic expansion.

Santa Fe itself remained a small settlement after the Granada campaign; parts of its original grid layout survive in the modern town of the same name. The capitulation document is preserved in the Archivo General de Indias in Seville.
