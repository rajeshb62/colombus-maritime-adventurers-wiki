Barcelona, Seville, and Valladolid — Columbus’s Spanish Cities

Three Spanish cities mark the triumphal peak, the declining years, and the final end of Christopher Columbus’s career in Spain: Barcelona, where he received his greatest honor; Seville, where he fell ill and petitioned in vain; and Valladolid, where he died.

Barcelona — Triumphal Reception (April 1493)

Barcelona, at the time the seat of the royal court of Ferdinand and Isabella, was the scene of Columbus’s most magnificent public honor. After his return from the first voyage, he was commanded by the sovereigns to present himself at court. Having made dispositions at Seville, he set out for Barcelona, taking with him six Taíno Indians, the various curiosities and natural productions of the New World, and samples of gold. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book V Ch. VI]

Journey to Barcelona

The fame of his discovery had resounded throughout the nation. Columbus’s journey resembled the progress of a sovereign: wherever he passed, inhabitants poured forth to line the roads and throng the villages. In the large towns, streets, windows, and balconies were filled with spectators acclaiming him. His progress was continually impeded by multitudes pressing to see him and the Indians, who were regarded with as much astonishment as if they had been natives of another planet.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book V Ch. VI]

Ceremonial Reception

It was about the middle of April 1493 that Columbus arrived at Barcelona. Every preparation had been made for a solemn and magnificent reception. The beauty and serenity of the spring weather in that favored climate added splendor to the ceremony.

The procession entered the city in Roman-triumph style:

  • First came the Indians, painted in their savage fashion and decorated with national ornaments of gold
  • Then various kinds of live parrots, stuffed birds and animals of unknown species, and rare plants
  • A conspicuous display of Indian coronets, bracelets, and other decorations of gold
  • Finally Columbus on horseback, surrounded by a brilliant cavalcade of Spanish chivalry

The streets were almost impassable from the multitude; windows and balconies were crowded; even the roofs were covered with spectators. The event was looked upon as a vast and signal dispensation of Providence in reward for the piety of the monarchs.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book V Ch. VI]

Audience with the Sovereigns

Ferdinand and Isabella had ordered their throne to be placed in public under a rich canopy of brocade of gold in a vast and splendid saloon. There the King and Queen awaited his arrival, seated in state with Prince Juan beside them, attended by the dignitaries of their court and the principal nobility of Castile, Valencia, Catalonia, and Aragon.

When Columbus entered the hall, the sovereigns rose as if receiving a person of the highest rank. He bent his knees to kiss their hands, but they raised him in the most gracious manner and ordered him to seat himself in their presence — a rare honor in that proud and punctilious court.

At their request, Columbus gave an account of the most striking events of his voyage and a description of the islands he had discovered. He displayed the specimens of unknown birds and animals, rare plants of medicinal and aromatic virtues, native gold in dust and crude masses or labored into barbaric ornaments, and above all the natives themselves. All these he pronounced mere harbingers of greater discoveries yet to make.

When he finished, the sovereigns sank to their knees and, with clasped hands raised to heaven and eyes filled with tears of joy and gratitude, poured forth thanks to God. The anthem of Te Deum laudamus was chanted by the choir of the royal chapel. As the venerable Las Casas wrote, “it seemed as if in that hour they communicated with celestial delights.”^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book V Ch. VI]

Sojourn at Barcelona

For many days Columbus was the object of universal curiosity; wherever he appeared he was surrounded by an admiring multitude. During his sojourn at Barcelona, the sovereigns took every occasion to bestow personal marks of their high consideration:

  • He was admitted at all times to the royal presence
  • The Queen delighted to converse with him on his enterprises
  • The King appeared on horseback with Prince Juan on one side and Columbus on the other
  • A coat of arms was assigned to him, quartering the royal castles and lions with a group of islands surrounded by waves
  • He was permitted to establish a mayorazgo (hereditary succession to his titles and estates)

Flush with success, Columbus also revived his pious scheme for the deliverance of the Holy Sepulchre, vowing to furnish an army of four thousand horse and fifty thousand foot within seven years for a crusade to rescue Jerusalem.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book V Ch. VII]

Seville — Illness and Petition (1504–1505)

Seville (Sevilla), the great inland port on the Guadalquivir River in Andalusia, was Columbus’s primary residence in Spain during his final years. It was the city from which he organized his second voyage in 1493, and to which he returned after his fourth and final voyage in 1504.

Return and Residence

After the disastrous fourth voyage, Columbus arrived back in Spain in November 1504, physically worn and burdened by the loss of his official positions and the collapse of his governorship. He took up residence in Seville, where he had long maintained connections. The city served as the administrative center for the affairs of the Indies, and Columbus’s brother Bartholomew Columbus had also spent time there.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. II]

Illness at Seville

Following his return, Columbus’s health deteriorated sharply. He suffered from gout and arthritis, the cumulative effects of decades of exposure and hardship at sea. His illness confined him much of the time and prevented him from personally pressing his claims at court with the energy he would have wished. Irving describes him during this period as “worn down by malady and vexation, and emaciated by care.”^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. II]

Petition to the Crown

From his sickbed in Seville, Columbus launched a sustained and ultimately fruitless campaign to restore his titles and revenues. He wrote lengthy letters and memorials to King Ferdinand, invoking the Capitulations of Santa Fe of 1492, which had granted him the titles of Admiral, Viceroy, and Governor of all lands he might discover, with a tenth of all revenues.

His petitions were met with evasion and delay. The Crown, under the influence of Bishop Juan Rodriguez de Fonseca and other adversaries, was reluctant to restore the extraordinary powers granted to Columbus a decade earlier. The legal and political obstacles raised against his claims proved insurmountable.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. II]

Death of Queen Isabella (November 26, 1504)

The death of Queen Isabella in November 1504 was a devastating blow to Columbus’s cause. Isabella had been his most steadfast patron at court, the one who had originally championed his enterprise against the doubts of Ferdinand and the Talavera commission. With her death, Columbus lost his most powerful advocate. King Ferdinand, never as enthusiastic about Columbus’s privileges, became the sole arbiter of his claims and proved far less sympathetic.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. II]

Journey to Court

In May 1505, Columbus — still suffering from illness and able to travel only with difficulty — journeyed from Seville to the court of King Ferdinand at Segovia for a personal audience. There he pleaded his case again, offering to accompany the king on any expedition and even proposing to finance a crusade to the Holy Land from his prospective revenues. Ferdinand, however, offered only vague promises; the titles and governance of the Indies would never be restored to Columbus.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. III]

Valladolid — Death of Columbus (May 20, 1506)

Valladolid, a city in the region of Old Castile on the Pisuerga River, was the place where Christopher Columbus died on May 20, 1506. The city was then a frequent residence of the royal court and lay approximately 120 miles north of Madrid.

Final Illness

After the failure of his petitions at court, Columbus’s health continued to decline. He followed the peripatetic royal court in its movements, and in early 1506 he was at Valladolid, where the court was then residing. There, in a modest house, he took to his bed for the last time. His final illness was a combination of gout, arthritis, and the general physical exhaustion of a man who had spent decades at sea and was now about 55 years old.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. IV]

Death and Testamentary Arrangements

On Ascension Day, May 20, 1506, Christopher Columbus died. His last days were spent in the presence of his two sons:

  • Diego Columbus — his legitimate son and heir, the future second Admiral of the Indies and Viceroy
  • Fernando (Ferdinand) Columbus — his natural son, the scholar and bibliophile who would later write his biography and amass the great Biblioteca Colombina in Seville

Also present were a few faithful servants and, according to some accounts, his brother Bartholomew.

In his final testamentary arrangements, Columbus reaffirmed the provisions of the mayorazgo (entailed estate) he had established at Seville in 1498. He directed that his remains be buried in the Cathedral of Santo Domingo in Hispaniola, the land he had discovered — a wish that would take several decades to fulfill and would involve multiple transfers of his bones across the Atlantic.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Book XVIII Ch. IV]

Circumstances of His Passing

The death of Columbus in Valladolid was a modest affair, far removed from the triumphal reception at Barcelona thirteen years earlier. He died in relative obscurity, his titles still unreinstated and his fortunes diminished. The court of King Ferdinand, preoccupied with other affairs, took little public notice. It was not until years later that the full measure of his achievement would be widely recognized.

Subsequent Translations of His Remains

Columbus’s body was originally buried in Valladolid, but his remains were later moved — first to the Monastery of La Cartuja in Seville, then (in 1542) to the Cathedral of Santo Domingo in Hispaniola, and finally (in 1795) to Havana, Cuba. Following the Cuban Revolution, they are now believed to rest in the Cathedral of Seville.^[source: irving-columbus-vol4.md, Appendix No. I]
