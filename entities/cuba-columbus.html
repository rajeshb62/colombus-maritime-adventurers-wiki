Cuba (Columbus Era)

Cuba — the largest island of the Greater Antilles — was first sighted by Christopher Columbus on October 28, 1492, during his first voyage across the Atlantic. Columbus named it Juana in honour of Prince Juan, heir to the Spanish throne. The island figured centrally in the Admiral’s geographical misconceptions: he first believed it was Cipango (Japan), then concluded it was part of the Asiatic mainland. Two embassies were sent inland seeking the Grand Khan of Cathay, and on a later expedition (Book VII, 1494) he explored its southern coast through the labyrinthine islands he named the Queen’s Garden (Jardines de la Reina). So convinced was Columbus that Cuba was a continent that he compelled his entire crew to swear a notarised oath to that effect under penalty of fine, flogging, and mutilation. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III-IV] ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. III-V]

Discovery (Book IV, Chapter III)

On the morning of October 28, 1492, after three days’ sailing from the island of Isabella (present-day Rum Cay, Bahamas), Columbus arrived in sight of Cuba. He had set sail at midnight on October 24, the wind, as he noted, blowing “most amorously.” Touching at a group of seven or eight small islands he called Islas de Arena (the modern Mucaras Islands), he crossed the Bahama bank and channel. The part first sighted is supposed to be the coast west of Nuevitas del Principe. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

Columbus was struck by the grandeur of the landscape — “its high and airy mountains, which reminded him of those of Sicily; its fertile valleys, and long sweeping plains watered by noble rivers; its stately forests; its bold promontories, and stretching headlands, which melted away into the remotest distance.” He anchored in a beautiful river, free from rocks or shoals, of transparent water, its banks overhung with trees. Landing and taking possession, he gave the island the name Juana and the river the name San Salvador. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

Two canoes had put off from the shore on the arrival of the ships, but their occupants fled in affright when the Spanish boat approached to sound the river. Columbus visited two abandoned cabins, finding only fishing implements — nets of palm fibre, hooks and harpoons of bone — and one of the same kind of mute dogs encountered on the smaller islands. He ordered that nothing be disturbed. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

Columbus’s Impression: Cipango and the Gilded Reports

During his cruise through the Bahamas, Columbus had gathered from the natives — through the imperfect medium of signs — reports of a great island to the south called Cuba. These reports, “coloured, and gilded, and exaggerated by his imagination,” led him to understand the land was of great extent, abounding in gold, pearls, and spices, frequented by large merchant ships. Comparing these misinterpreted accounts with the coast of Asia as laid down on Toscanelli’s map and the descriptions of Marco Polo, Columbus concluded that this island must be Cipango (Japan), and that the merchant ships belonged to the Grand Khan, who maintained extensive commerce in these seas. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. II-III]

He formed a plan: to sail for Cipango, establish relations of trade, seek the magnificent capital Quinsai (Hangzhou, described by Marco Polo), deliver the letters of the Castilian Sovereigns to the Grand Khan, receive his reply, and return triumphantly to Spain. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. II]

Coasting westward along Cuba, every new observation seemed to confirm the delusion. Columbus found shells of the kind of oyster that produces pearls, fragrant woods he believed were oriental spices, and the grass growing to the very edge of the water seemed proof of perpetual calm. “Cuba broke upon him like an elysium,” Irving writes. “It is the most beautiful island that eyes ever beheld, full of excellent ports and profound rivers.” ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

Embassy Inland Searching for the Grand Khan (Book IV, Chapters III-IV)

At the Cape of Palms (eastern entrance to the Laguna de Moron), three Indians from Guanahani informed Martin Alonso Pinzon that behind the cape lay a river from which it was but four days’ journey to Cubanacan, a province abounding in gold — the name meaning “the midst” in their language. Pinzon, steeped in Toscanelli’s map and Columbus’s Asian theories, mistook the word for Cublay Khan, the Tartar sovereign described by Marco Polo. He reported to Columbus that Cuba was not an island but terra firma, and that a local king was at war with the Great Khan. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

This communication “put an end to the delusion in which the Admiral had hitherto indulged, that this was the island of Cipango; but it substituted another no less agreeable.” Columbus now concluded he had reached the mainland of Asia (India), at no great distance from Mangi and Cathay, the ultimate destination of his voyage. He resolved to despatch a present to the supposed oriental monarch with the letters of recommendation from the Spanish sovereigns. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

After several days of contrary winds, he returned to a river he had named Rio de los Mares. On November 1, he sent boats ashore, but the inhabitants fled, fearing the armament was a slaving expedition of the Grand Khan. An interpreter swam to shore and calmed their fears; within hours, more than sixteen canoes surrounded the ships, bringing cotton yarn. Columbus forbade all trading for anything but gold. One native wore a piece of wrought silver in his nose and indicated — as Columbus understood it — that a king lived four days’ journey inland and that messengers had been sent to give him tidings of the strangers. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

Poring over Toscanelli’s map and musing on the misinterpreted words of the Indians, Columbus imagined himself on the borders of Cathay, about one hundred leagues from the capital of the Grand Khan. He determined not to wait for messengers, but to despatch two envoys to seek the monarch. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

The envoys were Rodrigo de Jerez and Luis de Torres — the latter a converted Jew who knew Hebrew and Chaldaic, languages Columbus supposed might be known to an oriental prince. Two Indians guided them, one from Guanahani and one from the river hamlet. They carried strings of beads for travelling expenses, specimens of spices and drugs, and instructions to inform the king that Columbus had been sent by the Castilian Sovereigns to establish amicable intercourse. They were also to ascertain the situation and distances of provinces, ports, and rivers that the Admiral specified by name from his descriptions of Asia. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. III]

After penetrating twelve leagues, the envoys reached a village of fifty houses containing at least a thousand inhabitants. They were received with solemnity, seated on chairs of state carved from single pieces of wood in the form of quadrupeds, and offered fruits and vegetables. Luis de Torres found his Hebrew, Chaldaic, and Arabic of no avail — the Lucayan interpreter had to be the orator. He extolled the power and munificence of the white men, and the Indians crowded around the envoys, touching them, kissing their hands and feet. Some women wore a slight covering of netted cotton; most of the inhabitants were entirely naked. There appeared to be ranks of society and a chieftain with some authority, unlike the complete equality prevailing among the islanders previously encountered. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. IV]

There was, however, “no appearance of gold, or other precious articles.” When shown specimens of cinnamon, pepper, and other spices, the inhabitants replied that such things were not to be found nearby, but far off to the southwest. The envoys returned, bringing with them one of the principal men, his son, and a domestic. On the return journey they witnessed, for the first time by Europeans, the smoking of tobacco — dried herbs rolled in a leaf, lit at one end and puffed from the mouth. ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. IV]

The report of the envoys dispelled many of Columbus’s splendid fancies about the barbaric prince and his capital. “Such were all the traces they found of the oriental city and court which they had anticipated.” ^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book IV Ch. IV]

Exploration of the Southern Coast and the Queen’s Garden (Book VII, 1494)

During his second voyage, Columbus set sail from the harbour of Isabella (Hispaniola) on April 24, 1494, with the plan of revisiting the coast of Cuba at the point where he had abandoned it during the first voyage. He still believed it to be a continent — the extreme end of Asia — and reasoned that by following its shores he must eventually arrive at Cathay. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. I]

Crossing the channel from Hispaniola, he sailed along the southern coast of Cuba, anchoring at Puerto Grande (Guantanamo), which he named for its size. The landscape was heavily cultivated, the shores “enlivened by numerous villages … cultivated to such a degree as to resemble gardens and orchards.” ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. I]

After discovering Jamaica and exploring its northern coast, Columbus returned to the southern coast of Cuba. Near the province of Macaca, a friendly cacique explained that Cuba was an island “of infinite extent,” but that no one had ever seen its end. This left the question of continent versus island unresolved. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. II-III]

Pressing westward, Columbus entered a labyrinth of keys, sandbanks, and small islands. “Finding them to increase in number, so as to render it impossible to give names to each, the admiral gave this whole labyrinth of islands, which in a manner enamelled the face of the ocean with variegated verdure, the name of the Queen’s Garden” (Jardines de la Reina), in honour of Queen Isabella. He recalled that Sir John Mandeville and Marco Polo had mentioned the coast of Asia was fringed with islands to the amount of several thousands, and persuaded himself that he was among that cluster. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. III]

The navigation was perilous: “The ships were obliged, in a manner, to grope their way, with men stationed at the mast-head, and the lead continually going.” They frequently touched upon sand banks. The variable weather followed a pattern — the wind rose in the east with the sun and died away in the west at sunset; heavy clouds gathered at evening, menacing tempests that melted away as the moon rose. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. III]

Among these islands, Columbus observed a remarkable fishing technique: the natives used a small fish with a flat head furnished with numerous suckers, tying a line to its tail and allowing it to swim until it attached itself to a turtle or larger fish, which was then drawn up. The Spaniards witnessed the taking of an immense turtle in this manner. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. III]

After emerging from the archipelago, Columbus landed at a large village on June 3, where he was received with the kindness and amity that distinguished the inhabitants of Cuba, whom “he extolled above all the other islanders for their mild and pacific character.” Here he erected a large cross with great ceremony, celebrated solemn mass on a Sunday morning, and received a remarkable address from a venerable Indian of fourscore years who warned Columbus against harming the innocent, speaking of rewards and punishments in the afterlife. Columbus was deeply moved by the “simple eloquence of this untutored savage.” ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. IV-V]

The Oath: Requiring the Crew to Swear Cuba Was a Continent (Book VII, Chapter V)

As Columbus continued coasting the southern side of Cuba, the ships became strained and leaky from repeated groundings, provisions grew scanty, and the crews — worn out by incessant labour — remonstrated against persisting further. They had already followed the coast far enough to satisfy their minds that it was a continent. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

Columbus, “as his imagination cooled, was himself aware of the inadequacy of his vessels to the voyage he had contemplated; but he felt it of importance to his fame, and to the popularity of his enterprizes, to furnish satisfactory proofs that the land he had discovered was a continent.” He persisted four more days exploring the coast as it bent to the southwest, until every one declared there could no longer be a doubt. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

The Admiral was determined, however, that “the fact should not rest merely on his own assertion, having had recent proofs of a disposition to gainsay his statements, and depreciate his discoveries.” He sent round a public notary, Fernan Perez de Luna, accompanied by four witnesses, to each of the vessels. Formal demand was made of every person on board — from the captain to the ship-boy — whether he had any doubt that the land before him was a continent: “the beginning and end of the Indias, by which any one might return overland to Spain.” If any entertained a doubt, he was called upon to express it, that it might be removed. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

The experienced navigators examined their maps, charts, reckonings, and journals, and after mature deliberation “declared under oath that they had no doubt upon the subject.” They grounded their belief on having coasted for three hundred and thirty-five leagues — an extent “unheard of as appertaining to an island” — while the land stretched forward interminably. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

Lest they should subsequently contradict the opinion thus solemnly avowed, a penalty was proclaimed by the notary: if any should offend in such manner, an officer would pay ten thousand maravedis; a ship-boy or person of like rank would receive a hundred lashes and have his tongue cut out. A formal statement was drawn up, including the depositions and names of every individual — a document that still existed in Irving’s time. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

This singular process took place near what is now called the Bay of Philippina or Bay of Cortes. As Irving notes, “at this very time, a ship-boy, from the mast-head, might have overlooked the group of islands to the south, and have beheld the open sea beyond. Two or three days further sail would have carried Columbus round the extremity of Cuba, would have dispelled his illusion, and might have given an entirely different course to his subsequent discoveries. In his present conviction he lived and died; believing to his last hour, that Cuba was the extremity of the Asiatic continent.” ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V]

Relinquishing further investigation, Columbus stood to the southeast on June 13, sighted the island he named Evangelista (present-day Isle of Pines), and subsequently continued to the island of Jamaica and then back to Hispaniola, closing the five-month exploration of the second voyage. ^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VII Ch. V-VII]
