Genoa and Lisbon — Columbus’s Birthplace and Adopted Home

Genoa

Genoa (Italian: Genova; Latin: Genua) is a port city on the Ligurian coast of northwestern Italy. In the fifteenth century it was a powerful maritime republic with a far-flung commercial empire reaching across the Mediterranean and into the Black Sea. It was the birthplace of Christopher Columbus, who was born there around 1435–1436, though the exact date and year remain uncertain.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. I]

Columbus’s Family Background

Columbus’s father, Domenico Colombo, was a wool-comber (lanaiuolo or cardatore di lana) who had long resided in Genoa. His trade was honest but humble, and the family was poor though respectable. Columbus was the eldest of four children. His brothers were Bartholomew (Bartolomeo) and Giacomo (also called Diego, the Spanish translation of his name). He had one sister, who married a man of obscure station named Giacomo Bavarello; nothing further is known of her.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. I]

The family surname in Italian is Colombo (meaning “dove”). Columbus himself latinized it to Columbus in his earlier letters, following the scholarly custom of the age. When he later moved to Spain he altered the name to Cristóbal Colón, a form derived from the supposed Latin root Colonus, adapted to Castilian.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. I]

Early Education

Columbus received a limited education, as befitted his family’s modest means. While still a child he was taught reading and writing — and wrote so good a hand, according to Las Casas, that he could have earned his bread by it. He also studied arithmetic, drawing, and painting, acquiring enough skill in each to have gained a livelihood. For a short time he was sent to the University of Pavia, the great school of learning in Lombardy, where he studied grammar and the Latin tongue. His education, however, was principally directed toward the sciences of maritime life: geometry, geography, astronomy (then called astrology), and navigation.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. I]

From his earliest childhood he evinced a strong passion for geographical science and an irresistible inclination for the sea. He later looked back on this early determination as a secret impulse from the Deity guiding him toward his destiny.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. I]

Apprenticeship at Sea

Columbus began his nautical career at the age of fourteen. Genoa, a city walled in by rugged mountains on the land side, offered little scope for enterprise on shore; but its opulent commerce and roving marine drew its youth naturally to the sea. The Mediterranean of this era was a rugged school: commercial voyages often resembled warlike cruises, piracy was semi-legitimized, and the seas were scenes of constant encounters between Italian states, Catalan privateers, and Mahometan powers.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

The first recorded voyage of Columbus was a naval expedition outfitted at Genoa in 1459 by John of Anjou, Duke of Calabria, to recover the kingdom of Naples for his father King René (Reinier) of Provence. The republic of Genoa furnished ships and money; many private adventurers also fitted out vessels under the Anjou banner. Among these was a hardy sea-captain named Colombo — actually two naval characters, an uncle and nephew, whom Fernando Columbus claimed as family connections. With these commanders Columbus sailed on several occasions and for a considerable length of time.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

Columbus at one time held a separate command in the service of King René and was employed in a daring enterprise to cut out a galley from the port of Tunis. The incident is preserved in Columbus’s own letter to Ferdinand and Isabella, written many years later. When his crew became fearful upon learning that two ships and a carrack were guarding the galley, and resolved to return to Marseilles, Columbus feigned compliance. He altered the ship’s compass at evening, and by the next morning the vessel was off Cape Carthagena while the crew still believed they were sailing toward Marseilles — a stratagem foreshadowing the false reckoning he would later employ on his first voyage of discovery.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

There follows an interval of many years during which only shadowy traces of Columbus survive. He is supposed to have been principally engaged in the Mediterranean and the Levant, sometimes in commerce, sometimes in warlike contests between Italian states, sometimes in expeditions against the infidels. He visited the island of Scio (Chios) and saw the harvesting of mastic. According to some authorities, he commanded several Genoese ships in the service of Louis XI of France in 1474, and attacked Spanish galleys as reprisal for an incursion into Roussillon. Another report places him in command of a Genoese squadron off Cyprus in 1475–1476.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

The Shipwreck off Portugal

The most dramatic episode of Columbus’s early maritime career — and the event that brought him to Portugal — is the desperate sea-fight narrated by his son Fernando. A Genoese corsair named Colombo the Younger, a kinsman of Columbus and a notorious rover, intercepted four richly laden Venetian galleys returning from Flanders on the Portuguese coast, between Lisbon and Cape St. Vincent. A furious engagement ensued, lasting from morning until evening. Columbus commanded a vessel that grappled with a huge Venetian galley; hand-grenades and fiery missiles were exchanged, and the galley burst into flames. The two ships, locked together by chains and iron grapplings, could not be separated, and both were consumed in a single conflagration. The crews threw themselves into the sea. Columbus seized a floating oar and, being an expert swimmer, reached the shore after a swim of two full leagues (approximately six miles).^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

After recovering from his exhaustion, Columbus repaired to Lisbon, where he found many of his Genoese countrymen and was induced to take up residence there. Modern historians note that the dating of this battle is problematic — it may have occurred in 1485, well after Columbus had left Portugal — and Fernando may have confused it with another action. Nonetheless, the story captures the hardihood and resourcefulness that marked Columbus’s early character.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. II]

Lisbon

Lisbon (Portuguese: Lisboa) is the capital and principal port of Portugal, situated on the north bank of the Tagus River where it opens into the Atlantic Ocean. In the late fifteenth century, under the reign of King Afonso V and then King John II, Lisbon became the vibrant hub of Portuguese maritime discovery — the great resort of men skilled in geographical and nautical science from all parts of the world. It was here that Columbus would spend the most formative years of his intellectual life, developing the theory that would lead to the discovery of the New World.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

Arrival and Marriage

Columbus arrived in Lisbon around 1470, in the full vigor of manhood. Contemporary descriptions — from his son Fernando and from Las Casas — portray him as tall, well-formed, muscular, and of an elevated and dignified demeanor. His visage was long, his complexion fair and freckled, inclined to ruddy; his nose aquiline, his cheek-bones rather high, his eyes light grey and apt to enkindle. His hair in youth was light in color, but care and trouble soon turned it grey; at thirty years of age it was quite white. He was moderate and simple in diet and apparel, eloquent in discourse, engaging and affable with strangers, and of an amiableness in domestic life that strongly attached his household to his person. His temper was naturally irritable, but he subdued it by magnanimity of spirit, comporting himself with courteous and gentle gravity. Throughout his life he was noted for strict attention to the offices of religion.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

While attending religious service at the Convent of All Saints in Lisbon, Columbus became acquainted with Doña Felipa Moñis de Perestrello (or Palestrello), a lady of rank who resided at the convent. She was the daughter of Bartolomeo Moñis de Perestrello, an Italian cavalier who had been one of the most distinguished navigators under Prince Henry the Navigator, and who had colonized and governed the island of Porto Santo in the Madeira archipelago. The acquaintance ripened into attachment and ended in marriage — apparently a match of mere affection, as the lady was destitute of fortune.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

This union fixed Columbus in Lisbon. The newly married couple went to reside with the mother of Felipa, who, perceiving Columbus’s interest in matters of the sea, related everything she knew of her late husband’s voyages and expeditions, and brought him all his papers, charts, journals, and memorandums. These were treasures to Columbus. He acquainted himself with all the routes of the Portuguese, their plans and conceptions, and by his marriage and residence became naturalized in Portugal.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

Years of Study and Chart-Making

Columbus sailed occasionally in Portuguese expeditions to the coast of Guinea. When on shore, he occupied himself in making maps and charts for the support of his family. His circumstances were limited, and he had to observe strict economy; nonetheless, he appropriated a part of his scanty means to the succor of his aged father in Genoa and to the education of his younger brothers.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

The construction of a correct map or chart in those days required considerable knowledge and skill, and the superior correctness of Columbus’s maps and charts gave him notoriety among men of science. Geography was just emerging from the darkness of the Middle Ages; Ptolemy remained the standard authority, and the maps of the fifteenth century displayed a mixture of truth and error. At this period, when the passion for maritime discovery was seeking every aid, the knowledge of an able cosmographer like Columbus was properly appreciated.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

As early as 1474, Columbus entered into correspondence with Paulo Toscanelli of Florence, one of the most scientific men of the day. Toscanelli’s communications had great influence in inspiriting Columbus toward his subsequent undertakings. In a letter applauding Columbus’s design of a westward voyage, Toscanelli sent him a map projected partly according to Ptolemy and partly according to the descriptions of Marco Polo. On this map, the eastern coast of Asia was depicted in front of the western coasts of Africa and Europe, with a moderate space of ocean between them, in which were placed the islands of Cipango (Japan) and Antilla at convenient distances. Columbus procured the work of Marco Polo, whose marvelous accounts of the riches of Cathay (northern China) and Mangi (southern China), and the gold-covered palace of the monarch of Cipango, inflamed his imagination and became a key to his subsequent representations to sovereigns.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VI]

In 1477, Columbus made a voyage to the north of Europe, sailing one hundred leagues beyond Thule — generally supposed to be Iceland — in the month of February. He noted that the sea was not frozen, and that the tides rose and fell twenty-six fathoms. This voyage beyond the Ultima Thule of the ancients reveals his ardent and impatient desire to break away from the limits of the known world.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VI]

Residence at Porto Santo

Columbus resided for some time at the recently discovered island of Porto Santo, where his wife had inherited some property. During this residence, Felipa bore him a son, whom he named Diego. Living on the very frontier of discovery, Columbus was frequently in company with his brother-in-law Pedro Correo, a navigator of note who had been governor of Porto Santo. Their domestic conversation naturally turned upon the discoveries prosecuting along the African coasts, the long-sought route to India, and the possibility of unknown lands existing in the west. The island residence was frequently visited by voyagers going to and from Guinea. Living thus surrounded by the stir and bustle of discovery, Columbus’s ardent mind kindled to enthusiasm.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. IV]

The Development of His Theory

Columbus’s theory of undiscovered lands in the west rested on three categories of evidence, as recorded by his son Fernando from his father’s papers:^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. V]

1. The nature of things: The earth was a sphere that could be traveled around from east to west. Columbus, following Ptolemy and the earlier map of Marinus of Tyre, calculated that fifteen of the twenty-four hours of the earth’s circumference were known to the ancients (from the Canaries to the city of Thine in Asia). The Portuguese had advanced the western frontier (the Azores and Cape Verde) by one hour more, leaving eight hours — one-third of the globe — unknown. This space, he reasoned, must be largely filled by the eastern regions of Asia, which might extend so far as to approach the western shores of Europe and Africa. The intervening ocean would therefore be narrower than commonly supposed, especially if Alfraganus’s smaller estimate of the earth’s circumference were adopted.

2. Authority of learned writers: Aristotle, Seneca, and Pliny held that one might pass from Cadiz to the Indies in a few days. Strabo observed that the ocean surrounds the earth, bathing the shores of India on the east and those of Spain on the west. Marco Polo and Sir John Mandeville described the eastward extent of Asia far beyond Ptolemy’s limits, and their accounts of Cathay and Cipango convinced Columbus that a voyage of no great duration westward would bring him to those wealthy shores.

3. Reports of navigators: Martin Vicenti, a Portuguese pilot, reported finding a piece of carved wood — evidently not worked with an iron instrument — after sailing 450 leagues west of Cape St. Vincent. Pedro Correo had seen similar wood on Porto Santo. The King of Portugal reported giant reeds drifting from the west, which Columbus recognized as resembling the immense reeds said by Ptolemy to grow in India. Inhabitants of the Azores reported huge pine-trunks (of species unknown on those islands) and two dead men of unfamiliar features cast onto the island of Flores. A mariner from the port of St. Mary claimed to have seen land to the west while voyaging to Ireland.

From this chain of reasoning, Columbus concluded that undiscovered, fertile, and inhabited land existed in the western ocean and was attainable by a westward voyage.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. V]

Proposition to the Court of Portugal

After the application of the astrolabe to navigation — a timely invention that divested his enterprise of much of its hazardous character — Columbus obtained an audience with King John II of Portugal. He proposed, if the king would furnish ships and men, to undertake a shorter and more direct route to India than the African coastal route the Portuguese were pursuing. His plan was to strike directly west across the Atlantic. He unfolded his hypothesis concerning the extent of Asia and described the immense riches of the island of Cipango, the first land at which he expected to arrive.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VII]

According to his son Fernando, the king listened with great attention and was initially discouraged by the cost and trouble already sustained in the African route. But Columbus supported his proposition with such excellent reasons that the king was induced to give consent; the only remaining difficulty was Columbus’s demands for high and honorable titles and rewards — demands he made, says Fernando, so that he might leave behind a name and family worthy of his deeds.

The Portuguese historian Barros, writing in a cooler spirit, attributes the king’s seeming acquiescence merely to importunity. The proposition was referred to a learned junto charged with matters of maritime discovery. This body consisted of Master Roderigo and Master Joseph (the king’s two physician-cosmographers) and Diego Ortiz de Cazadilla, Bishop of Ceuta, a man greatly reputed for learning. This scientific body treated the project as extravagant and visionary.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VII]

The king convoked a larger council of prelates and learned men. The Bishop of Ceuta delivered a speech opposing not only Columbus’s project but all further African discoveries, arguing they drained the nation’s resources. The Count of Villa Real, Don Pedro de Meneses, replied with a lofty patriotic oration defending the Portuguese discoveries. The council ultimately condemned Columbus’s proposal.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VII]

The Secret Caravel and Departure from Portugal

Certain councillors, seeing that the king remained dissatisfied and still harbored an inclination for the enterprise, suggested a stratagem. It was proposed that a caravel be secretly dispatched in the direction Columbus had pointed out to test his theory, while Columbus himself was kept in suspense. This perfidious advice — attributed to the Bishop of Ceuta — the king unfortunately accepted.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VIII]

Columbus was required to furnish a detailed plan of his proposed voyage, with charts and documents. He complied readily. A caravel was then despatched under the pretext of carrying provisions to the Cape Verde Islands, but with private instructions to pursue the route Columbus had designated. Departing from those islands, the caravel stood westward for several days until stormy weather, the pilots’ lack of zeal, and the sight of nothing but an immeasurable waste of waves caused them to lose courage. They put back to the Cape Verdes and then to Lisbon, excusing their failure by ridiculing Columbus’s project as extravagant and irrational.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VIII]

This unworthy attempt to defraud him of his enterprise aroused Columbus’s indignation. King John is said to have offered to renew the negotiation, but Columbus resolutely declined. His wife Felipa had died by this time; the domestic tie that bound him to Portugal was broken. Toward the end of 1484, he departed secretly from Lisbon, taking with him his young son Diego. He feared being prevented by the king, and his affairs had run to ruin — he was even in danger of arrest for debt.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VIII]

From Portugal, Columbus carried his proposal first to Genoa, where he appealed to his native republic but received a contemptuous refusal. The Genoese republic, languishing under a long decline and embarrassed by war, was in no condition to undertake such a venture. He then proceeded to Venice, where an old tradition holds that his offer was declined due to the critical state of national affairs. After visiting his aged father in Genoa, Columbus departed to try his fortunes in the Spanish court — the beginning of a new and decisive chapter in his career.^[source: irving-columbus.md, Book I Ch. VIII]
