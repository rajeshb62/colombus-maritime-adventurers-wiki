Ferdinand Magellan

Ferdinand Magellan (Portuguese: Fernao de Magalhaes; Spanish: Fernando de Magallanes; c. 1480–1521) was a Portuguese-born navigator and explorer who led the first expedition to circumnavigate the globe. Originally in the service of Portugal, he transferred his allegiance to Spain and commanded the Armada de Molucca (five ships, 1519–1522), discovering the Strait of Magellan at the southern tip of South America and becoming the first European to cross the Pacific Ocean. He was killed at the Battle of Mactan in the Philippines before the voyage was completed; the expedition’s sole surviving ship, the Victoria, returned to Spain under Juan Sebastian Elcano. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Narrative Overview

Magellan was born around 1480 near the town of Villa Real in the province of Traz os Montes, in northern Portugal. He was the son of a noble but not wealthy hidalgo (minor noble) who maintained a fortified ancestral home in the mountainous region. Young Fernan (as he was then called) was raised in comfort, spending his boyhood hunting wild boar and deer in the mountains, fighting brigands who infested the region, and enjoying the rough outdoor life of the Portuguese countryside. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

His family spent part of each year in Oporto, the port city on the Atlantic. There the young Magellan became fascinated with the sea: he befriended captains and sailors, explored the caravels in the harbor, and listened to tales of perilous voyages, shipwrecks, and the discoveries of Columbus and Vasco da Gama. He yearned for a life of adventure beyond the quiet valleys of his home. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

At about age twenty, Magellan asked his father for permission to go to the royal court in Lisbon to seek a career. His father consented, equipped him with fine clothes, armor, and a white horse, and Magellan traveled to Lisbon to seek service with King Manuel of Portugal. The king received him graciously and gave him a place in the royal household. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen notes that at age twelve Magellan and his brother Diogo moved to Lisbon to become pages at the royal court, where Ferdinand received education in “religion, writing, mathematics, music and dance, horsemanship, martial arts, and, thanks to the legacy of Prince Henry the Navigator, algebra, geometry, astronomy, and navigation.” He came of age hearing about Portuguese and Spanish discoveries in the Indies and was privy to the secrets of Portuguese exploration of the ocean, even assisting with preparing fleets leaving for India. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Service in India Under Albuquerque and the Battle of Diu

Magellan’s court service did not satisfy his ambition. The return of Vasco da Gama from India and the preparations for a major expedition under Francisco D’Almeyda, the first viceroy of India, stirred him to action. He secured a place in D’Almeyda’s fleet and sailed for India at about age twenty-five. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

In India and East Africa, Magellan fought in numerous battles: the assault on Quiloa, the capture of Mombaza, and the campaigns at Cochin, Cananore, Coulam, and Onor. He served under both D’Almeyda and the great Portuguese general Alfonso de Albuquerque, taking part in the attack on Ormuz (1507). He distinguished himself for bravery and leadership, notably when he voluntarily remained on a stranded ship while officers evacuated, inspiring the sailors to remain calm. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen provides additional depth on this period: following the death of his patron King Joao II in 1495, Manuel I mistrusted Magellan, who had been allied with the opposition. Finally in 1505, after a decade of anonymous court service, Magellan and his brother Diogo received assignments aboard a fleet of twenty-two ships bound for India under Francisco de Almeida. Magellan spent the next eight years trying to establish a permanent Portuguese presence in India, “dashing from one trading post to another, and from one battle to the next; he survived multiple wounds and, if nothing else, learned to stay alive in a hostile environment.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

He fell out with Albuquerque by opposing the planned attack on Goa in a council of war, arguing that contrary winds would prevent the ships from returning to Portugal that year. This opposition cost him Albuquerque’s favor and effectively ended his service in India. During this period he invested most of his fortune with a merchant who soon died; in the ensuing confusion, Magellan lost most of his assets. He petitioned King Manuel for restitution, but the king refused. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt] ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The Moroccan Campaign and Permanent Lameness

Magellan then transferred to Morocco, where he commanded Portuguese troops at the settlement of Azamor, engaging in almost continual conflicts with Moors and Arabs. In one rash sally, he was struck by a poisoned javelin that entered his leg. Though he recovered, he was permanently lame from that wound for the rest of his life. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen recounts the Moroccan campaign in greater detail. In 1513, when the city of Azamor refused to pay its annual tribute to Portugal, King Manuel responded with the largest seaborne force ever to sail for his kingdom: five hundred ships and fifteen thousand soldiers. Among them was Magellan, riding an aging steed — the only mount he could afford on his reduced budget. He lost his horse to the Arabs and barely escaped the siege with his life. Insulted that the Portuguese army offered only a fraction of the horse’s value as compensation, Magellan wrote directly to the king, circumventing ministers — an act of hotheadedness and tactlessness that typified his career. The king dismissed the request. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan stubbornly remained at his post, acquired a new horse, and participated in daily skirmishes with Arabs who swooped out of the desert to harass Portuguese soldiers guarding Azamor. “In one confrontation, he received a serious wound from an Arab lance, which left him with a shattered knee and a lifelong limp; it also ended his career as a soldier.” His battle service earned him promotion to quartermaster, entitling him to a share of spoils. In a subsequent battle, Arabs surrendered a herd of over 200,000 goats, camels, and horses. Magellan was among the officers responsible for distributing spoils and decided to pay off tribal allies with some captured animals. He and another officer were indicted for selling four hundred goats to the enemy and keeping the proceeds. The charges were preposterous — Magellan was entitled to his spoils — but he failed to respond, left Morocco without authorization, and appeared before King Manuel demanding an increase in his allowance, lecturing the king on his lifelong service and wounds. “Jealous rivals whispered behind Magellan’s back that his limp was merely an act designed to elicit sympathy.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

King Manuel ordered Magellan to return to Morocco to face charges of treason, corruption, and leaving the army without authorization. A tribunal dismissed all charges, and he returned with a letter of recommendation. But when he again demanded an increased allowance, the king refused. “Short and dark, and teetering on the brink of poverty, he looked nothing like the aristocrat he thought himself to be.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Rejection by King Manuel I and Renunciation of Portuguese Citizenship

Disillusioned with the petty wars in Morocco and convinced that greater glory lay in exploration, Magellan returned to Lisbon to petition King Manuel for an expedition to find a westward route to the Molucca (Spice) Islands. His proposal was based on his study of maps and his friendship with Francisco Serrano, who had written of the riches of the Moluccas. King Manuel, who had taken a dislike to Magellan (partly due to the Goa incident), curtly refused. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen reveals that Magellan brought Ruy Faleiro, a mathematician, astronomer, and nautical scholar, to help persuade the king. But King Manuel was already prejudiced against both men. At age fifty-one, the king had entered a personal crisis: his wife died in childbirth, he briefly abdicated, then changed his mind and arranged to marry his son’s fiancee, Leonor, King Charles’s sister — a situation causing scandal. “Three times, Magellan asked for royal authorization for a voyage to the Indies to discover a water route to the fabled but little known Spice Islands. Three times, the king, who had disliked and mistrusted Magellan for more than twenty years, refused.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Finally, in September 1517, Magellan asked if he could offer his services elsewhere. To his astonishment, the king replied that Magellan was free to do as he pleased. “When Magellan knelt to kiss the king’s hands, as custom dictated, King Manuel concealed them behind his cloak and turned his back on his petitioner.” Within days of arriving in Seville, Magellan signed documents formally making him a subject of Castile and its young king, Charles I. No longer Fernao de Magalhaes, he became known as Hernando de Magallanes. He had formally renounced his allegiance to King Manuel and given his loyalty to King Charles. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Marriage to Beatriz Barbosa

In the palace corridor after his rejection, Magellan was approached by his friend Ruy Faleiro, an astrologer, who encouraged him to offer his services to King Charles of Spain instead. Taking Faleiro’s advice, Magellan resigned his Portuguese commission and traveled to Seville, where his cousin Diego Barbosa was mayor. There he reunited with Barbosa’s daughter Beatrix (whom he had known in Lisbon), courted her, and won her hand in marriage. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen adds that Magellan became acquainted with Diogo Barbosa, another Portuguese expatriate who had settled in Seville fourteen years earlier and was now Knight Commander of the Order of Santiago. “At the same time, Magellan began to woo Diogo’s daughter Beatriz; the relationship developed very quickly, and they married before the year was out. Suddenly, Magellan had an important sponsor in Seville, as well as a financial stake, because Beatriz brought with her a dowry of 600,000 maravedis. She might have been pregnant at the time of their marriage; the child, named Rodrigo, was born the following year.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan and Faleiro traveled to Valladolid to present their proposal to King Charles (later Emperor Charles V). Despite opposition from Portuguese Ambassador Alvaro da Costa (who attempted to bribe, persuade, and even assassinate Magellan), and from jealous Spanish courtiers, Charles approved the expedition. The agreement granted Magellan command of five ships, the title of admiral, a twentieth of revenues from discovered lands, and the governorship of any islands discovered. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen notes that the Portuguese ambassador, Alvaro da Costa, sought out Magellan, promising King Manuel would reconsider. “Magellan refused to be swayed by the ambassador’s entreaties. He suspected that if he returned to Portugal he would be thrown into jail, tried for treason, and executed.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Political Context: The Line of Demarcation and the Spice Trade

Bergreen situates Magellan’s voyage within the broader geopolitics of the era. On June 7, 1494, Pope Alexander VI — a Spaniard, Rodrigo de Borja — divided the world between Spain and Portugal through the Treaty of Tordesillas. The line of demarcation extended from the North Pole to the South Pole, one hundred leagues (about four hundred miles) west of the Cape Verde Islands. Spain received the western portion, Portugal the eastern. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The spice trade was the driving economic force: “Like oil today, the European quest for spices drove the world’s economy and influenced global politics.” Cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, pepper, and mace were the most precious commodities. Arab merchants maintained a monopoly by concealing the origins of spices, insisting they came from Africa when they actually grew in India, China, and Southeast Asia. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 severed the overland spice routes, opening new possibilities for any European nation able to master the seas. For those willing to assume the risks, “the rewards of an oceanic spice trade, combined with control over the world’s economy, were irresistible.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan’s proposal to Charles V argued that the Spice Islands lay within the Spanish hemisphere, based partly on the map of his friend Francisco Serrano, who had written from Ternate describing “a new world richer and greater than that of Vasco da Gama.” Magellan also displayed his slave Enrique, a native of the Spice Islands, as an interpreter. The contract signed on March 22, 1518, granted Magellan a ten-year exclusive on voyages to the Spice Islands (which Charles ultimately violated by dispatching follow-up expeditions), 5 percent of revenues from discovered lands, and governorship of any islands discovered. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Command of the Armada de Molucca

Magellan’s fleet consisted of five ships: the Trinidad (flagship, 100 tons), the San Antonio (80 tons), the Conception (80 tons), the Victoria (60 tons), and the Santiago (60 tons). The captains were Spanish: Juan de Cartagena (San Antonio), Gaspar de Quesada (Conception), and Luis de Mendoza (Victoria); with Juan Serrano (Santiago) as Magellan’s trusted friend. The fleet carried 250 men, 80 cannon, and enough provisions for two years. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen adds crucial context about the political intrigues. Archbishop Juan Rodriguez de Fonseca, who controlled the Casa de Contratacion and despised Magellan, appointed his own illegitimate son Juan de Cartagena as inspector general with a salary higher than Magellan’s. Fonseca also appointed his close allies Luis de Mendoza and Gaspar de Quesada as captains. “Not surprisingly, all three captains appointed by Fonseca — Cartagena, Quesada, and Mendoza — despised and looked down on Magellan from the moment they came on board.” The ship names and tonnages also differ slightly in Bergreen: San Antonio 120 tons, Concepcion 90 tons, Victoria 85 tons, Santiago 75 tons. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan married Beatrix Barbosa in the Seville Cathedral before departing. He left her pregnant, never to see her again. The fleet sailed from Seville in August 1519 and from San Lucar on September 20, 1519. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Circumnavigation

The fleet crossed the Atlantic, stopping at the Canary and Cape Verde Islands, and reached the coast of Brazil in December 1519. They encountered friendly natives who worshipped them as rain-bringers, endured storms, and observed marine wonders (sharks, flying fish). They explored the Rio de la Plata and continued southward as winter approached. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Port St. Julian Mutiny: Mendoza’s Death, Quesada’s Beheading, Cartagena Marooned

At Port St. Julian (Argentina), Magellan wintered from March to August 1520. There he faced a serious mutiny led by Cartagena, Quesada, and Mendoza. He suppressed it ruthlessly: Mendoza was killed on his own deck by Magellan’s constable, Cartagena was executed by firing squad, and Quesada was imprisoned. The mutineers’ bodies were quartered and displayed. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen provides far richer detail. The Easter mutiny of 1520 saw Quesada and Cartagena seize three ships — the San Antonio, Concepcion, and Victoria — leaving Magellan with only the Trinidad and the Santiago. Bergreen describes how Magellan’s constable, Gonzalo Gomez de Espinosa, along with several loyalists, approached the Victoria in a longboat. Espinosa boarded, stabbed Captain Luis de Mendoza through the throat, and killed him on his own deck. Magellan’s men then cut the anchor cables, leaving the mutinous ship to drift. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The San Antonio and Concepcion attempted to escape the harbor at night, but their anchor cables parted in the current and they drifted helplessly past the Trinidad, where Magellan’s gunners opened fire. The mutineers’ ships were captured. Magellan convened a trial; Gaspar de Quesada was condemned to death, beheaded, and his body drawn and quartered, with his severed head displayed on a pike at the harbor’s entrance. Juan de Cartagena and a priest, Pero Sanchez de la Reina, were not executed but were sentenced to marooning. On August 11, 1520, both were left on a small island in sight of the ships “with no longboat, no firewood, and scant clothing. Their supplies consisted mainly of bread and wine, enough to last them the summer.” Their fate was never known; they were never rescued. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

At St. Julian, Magellan encountered the Patagonian giants, whom he and his men observed with great curiosity. He captured two as interpreters. The Santiago was wrecked on a scouting mission. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen narrates the Patagonian encounters at length: Magellan’s initial friendly contact with the Tehuelche people, the conversion of one giant named “John” to Christianity, the subsequent deterioration of relations when Magellan’s men discovered a cache of Indian weapons, the trick of luring four giants aboard the Trinidad and fettering them, and the final violent clash that left crew member Diego Barrasa dead from a poisoned arrow. Two Patagonian captives remained aboard, one of whom (named “Paul”) later died and was buried at sea after conversion by Pigafetta. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Discovery of the Strait

On October 21, 1520 (the Day of the Eleven Thousand Virgins), after sailing south from St. Julian, Magellan discovered the strait that now bears his name. The passage was treacherous and winding, with branching channels. The San Antonio deserted and returned to Spain. After five weeks, Magellan emerged into the open ocean at the western end, weeping for joy at the discovery. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen describes the 350-mile strait as a “nautical nightmare” with tides exceeding twenty-four feet and powerful currents. He recounts how Magellan dispatched the San Antonio and Concepcion to scout ahead, the terrifying “williwaw” storms (violent squalls peculiar to the strait), and the dramatic reunion when the two scout ships returned “with sails full and banners flying to the wind” discharging mortars in celebration. Bergreen also details the desertion of the San Antonio under Esteban Gomes, who had long resented Magellan’s leadership and whose opposition had “set the stage for another mutiny.” Gomes stabbed Magellan’s cousin Alvaro de Mesquita, seized the ship, and returned to Spain. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The 99-Day Pacific Crossing: Starvation, Scurvy, Rats, Leather, and Sawdust

The subsequent crossing of the Pacific took 99 days. Magellan named the ocean “Pacific” because of its placid surface. The crossing was a nightmare of starvation: the sailors ate rats, leather from the rigging, and sawdust; scurvy ravaged the crew; many died. They sighted only two barren uninhabited islands before reaching Guam in the Marianas in March 1521. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen’s account is harrowing and comprehensive. He explains the sheer scale of the Pacific — encompassing one-third of the earth’s surface, sixty-three million square miles — and Magellan’s fatal underestimation of its size. For ninety-eight days after leaving the strait, the fleet saw nothing but ocean. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s account, quoted by Bergreen, describes the appalling deterioration: “We were three months and twenty days without getting any kind of fresh food. We ate biscuit which was no longer biscuit, but powder of biscuits swarming with worms, for they had eaten the good. It stank strongly of the urine of rats. We drank yellow water that had been putrid for many days. We also ate some ox hides that covered the top of the mainyard to prevent the yard from chafing the shrouds, and which had become exceedingly hard because of the sun, rain, and wind. We left them in the sea for four or five days, and then placed them for a few moments on top of the embers, and so ate them; and often we ate sawdust from boards. Rats were sold for one-half ducado apiece, and even then we could not get them.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Scurvy claimed at least twenty-nine men. Gums swelled, teeth fell out, old scars reopened, and men’s bodies literally fell apart. Bergreen explains that Magellan and his officers were spared because they regularly ate preserved quince, which unknowingly contained an antiscorbutic. “Throughout the ordeal, the officers regularly dipped into their supply of preserved quince, an applelike fruit, without realizing it was actually a potent antiscorbutic.” The only landfall before Guam were two barren atolls — San Pablo (January 24) and Shark Island (February 4) — where no anchorage could be found. Desperate, Magellan “flung his useless maps overboard, crying, ‘With the pardon of the cartographers, the Moluccas are not to be found in their appointed place!‘” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The Philippines and Death at Mactan

From Guam the fleet reached the Philippines, first landing at the island of Mazzava, where Magellan established friendly relations with the local king through a blood-brotherhood ceremony and the exchange of gifts. The Malay interpreter he had brought with him from earlier voyages proved invaluable. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Easter Sunday Cross (March 31, 1521)

Pigafetta, who was present throughout, provides the sole eyewitness account of the Easter Sunday ceremonies at Mazzava (Limasawa). “Early on the morning of Sunday, the last of March, and Easter Day, the captain-general sent the priest with many men to prepare for saying mass, and the interpreter went to tell the king that we were not going to land in order to dine with him, but to say mass.” The king sent two slaughtered swine. “When the hour for mass arrived, we landed with about fifty men, without our body armour, but carrying our other arms, and dressed in our best clothes.” Magellan sprinkled the two kings with musk water before the mass began. “We set up the cross there, for those people were heathens.” Had they been Moors, Pigafetta notes, “we would have erected there a column as a sign of greater harshness, for the Moors are much harder to convert than the heathens.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Account of Magellan’s Death at Mactan (April 27, 1521)

Pigafetta’s journal is the sole surviving eyewitness report of the Mactan battle. He records that Magellan took sixty men in three boats, crossing at midnight under a calm, starlit sky. The boats could not approach the shore due to coral rocks and shallow water, so the Spaniards waded through the surf. The native force numbered approximately 1,500. “So many of them charged down upon us that they shot the captain through the right leg with a poisoned arrow.” Magellan ordered a slow retreat, but the men fled. “The natives shot only at our legs because they were naked.” Only six or eight men remained with the captain. “Recognizing the captain, so many turned upon him that they knocked his helmet off his head twice, but he always stood firm like a good knight.” After more than an hour of fighting: “An Indian hurled a bamboo spear into the captain’s face. The latter immediately killed him with his lance.” Magellan then “trying to lay hand on sword, he could draw it out only halfway, because he had been wounded in the arm with a bamboo spear.” At that moment “one of them wounded him on the left leg with a large terciado, which resembles a scimitar, only being larger; that caused the captain to fall face downward. Immediately they rushed upon him with iron and bamboo spears and with their cutlasses, until they killed our mirror, our light, our comfort, and our true guide.” Pigafetta’s eulogy follows: “Among the other virtues, he was more constant than anyone else in the greatest of storms; he endured hunger better than all others; and more accurately than any man in the world did he chart and navigate.” After the battle, the Christian king of Cebu sent a message offering merchandise for Magellan’s body. The people of Mactan “answered that they would not give up such a man, as we had hoped they would, and that they would not give him up for all the riches in the world, but that they intended to keep him as a memorial.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Proceeding to the larger island of Sebu (Cebu), Magellan negotiated an alliance with its king, who agreed to become a Christian and a vassal of Spain. On April 14, 1521, the king and his court were baptized in an elaborate ceremony; hundreds of his subjects followed. The king then asked Magellan’s help in subduing a neighboring chief, Cilapulapu of the nearby island of Matan (Mactan), who refused to submit. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

On April 27, 1521, Magellan attacked Mactan with sixty men in three boats. The water was too shallow for the boats to approach the shore, so the Spaniards waded through the surf. They were met by a large force of natives armed with arrows, spears, stones, and scimitars. The battle was fierce; Magellan fought at the front of his men. He received a lance wound in the face, a javelin wound in the right arm, and a scimitar blow to the left leg that brought him down. A swarm of natives fell upon him with spears and stones, and Magellan was killed on the beach. Cilapulapu refused to surrender his body, saying he would keep it as a monument of his triumph. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen adds that Magellan’s landing force consisted of only forty-nine men against approximately 1,500 warriors. The dead included Cristovao Rebelo, Magellan’s illegitimate son who had served as his constant companion on the voyage. Bergreen also discusses the historiographical controversy: “In his misguided quest for glory, Magellan had squandered lives and the resources of the armada.” Pigafetta’s eulogy is quoted: “Among the other virtues that he possessed, he was more constant than anyone else in the greatest adversity. He endured hunger better than all the others.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The Sebu Massacre: 27 Killed

After Magellan’s death, the expedition elected Edward Barbosa (his brother-in-law) as commander. Barbosa was invited to a feast by the king of Sebu, who had reverted to his old allegiances; Barbosa, Serrano, and most of the officers were massacred. The remaining men abandoned the Conception, burning her, and sailed on with the Trinidad and the Victoria. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen’s account is far more detailed. After Magellan’s death, Enrique (Magellan’s slave and interpreter) was told by Duarte Barbosa that he would remain a slave of Beatriz Barbosa. Enraged, Enrique “sought out Humabon, ‘the Christian King,’ to scheme against the armada,” revealing that the Europeans were about to depart and urging Humabon to attack. On Wednesday, May 1, Humabon invited the armada’s leaders to a feast. Around thirty men, most of them officers, accepted. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

“As the banquet was about to end, some armed people emerged from the palm grove and attacked the invitees, killing twenty-seven of them.” The victims included Duarte Barbosa (commander for just three days), Juan Serrano, Andres de San Martin (the astrologer), Father Valderrama, and numerous other officers and sailors. Juan Serrano was brought to the shore, weeping, pleading for rescue, but the ships weighed anchor and abandoned him. “Only 115 men remained of the 260 who had left Spain.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Aftermath: The Trinidad’s Fate and the Trial of Survivors

After further adventures at Borneo, the fleet finally reached the Molucca (Spice) Islands. There the Trinidad was found too leaky to continue and was left behind (later captured by the Portuguese). The Victoria, under Juan Sebastian Elcano, sailed west across the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope, and after great hardship (only 18 of the original 250 men survived) reached San Lucar, Spain, on September 6, 1522 — the first ship to circumnavigate the globe. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Bergreen traces the Trinidad’s tragic fate in detail. Under Gonzalo Gomez de Espinosa, the Trinidad attempted to sail back to Panama but was caught by contrary winds, storms, and scurvy. After seven months at sea and thirty deaths, Espinosa retreated to Tidore, where a Portuguese fleet under Antonio de Brito had arrived. The Portuguese seized the ship, stripped her, and a storm smashed her remains into driftwood. “The flagship of the Armada de Molucca ended up as driftwood.” Most of her crew were imprisoned; some were forced to work on a Portuguese fort built from the Trinidad’s own timbers. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The return of the Victoria triggered a fierce legal and political battle. The San Antonio’s mutineers had already returned to Seville in May 1521 and given depositions accusing Magellan of brutality, torture, and disloyalty to Spain. Fifty-three of the fifty-five crew members gave testimony. In their account, Magellan was “a Portuguese tyrant… a cunning agent of his native land.” Archbishop Fonseca, Cartagena’s father, responded to the depositions with shock and dismay at Magellan’s treatment of Spanish officers. The mutineers went free; Gomes even received a royal appointment to another expedition. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Magellan’s wife, Beatriz, was placed under house arrest with her young son while the inquiry continued, “not knowing that her husband had died only weeks before.” His father-in-law, Diogo Barbosa, wrote a long defense of Magellan to King Charles, arguing that Magellan “had to take great care so that it would be to your advantage… when the men he brought with him mutinied with three of the principal ships, he did not punish them severely when he could have.” Alvaro de Mesquita, who had been tortured by the mutineers, languished in jail for a year despite his innocence. Ruy Faleiro, the brilliant cosmologist who had co-created the expedition, was imprisoned in Portugal, suffered a breakdown, and later returned to Seville destitute. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

When the Victoria returned, Elcano faced an inquiry in Valladolid in October 1522. He condemned Magellan to exculpate himself, claiming Magellan had defied the king’s orders and “abandoned the fleet to its fate.” King Charles waived duties on the spices, awarded Elcano a knighthood, a coat of arms depicting a globe with the legend “Primus circumdedesti me” (Thou first circled me), and an annual pension. “Meanwhile, the coat of arms for the Magellan family remained defaced and dishonored.” Mesquita was finally freed in the general amnesty and fled to Portugal. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Legacy for Cartography and Empire

Bergreen synthesizes the expedition’s enduring significance. The circumnavigation “forever altered the Western world’s ideas about cosmology… as well as geography. It demonstrated, among other things, that the earth was round, that the Americas were not part of India but were actually a separate continent, and that oceans covered most of the earth’s surface. The voyage conclusively demonstrated that the earth is, after all, one world.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

In cartography, Pigafetta’s observations of two dwarf galaxies — now known as the Magellanic Clouds — marked a significant astronomical discovery. Oronce Fine’s 1531 map placed the Strait of Magellan accurately in South America, naming the Pacific “Magellanicum.” Gerardus Mercator canonized the strait on his famous globe in 1536. The name “Magellan” ultimately designated only the strait, not the territories he dreamed of bequeathing to his heirs. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The voyage established that the Pacific was not a small gulf but an ocean covering one-third of the earth’s surface. It added seven thousand miles to the globe’s circumference. The international date line, necessitated by the circumnavigation, was another enduring legacy — when the Victoria reached the Cape Verde Islands, the crew discovered they had lost a day, an error that “took the first circumnavigation to demonstrate the need for a twenty-four hour gain.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Politically, the voyage triggered a race to claim the Spice Islands. King Charles dispatched multiple follow-up expeditions, all ending in disaster (the second Armada de Molucca under Loaysa and Elcano lost all but one ship, with only 8 of 450 men surviving). Finally, in 1529, facing bankruptcy, Charles signed the Treaty of Saragossa, pledging the Moluccas to Portugal. Not until 1580 did Francis Drake complete the second circumnavigation, relying on knowledge “so painfully and heroically acquired by the Captain General and his crew.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

“Worthier, indeed, are our sailors of eternal fame than the Argonauts who sailed with Jason,” wrote Peter Martyr, Magellan’s contemporary. “And much more worthy was their ship of being placed along the stars than that old Argo; for they only sailed from Greece through Pontus, but ours through the whole west and southern hemisphere, penetrating into the east, and again returned to the west.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Key Figures in Magellan’s Story

  • King Manuel I of Portugal — Rejected Magellan’s services, driving him to Spain; turned his back when Magellan knelt to kiss his hands
  • King Charles I of Spain (later Emperor Charles V) — Sponsored Magellan’s expedition; never mourned Magellan’s death
  • Ruy Faleiro — Portuguese astrologer, mathematician, and cosmologist; co-created the expedition but was removed due to mental instability; later imprisoned in Portugal
  • Juan Rodriguez de Fonseca — Bishop of Burgos, head of Casa de Contratacion; Cartagena’s father; worked to undermine Magellan’s authority
  • Diego Barbosa — Magellan’s father-in-law, Knight Commander of Order of Santiago; defended Magellan after his death
  • Beatriz Barbosa — Magellan’s wife, married 1517–1518; placed under house arrest after the San Antonio returned
  • Juan de Cartagena — Fonseca’s illegitimate son; Spanish captain of the San Antonio; led the mutiny at St. Julian; marooned on Patagonian coast
  • Luis de Mendoza — Spanish captain of the Victoria; mutineer killed by Espinosa on his own deck
  • Gaspar de Quesada — Spanish captain of the Conception; mutineer; beheaded, drawn and quartered
  • Juan Serrano — Faithful captain of the Santiago; later killed in the Sebu massacre
  • Duarte Barbosa — Magellan’s brother-in-law; succeeded him as commander for three days; killed in the Sebu massacre
  • Juan Sebastian Elcano — Basque master of the Conception; completed circumnavigation in command of Victoria; condemned Magellan to exculpate himself at the Valladolid inquiry; died on a follow-up expedition in 1526
  • Lapu-Lapu (Cilapulapu) — Chieftain of Mactan who led the forces that killed Magellan; now a Filipino national hero
  • Antonio Pigafetta — Italian nobleman and chronicler whose journal preserved the detailed account of the voyage; his diary is the most important surviving document of the expedition
  • Esteban Gomes — Portuguese pilot of the San Antonio; led the desertion at the strait; returned to Spain and gave damning testimony against Magellan
  • Humabon — King of Cebu; initially allied with Magellan; after Magellan’s death, massacred 27 Spaniards at a feast
  • Enrique of Malacca — Magellan’s slave and interpreter; after Magellan’s death was told he would remain a slave; betrayed the Europeans to Humabon, triggering the Sebu massacre
