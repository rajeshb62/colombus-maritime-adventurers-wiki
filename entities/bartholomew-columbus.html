Bartholomew Columbus

Bartholomew Columbus (c. 1461–1514) was a Genoese navigator, cartographer, and colonial administrator who served as adelantado (lieutenant-governor) of Hispaniola under his older brother Christopher Columbus. A loyal and capable deputy, he governed the colony during the Admiral’s absences, led military campaigns against the native caciques Caonabo and Guarionex, and accompanied his brother on the fourth voyage. His strict and severe administration provoked the rebellion of Francisco Roldan, and he was arrested alongside Christopher and Diego Columbus by Francisco de Bobadilla in 1500 and returned to Spain in chains.

Early Career in Portugal

Bartholomew Columbus was the second of the four children of Domenico Colombo, a wool-comber of Genoa. He was the constant companion of his elder brother Christopher in youth, and like him, took early to the sea.^[source: irving_columbus_full.txt]

When Christopher Columbus left Portugal for Spain around 1485, he entrusted Bartholomew with a crucial mission: to travel to England and propose the westward project to King Henry VII of England. However, according to accounts by both Fernando Columbus and Bartolome de las Casas, Bartholomew did not proceed directly to England. Las Casas found a memorandum in Bartholomew’s handwriting suggesting that he first accompanied Bartholomew Diaz in 1486 on a Portuguese voyage along the coast of Africa, serving the king of Portugal. This expedition rounded the southern tip of Africa and discovered the Cape of Good Hope, returning to Lisbon in December 1487. The memorandum, written in a barbarous mixture of Latin and Spanish in the margin of a treatise on the form of the globe belonging to Christopher Columbus, recorded the voyage league by league and bore the note: “in all which, adds the writer, I was present.”^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

Mission to England

After the African voyage, Bartholomew Columbus proceeded to England to present his brother’s plan to King Henry VII. On his way, he was captured and plundered by a corsair and reduced to such poverty that he had to struggle for a mere subsistence for a long time by making sea charts, so that several years elapsed before he could make his application to the English monarch.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

When the proposition was eventually made to Henry VII, it met with a more ready attention than from any other sovereign. An agreement was actually concluded with Bartholomew for the prosecution of the enterprise, and he departed for Spain to find his brother. Reaching Paris, he first received the joyful intelligence that the discovery had already been made — Christopher Columbus had returned to Spain in triumph. King Charles VIII of France, understanding Bartholomew was low in purse, furnished him with one hundred crowns to defray the expenses of his journey to Spain. He reached Seville just as his brother had departed on the second voyage. Bartholomew immediately repaired to court at Valladolid, taking with him his two nephews, Diego and Fernando. He was received with distinguished favour by Ferdinand and Isabella, who found him an able and accomplished navigator and gave him the command of three ships freighted with supplies for the colony, sending him to aid his brother.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

Arrival in Hispaniola (1494)

Bartholomew Columbus reached the settlement of Isabella on the island of Hispaniola just after his brother’s departure for the coast of Cuba. The colony was suffering from famine and disease, and the admiral’s return in September 1494 brought a joyful reunion. Irving records that Columbus found at his bedside “his brother Bartholomew, the companion of his youth, his confidential coadjutor, and in a manner his second self, from whom he had been separated for several years.”^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

Character and Appointment as Adelantado

The character of Bartholomew Columbus is preserved by Las Casas from personal observation. He was:

  • Prompt, active, decided, and fearless — whatever he determined, he carried into instant execution, without regard to difficulty or danger.
  • Physically commanding — tall, muscular, vigorous, with an air of great authority but somewhat stern, lacking the sweetness and benignity that tempered the Admiral’s authoritative demeanour.
  • Stern in manner — there was a certain asperity in his temper and a dryness and abruptness in his manners that made him many enemies, yet he was of generous disposition, free from arrogance or malevolence, and as placable as he was brave.
  • A thorough seaman — understanding both the theory and practice of navigation, having been formed largely under the eye of the Admiral, and being little inferior to him in science. He was superior to the Admiral in the exercise of the pen, according to Las Casas, who had letters and manuscripts of both in his possession. He was acquainted with Latin though not highly educated, his knowledge being chiefly derived from varied experience and attentive observation.
  • Pragmatic and worldly — equally vigorous and penetrating in intellect with the Admiral but less enthusiastic in spirit and soaring in imagination, with less simplicity of heart. He surpassed Christopher in the subtle and adroit management of business, was more attentive to his interests, and had more of that worldly wisdom important in the ordinary concerns of life.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

Anxious to relieve himself from the pressure of public business during his present malady, Columbus immediately invested his brother Bartholomew with the title and authority of Adelantado, an office equivalent to lieutenant-governor. Columbus considered himself entitled to do so from the articles of his arrangement with the sovereigns, but King Ferdinand later looked upon this as an undue assumption of power, a matter that gave great offence to that jealous monarch.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. I]

Campaigns against Caonabo

During Columbus’s absence exploring Cuba and Jamaica in 1494, the colony had fallen into disorder. The Carib cacique Caonabo, the most fierce and puissant of the native chieftains and the inveterate enemy of the white men, attacked the fortress of Santo Tomas. Alonso de Ojeda defended the post with a small force and subsequently lured Caonabo into captivity using a famous ruse involving polished manacles. Caonabo was brought to Isabella in chains.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. III–IV]

After Caonabo’s capture, Columbus marched into the Vega Real with 200 infantry, 20 cavalry, and dogs, routing the native forces. He imposed a system of tribute on the native population — every Indian over fourteen had to pay a hawk’s bell full of gold every three months — a system that proved unworkable and generated deep resentment.^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book VIII Ch. VI–VII]

Administration and Campaigns against Guarionex

When Columbus returned to Spain in April 1496, he left Bartholomew as adelantado with full governing authority over the colony. Under Bartholomew’s administration, Isabella was abandoned, and the settlement was moved to the southern coast at the mouth of the Ozema River, where the town of Santo Domingo was founded — the first permanent European settlement in the Americas.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book IX Ch. I]

Bartholomew established a chain of fortified military posts across the island. The cacique Guarionex of the Royal Vega, who had initially been more tractable than Caonabo, grew restive under the Spanish tribute demands and the heavy hand of Spanish rule. He rose in rebellion in 1497, leading a major insurrection.

The Adelantado acted swiftly. He marched against Guarionex and defeated him in the Vega, but the cacique escaped and fled to the mountains of Ciguay. Bartholomew pursued him through forests and mountains in a gruelling campaign, eventually capturing Guarionex and ending organized native resistance in the northern part of the island.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book XI Ch. II, VI–VII]

Expedition to Xaragua

After the revolt of Guarionex was suppressed, Bartholomew turned his attention to the western province of Xaragua, the most populous and extensive domain on the island, ruled by the female cacique Anacaona. Her brother, the sovereign Behechio, had recently died. The province was wealthy and described by Irving as having inhabitants “finely formed, with a nobler air, a more agreeable elocution, and more soft and graceful manners than the natives of the other parts of the island.”^[source: irving_columbus_carvill_v2.txt, Book XI Ch. III]

The Adelantado led an expedition into Xaragua in 1498, demonstrating Spanish military power while negotiating for the submission of the province. He established contact with Anacaona and her nobles, demanding tribute in recognition of Spanish sovereignty. The negotiations were outwardly peaceful but tense, and Bartholomew later returned to collect tribute from the province.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book XI Ch. I, III]

The Roldan Rebellion

Upon Christopher Columbus’s return from the third voyage in August 1498, he found the colony in chaos. The Adelantado’s strict governance had created widespread discontent among the Spanish colonists. Francisco Roldan, the alcalde mayor (chief magistrate), organized a conspiracy in 1497 against the Columbus brothers, accusing them of tyranny, misgovernment, and favouring foreigners over native Castilians.

Roldan withdrew to Xaragua and established a rival government, allying himself with Anacaona and gathering malcontents. The Adelantado attempted to negotiate with Roldan but the rebel leader refused to submit. Columbus, weakened by illness and with no military option available, was forced into humiliating concessions — amnesty for Roldan and his followers, reinstatement of Roldan as alcalde mayor, and grants of land and native labour (encomiendas).^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book XI Ch. IV–V; irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. I–V]

Fresh conspiracies followed. Hernando de Guevara and Adrian de Moxica hatched a new plot against the Adelantado. The conspiracy was discovered and suppressed: Moxica was captured and executed, and Guevara was imprisoned. But the persistence of rebellion demonstrated that the Columbus brothers’ authority had been fatally undermined.^[source: irving-columbus-vol3.md, Book XII Ch. VIII]

Arrest and Return to Spain in Chains

The rebels who returned to Spain carried accounts that painted Columbus and his brothers as arrogant and oppressive foreigners unfit to govern. Their complaints found a receptive audience at court, where Bishop Juan Rodriguez de Fonseca and other enemies of Columbus had long been working against the Admiral. In 1500, King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella dispatched Francisco de Bobadilla as royal commissioner with full powers to investigate Columbus’s conduct.

Bobadilla arrived at San Domingo and, without any prior investigation, publicly read his commission, seized the fortress, took command of the colony, and ordered the seizure of the Columbus brothers’ property. He invited depositions from the very men who had rebelled under Roldan. Columbus was summoned from the fortress of Conception and, upon his return to San Domingo, was arrested and placed in chains.

Bartholomew and his younger brother Diego Columbus were arrested alongside the Admiral. Bobadilla clapped them all in irons. They were shipped to Spain as prisoners. The governor of the caravel refused to remove the chains despite Christopher’s age and infirmity, and Columbus famously refused to have his irons removed during the voyage, maintaining a dignified composure throughout the passage.^[source: irving-columbus-v3.md, Book XIII Ch. I–IV]

The arrival of the Columbus brothers in chains at Cadiz created a public outcry. Queen Isabella was outraged at their treatment. They were ordered released and summoned to court at Granada, where they were received with kindness. However, Columbus was never restored to his governorship, and Bartholomew’s authority in Hispaniola was permanently ended.^[source: irving-columbus-v3.md, Book XIV Ch. I]

Fourth Voyage

Bartholomew accompanied his brother on the fourth and final voyage (1502–1504). He was present during the exploration of the coasts of Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama (Veragua). When the two remaining ships were worm-riddled and beached on Jamaica, he played a critical role:

  • He led an inland expedition in Veragua, making contact with the native chief Quibian and learning of nearby gold mines.^[source: irving-columbus-v3.md, Book XV Ch. VI]
  • He led a preemptive expedition to surprise and capture Quibian when the natives conspired to attack the Spanish settlement on the River Belen.^[source: irving-columbus-v3.md, Book XV Ch. VII]
  • When the mutiny of Francisco de Porras broke out on Jamaica, Bartholomew led the loyal Spaniards against the mutineers in a pitched battle on the beach. Porras was defeated and captured, and the mutineers were pardoned by Columbus, restoring order among the castaways.^[source: irving-columbus-v3.md, Book XVI Ch. VI]

After the fourth voyage, Bartholomew Columbus returned to Spain with his brother. He died around 1514, predeceasing Christopher by several years (Christopher died in 1506). His later years were spent in relative obscurity, overshadowed by the eclipse of his brother’s fortunes.
