Key Magellan Crew — Elcano, Pigafetta, Cartagena

Three figures whose roles in the first circumnavigation were critical: Juan Sebastian Elcano, the Basque mariner who completed the voyage after Magellan’s death; Antonio Pigafetta, the Venetian scholar whose journal preserved the only surviving eyewitness account; and Juan de Cartagena, the Spanish captain who led the mutiny at Port St. Julian. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Juan Sebastian Elcano (c. 1476–1526)

Juan Sebastian Elcano (also spelled del Cano; c. 1476–1526) was a Basque mariner from Getaria, in the province of Guipuzcoa, northern Spain. He was the commander who brought the Victoria home to Spain, completing the first circumnavigation of the globe after Ferdinand Magellan’s death at Mactan. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Role in the Expedition

Elcano was not among the original captains of the Armada de Molucca. He began the voyage as a subordinate officer — likely the master or pilot of one of the ships — and survived the mutiny at Port St. Julian, the Pacific crossing, Magellan’s death at Mactan, and the subsequent massacre of Barbosa and Serrano at Sebu. After the massacre, the depleted fleet elected new commanders: Carvalho became admiral, Espinosa captain of the Trinidad, and Elcano was given command of the Victoria. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XIV]

Bergreen’s Account — Basque Origins

Bergreen provides extensive detail on Elcano’s Basque background. “To be a Basque meant, and still means, to be a historical anomaly. The Basques are the oldest ethnic group of Europe, a breed apart ever since Paleolithic times. In their province in northern Spain, next to the French border, the Basques speak a distinct language, actually, eight dialects of a distinct language. No direct link between the Basque tongue and another language has been identified.” The sea loomed large in Basque life; Basque men “were born facing the sea, they lived by the sea, they died at sea.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Juan Sebastián Elcano was born in 1487 in the Basque province of Guipúzcoa. His name derives from Elk-ano, a Basque word for a district of fields. Of his eight siblings, at least two brothers became mariners, and one sister married a pilot. At twenty, Elcano found work ferrying Spanish soldiers in ships; two years later, he served aboard an expeditionary ship to Africa. He later took refuge in Seville, where he attended the Casa de Contratación’s School of Navigation, receiving formal training as a pilot — probably from Amerigo Vespucci, who served as head of its board of examiners. Under Vespucci’s supervision, Elcano learned his navigation, was “examined and approved as pilot, his path to fame secured.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Just before the fleet departed from Seville, Elcano was called to testify before a formal board of inquiry, where he testified that Magellan was a “discreet and virtuous man and careful of his honor.” In his entire chronicle of the voyage, Pigafetta did not mention Elcano’s name even once. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Command of the Victoria

When the fleet finally reached the Molucca (Spice) Islands, the Trinidad was found to be too leaky for the long voyage home. Espinosa decided to remain at Tidor with the Trinidad, attempting to sail east to Panama, and entrusted the Victoria to Elcano for the westward return. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVI–XVII]

Elcano’s voyage on the Victoria was a desperate ordeal. He sailed from Tidor through the Malay Archipelago, stopping at Timor, and then took the bold decision to risk the Cape of Good Hope route — fully aware that the waters around the Cape were claimed by Portugal and patrolled by Portuguese ships. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVII]

The crossing of the Indian Ocean was brutal. Provisions ran desperately short; scurvy and starvation ravaged the crew. Twenty-one men died before the ship could reach a friendly port. The Victoria rounded the Cape of Good Hope with difficulty, fighting contrary winds. Elcano refused to stop at Portuguese Mozambique for fear of capture. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

A brief stop at the Cape Verde Islands proved costly: the Portuguese seized 13 men from shore parties, but Elcano, realizing the danger, quickly weighed anchor and fled, leaving the captured men behind. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

Return to Spain and Trial Testimony

On September 6, 1522 — nearly three years after the fleet’s departure from San Lucar — the Victoria, battered, leaking, and crewed by only 18 emaciated survivors of the original 250 men, arrived at San Lucar de Barrameda near Seville. The ship then sailed up the Guadalquivir to Seville, where the crew walked in barefoot procession to the church carrying tapers in thanksgiving. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

Elcano was received with great honor by King Charles I of Spain. He was granted a coat of arms depicting a globe with the motto Primus circumdedisti me (“You first circumnavigated me”) and awarded a pension. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

Bergreen recounts that King Charles summoned Elcano and two men of his choosing to visit the royal residence at Valladolid to provide a full account of their exploits. Elcano selected the pilot Albo and the barber-medic Bustamente, pointedly excluding Pigafetta, whom he knew to be a Magellan loyalist. On October 18, 1522, the three men faced an inquiry conducted by Valladolid’s mayor, acting on orders from the king himself. The proceeding consisted of thirteen questions concentrated on two themes: the mutiny and the commercial aspects of the voyage. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Elcano condemned Magellan to exculpate himself, claiming Magellan had defied the king’s orders and “abandoned the fleet to its fate.” He was asked why he had failed to keep accounts; he tried to shift the blame to Magellan, asserting that as long as Magellan was alive he had written nothing “because he dared not to do so.” Concerning the death of Magellan, Elcano held the Mactanese islanders completely responsible, suggesting Magellan had goaded them by burning their hamlet. His testimony was “sufficiently dexterous to exculpate himself from royal disfavor or worse.” King Charles waived the royal duties on the spices, granted Elcano a knighthood, a coat of arms with the legend Primus circumdedesti me (“Thou first circled me”), and an annual pension of five hundred ducats. Of equal importance, Elcano received a royal pardon for his role in the failed mutiny against Magellan’s command — and insisted on having the document published. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Later Life and Death: The Loaysa Expedition

Elcano did not long enjoy his fame. In 1525, King Charles commissioned Francisco García Jofre de Loaysa to lead a second Armada de Molucca to establish a Spanish trading post in the Spice Islands. Elcano, honored as the first circumnavigator, received an appointment as second-in-command. The expedition left Seville with five ships and 450 men — hope was high that retracing Magellan’s route would make the voyage far safer. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Instead, the second armada met with an even harsher fate than the first. Without Magellan’s navigational genius, Elcano “made one navigational error after another,” and storms reduced the fleet from five ships to just two. In the Pacific, scurvy broke out among officers and crew — this time, no one had quince preserves to protect them. Loaysa died, leaving behind an envelope prepared by King Charles naming a successor. When the seal was broken, the letter appointed Elcano as admiral. “The Basque mariner had finally reached the summit of ambition, but the time remaining to him proved to be cruelly brief, because he was already suffering from scurvy.”^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Retreating to his small cabin, Elcano drew up his will: it carefully inventoried all his worldly possessions down to the last article of clothing and ream of paper, listed his charitable bequests, specified gifts to his two mistresses, and requested burial in his hometown of Guetaria. Seven others — each a Basque — witnessed the document. Five days after assuming command, on August 4, 1526, Juan Sebastián Elcano died at sea. His body was committed to the deep in the Pacific Ocean. Of the 450 men who set sail, only 8 lived to see Spain again — a death rate even worse than Magellan’s crew had suffered. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Antonio Pigafetta (c. 1491–c. 1531)

Antonio Pigafetta (c. 1491–c. 1531) was a Venetian nobleman and scholar who joined Magellan’s expedition as a supernumerary passenger and amateur chronicler. His detailed journal, Relazione del primo viaggio intorno al mondo (Report on the First Voyage Around the World), is the most complete surviving eyewitness account of the circumnavigation. Without Pigafetta’s meticulous observations, much of what is known about the voyage — and especially about the peoples, languages, and natural history encountered — would have been lost. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Background and Motivation (Pigafetta’s Own Account)

Pigafetta introduces himself in the dedicatory letter as “Antonio Pigafetta, patrician of Vicenza and Knight of Rhodes.” He was born into a wealthy Venetian family from Vicenza and received a humanist education typical of the Italian Renaissance. He studied navigation, astronomy, geography, and the natural sciences. His appetite for exploration was whetted by accounts of earlier voyages, including those of Columbus and Vasco da Gama. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s own words explain his motivation: “finding myself, in the year of the nativity of Our Saviour 1519 in Spain, in the court of the most serene King of the Romans, with the reverend Monsignor, Francesco Chiericati… and having obtained much information from many books that I had read… I determined, with the good grace of His Caesarean Majesty, and of his Lordship abovesaid, to experience myself and to see those things that might satisfy me somewhat, and that might grant me some renown with posterity.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

He arrived in Spain around 1518 in the retinue of the papal nuncio, Francesco Chieregati. Hearing of Magellan’s impending expedition, he applied to join as a passenger and chronicler. He describes himself as a man of learning who had “read many books” and had a “craving for experience and glory.” He helped gather navigational instruments and ingratiated himself into Magellan’s trust. Accepting a salary of just 1,000 maravedis, he joined as a sobrasaliente (supernumerary) — an extra man not part of the regular crew. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt] ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Bergreen’s Account — Venetian Scholar and Survivor

Bergreen adds significant depth to Pigafetta’s background. Among those in attendance at the fleet’s departure mass at Santa María de la Victoria in Triana was “a Venetian scholar named Antonio Pigafetta who had spent long years in the service of Andrea Chiericati, an emissary of Pope Leo X.” When the pope appointed Chiericati ambassador to King Charles, Pigafetta — about thirty years old at the time — followed the diplomat to Spain. By his own description, he was a man of learning (he boasted of having “read many books”) and religious conviction, but also had a thirst for adventure — “a craving for experience and glory.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Learning of Magellan’s expedition, he excused himself from the diplomatic circles to seek out the renowned navigator in Seville in May 1519. He helped gather navigational instruments and ingratiated himself into Magellan’s trust. “Pigafetta quickly came to idolize the Captain General, despite their differing nationalities, and was awestricken by the ambitiousness and danger of the mission.” He lacked experience at sea but had funds and impeccable papal credentials. Accepting a salary of just 1,000 maravedís, he joined as a sobrasaliente (supernumerary), receiving four months’ modest pay in advance. Magellan assigned him to keep “a more personal, anecdotal, and free-flowing account” in the tradition of Marco Polo and Ludovico di Varthema. “Making no secret of his ambition to take his place in letters beside them, Pigafetta readily accepted the assignment.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Bergreen notes that Pigafetta’s account “is also a humane and compassionate record written in a distinctive voice, naïve yet cultivated, pious yet bawdy. Of the handful of genuine chronicles of foreign lands available at the time, only Pigafetta’s preserved moments of self-deprecation and humor; only his betrayed the realistic fears, joys, and ambivalence felt by the crew. His narrative anticipates a modern sensibility, in which self-doubt and revelation play roles.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

After Magellan’s death and the massacre at Cebu, Pigafetta found himself in a uniquely vulnerable position. As Bergreen relates, “He had always identified himself as a Magellan loyalist, but the Captain General’s inner circle — his slave, Enrique; his illegitimate son, Cristóvão Rebêlo; his cousin, Álvaro de Mesquita; and his brother-in-law, Duarte Barbosa — had all perished or disappeared. Only Pigafetta survived.” He continued as chief chronicler and interpreter because he alone had troubled to compile a vocabulary of local Malay dialects. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Upon his return to Spain, Pigafetta petitioned the Doge of Venice for a twenty-year exclusive printing privilege for his “relation” of the voyage, writing: “I, Antonio Pigafetta, Venetian knight of Jerusalem, who, desiring to see the world, have sailed… with the caravels of his Caesarean Majesty… I circumnavigated the whole world, and since it is a feat that no man had accomplished, I have composed a short narration of the entire voyage, which I desire to have printed.” His request was granted. The first copies were lavish handwritten manuscripts illustrated with maps of his own devising. “By general agreement, the most authoritative of the four surviving versions is the one in Italian, the so-called Ambrosian manuscript, now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Survivors and Manuscript Legacy

Pigafetta was among the 18 survivors who returned to Spain on the Victoria. In his own account, after the Victoria departed Tidore on December 21, 1521, “we were forty-seven men and thirteen Indians.” By the time the ship reached Sanlucar de Barrameda on September 6, 1522, only 18 remained. Pigafetta disembarked on September 8, 1522, and travelled to the imperial court at Valladolid, where he presented to Charles V “a book, written by my hand, concerning all the matters that had occurred from day to day during the voyage.” He then went to Portugal, and finally to France, where he presented a copy to the mother of the French king. He returned to Italy and presented his manuscript to the Grand Master of the Knights of Rhodes, Philipe Villiers de l’Isle-Adam. The most authoritative of the four surviving manuscript versions is the Ambrosian manuscript in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan; others are in Paris (Bibliotheque Nationale) and in a Yale University library collection. None of the manuscripts are in Pigafetta’s own hand — they are scribal copies. The journal was first published in French in 1525 and in Italian in 1536 by Gian Battista Ramusio. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Upon his return to Spain, Pigafetta petitioned the Doge of Venice for a twenty-year exclusive printing privilege for his “relation” of the voyage, writing: “I, Antonio Pigafetta, Venetian knight of Jerusalem, who, desiring to see the world, have sailed… with the caravels of his Caesarean Majesty… I circumnavigated the whole world, and since it is a feat that no man had accomplished, I have composed a short narration of the entire voyage, which I desire to have printed.” His request was granted. The first copies were lavish handwritten manuscripts illustrated with maps of his own devising. “By general agreement, the most authoritative of the four surviving versions is the one in Italian, the so-called Ambrosian manuscript, now in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in Milan.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Observations of Patagonian Giants

Pigafetta’s journal provides the most detailed European account of the Patagonian giants encountered at Port St. Julian (Patagonia, southern Argentina) in 1520. He described the natives as astonishingly tall — so tall that the Spaniards’ heads only reached their waists — and powerfully built. He recorded their clothing (animal skins), their weapons (bows and arrows), their dwellings (huts of branches and hides), and their customs. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VII]

Among the details Pigafetta recorded: that the giants painted their faces and bodies, that they used a peculiar emetic ritual (pushing an arrow down the throat to induce vomiting), that they bled themselves for headaches by cutting their skin, that they ate raw meat and rats, and that they called upon a god named Setebos. Pigafetta witnessed the capture of two young giants whom Magellan intended to take back to Spain as interpreters; he recorded their rage when put in leg-irons and their desperate cries to Setebos. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VII]

Observations of the Philippines and Spice Islands

Throughout the voyage’s final phase, Pigafetta kept careful notes on the islands, peoples, languages, flora, and fauna encountered. His journal includes:

  • Philippine societies: Detailed accounts of the social hierarchies, trade practices, religious beliefs, and material culture of the peoples of Mazzava, Sebu (Cebu), and other islands. He recorded the blood-brotherhood ceremony between Magellan and the king of Mazzava, the baptism of the king and queen of Sebu, and the diplomatic negotiations with local rulers. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. X–XII]

  • The Battle of Mactan: Pigafetta was present at the battle where Magellan was killed and provided a vivid, moment-by-moment account of the fighting, Magellan’s wounds (a lance to the face, a javelin to the right arm, a scimitar blow to the left leg that felled him, and the final swarm of spears and stones), and the Spaniards’ retreat under fire. He named Cilapulapu (Lapu-Lapu) as the chieftain who led the defense. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XIII]

  • The Moluccas (Spice Islands): Pigafetta described the clove trees, the trade in nutmeg and mace, the palaces of the sultans of Tidor and Ternate, and the complex political rivalries among the island kingdoms. He recorded vocabulary lists of local languages. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVI]

Survival and Return

Pigafetta was among the 18 survivors who returned to Spain on the Victoria in September 1522. Upon his return, he presented a manuscript of his journal to King Charles I of Spain and later returned to Italy, where he prepared a more polished version for publication. The journal was first published in French (1525) and later in Italian and other languages, becoming one of the most important travel narratives of the Age of Discovery. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

Pigafetta’s Linguistic and Cartographic Legacy

Beyond his narrative, Pigafetta made two contributions of enduring scholarly importance: his linguistic work and his cartography.

The Four Word Lists: Pigafetta compiled four extensive vocabularies of the languages encountered — the earliest recorded specimens of each. The lists grew progressively longer as his method improved: a short list of 8 Tupi-Guarani words from Brazil; approximately 90 words of Tehuelche (Patagonian giants); approximately 160 words of Philippine languages (what he called “the language of the heathen” to distinguish it from Malay); and a remarkable 426-450 word vocabulary of Malay. The Malay vocabulary is one of the oldest written specimens of the Malay language, with the earliest surviving manuscripts dated to around 1500–1550. The Cachey edition notes that Pigafetta’s “record of indigenous toponyms, like his list of 450 words of Malay, have been described by competent scholars as extremely accurate.” The Philippine and Malay word lists “cumulatively constitute a kind of implicit anthropological portrait of the East Indian societies encountered.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt, Cachey editorial notes]

The 23 Painted Maps: The earliest manuscripts of Pigafetta’s journal included 23 hand-painted maps of the regions visited — a cartographic record that was as important as the verbal one. The maps follow the narrative sequentially, with pairs of charts positioned at key transitions in the journey (the passage through the Strait, the Pacific crossing, the Moluccas). ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt, Cachey editorial notes]

Literary and Cultural Influence: Pigafetta’s account had a surprisingly long literary afterlife. His description of the Patagonian deity “Setebos” was picked up by Richard Eden’s 1555 English translation and later used by William Shakespeare in The Tempest (where Caliban’s god is named Setebos). Gabriel García Márquez, in his Nobel Prize acceptance speech, referenced Pigafetta’s account of the Patagonian giant who confronted a mirror and was terrified by his own reflection. The Cachey edition argues that Pigafetta’s narrative “represents a particularly mature expression of courtly Italian Renaissance travel writing,” positioning it within the lingua e letteratura cortigiana that flourished in the Italian High Renaissance. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt, Cachey editorial notes]

Juan de Cartagena (d. 1520/1521)

Juan de Cartagena (also spelled Cartagena; died 1520 or 1521) was a Spanish nobleman and captain who commanded the San Antonio, the largest vessel in the Armada de Molucca. He was the leader of the mutiny at Port St. Julian and, after Magellan’s suppression of the revolt, was marooned on the Patagonian coast. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Background and Appointment

Cartagena was a Spanish hidalgo who owed his appointment not to his seafaring experience but to his connections at court. He was a personal appointee of Bishop Fonseca, the president of the Council of India, who had opposed Magellan from the start. His rank as captain of the San Antonio and his role as the fleet’s veedor (inspector/overseer) gave him authority second only to Magellan himself. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV]

Bergreen’s Account — Fonseca’s Illegitimate Son

Bergreen reveals that the relationship between Cartagena and Archbishop Fonseca was far more intimate than mere patronage. “Cartagena was considered Fonseca’s nephew, but as everyone realized, that term was a euphemism: In reality, Cartagena was Fonseca’s illegitimate son.” He was not the only beneficiary of this brand of nepotism; the fleet’s accountant, Antonio de Coca, was also Fonseca’s protege. Bergreen describes the three captains Fonseca appointed — Cartagena, Quesada, and Mendoza — as all men who “despised and looked down on Magellan from the moment they came on board.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The appointment of Cartagena as Faleiro’s replacement under the title persona conjunta was a deliberate move by Fonseca to undermine Magellan’s authority. The exact meaning was ambiguous, but “at the minimum it meant that Magellan was supposed to consult with Cartagena in all matters. At the maximum, it meant that the two were cocommanders, with Cartagena, as inspector general, having a slight edge in his capacity as Magellan’s official supervisor.” Bergreen calls this arrangement “Fonseca’s revenge on Magellan” — the archbishop had “managed to stifle Magellan’s authority, and, potentially, his share of the proceeds of the expedition, by appointing his natural son and his close allies to virtually all the important positions in the armada.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

After Cartagena’s second mutiny attempt at Port St. Julian, Magellan refrained from executing him precisely because of his blood ties to Fonseca. “His blood ties to Archbishop Fonseca prevented Magellan from taking severe disciplinary action such as execution or torture.” Instead, Cartagena was marooned on the Patagonian coast along with a priest. Bergreen confirms that later, when the San Antonio mutineers returned to Spain, there was talk of sailing back to Port St. Julian to rescue Cartagena, “but in all likelihood the ship never attempted to rescue them.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Bergreen also notes that Fonseca, upon hearing the mutineers’ depositions, ordered the arrest of Magellan’s wife Beatriz and their young son, placing them under house arrest while the inquiry continued — “not knowing that her husband had died only weeks before, on April 27, in the battle of Mactan, followed by her brother, Duarte Barbosa, who died on May 1 in the massacre at Cebu.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

From the outset, Cartagena resented serving under a Portuguese admiral. He and the other Spanish captains — Luis de Mendoza (Victoria) and Gaspar de Quesada (Concepcion) — viewed Magellan as a foreign interloper and chafed at his command. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

The Mutiny at Port St. Julian

The expedition wintered at Port St. Julian (on the coast of present-day Argentina) from March to August 1520. The harsh conditions, dwindling provisions, and the uncertainty of finding a passage southward created a climate of fear and discontent among the crew. Cartagena exploited this discontent to organize a conspiracy. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

The mutineers’ plan was to seize the fleet and either depose or kill Magellan. On the night of the planned uprising, Cartagena and Quesada boarded the Concepcion and took control of that ship, winning its crew to their side. They then seized the San Antonio and the Victoria, leaving Magellan with only the Trinidad (his flagship) and the Santiago (commanded by the loyal Juan Serrano). ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Suppression of the Revolt

Magellan responded with decisive brutality. He sent a party of armed men in a launch to the Victoria under pretense of delivering a message; the constable, Fernandes, boarded the ship, approached Captain Mendoza, and stabbed him dead on his own deck. With the Victoria recaptured, Magellan then blockaded the two remaining mutinous ships until they surrendered. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Quesada was arrested and imprisoned. Cartagena was tried and sentenced to death. According to Towle’s account, Cartagena was executed by firing squad and his body quartered and displayed as a warning. However, other historical accounts — and the broader consensus among later historians — record that Cartagena was instead marooned on the desolate Patagonian coast along with a priest who had supported the mutiny, left to die of exposure and starvation. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Legacy

Cartagena’s mutiny was the most dangerous internal threat Magellan faced during the voyage. Had it succeeded, the expedition would likely have turned back, and the first circumnavigation would not have been completed. Its failure cemented Magellan’s authority for the remainder of the voyage, but it also deepened the rift between the Spanish and Portuguese officers — a rift that would widen further after Magellan’s death. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]
