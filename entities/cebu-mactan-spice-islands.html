Cebu, Mactan, and the Spice Islands (Moluccas)

Three key locations in the final phase of Magellan’s voyage: Cebu (Sebu), where Magellan forged an alliance and staged a mass baptism; Mactan (Matan), where he was killed in battle; and the Spice Islands / Moluccas (Tidor and Tarenate), the expedition’s ultimate destination, where the Victoria loaded cloves for the return voyage. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Pacific Crossing — Atolls and Guam

Before reaching the Philippines, the fleet endured the 99-day Pacific crossing from the Strait of Magellan. The ordeal is described in detail on the ferdinand-magellan page. Two barren landfalls punctuated the crossing. On January 24, 1521, the fleet sighted a small atoll — San Pablo (likely Puka-Puka in the Tuamotus) — but found neither fresh water nor anchorage; the water was so deep that “no bottom can be found.” On February 4, 1521, a second islet was sighted — most likely Caroline Island in Micronesia — which Albo’s log recorded as “an uninhabited island, where we caught many sharks, and therefore we gave it the name of Isla de los Tiburones” (Shark Island). Again, the fleet could not land. Desperate, Magellan flung his useless maps overboard, crying, “With the pardon of the cartographers, the Moluccas are not to be found in their appointed place!” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Guam — The Islands of Thieves

On March 6, 1521, the fleet discovered Guam, part of the Marianas archipelago. Hundreds of Chamorro islanders in fast outrigger canoes (described by pilot Albo as having “mat sails of a triangular shape” that allowed them to go in both directions “as they wished”) encircled the ships and swarmed aboard. The Chamorros — taller and stronger than the Europeans — stole everything they could touch: rigging, crockery, weapons, and iron. The crew, weakened from the crossing, begged Magellan to force them off. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

The encounter turned violent when the boatswain of the flagship slapped a Chamorro “for a small reason” and the Chamorro slapped him back. “Insulted, the boatswain stabbed him in the back with a machete.” The Chamorros hurled themselves overboard and attacked with sticks and arrows from their canoes. Magellan, seeing the numbers increasing, ordered his men to stop shooting. “With this, the Indians stopped, the fighting subsided, and they resumed selling food as before.” Magellan’s restraint restored trade: the fleet obtained coconuts and fish in exchange for glass beads. He named the islands Islas de los Ladrones — “Islands of Thieves.” ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

From Guam, the fleet sailed westward and sighted the Philippines on March 16, 1521.

CEBU (Sebu)

Cebu (rendered as Sebu in Towle’s text) is an island in the Philippine archipelago, described as “the most beautiful and fruitful of the vast labyrinth of islands which cluster in the Archipelago.” When Magellan arrived around April 1521, it was ruled by one of the most intelligent and powerful potentates in the Eastern seas. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Arrival at Sebu

After leaving the island of Mazzava (where Magellan had performed a blood-brotherhood ceremony with a local king), the fleet sailed westward toward Sebu. The voyage was pleasant, and Magellan conversed at length with the friendly king of Mazzava, who traveled with him, learning about the peoples and customs of the islands. The Spaniards observed exotic wildlife: birds “as large as eagles,” doves of various brilliant hues, parrots with gorgeous plumage, long-tailed blackbirds as large as hens, and enormous tortoises. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

On a Sunday at about noon, the fleet came in sight of Sebu. Skirting its shores, the Spaniards saw many closely-built, busy villages along the beach and nestled in picturesque valleys. They sailed into a pretty bay at the head of which stood the principal town. As the ships entered, Magellan ordered the standards run up, sails lowered, and cannon fired. The terrified natives huddled together and fled. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Alliance with King Humabon

Magellan sent a young Portuguese and his Malay interpreter ashore to seek the king. The town emptied in fright, but they overtook an old man who led them to the royal presence. The king (named Humabon in standard histories; unnamed in Towle’s account) was found seated on a wide mat in a low palace built in the form of a quadrangle, surrounded by courtiers with his dark-brown wives lying at his feet. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Malay interpreter knelt before the king, who lifted his hands heavenward in welcome. The king initially demanded tribute, citing that a ship from Siam had paid it only four days earlier. The interpreter replied proudly that Magellan would “not pay tribute to any sovereign in the world.” The king darkened with anger and half-rose, clutching his short sword. A Siamese merchant present intervened, warning the king that these were the same people who conquered Calicut, Malacca, and all the greater India. The interpreter added that Magellan’s sovereign was not only king of Spain but emperor of Christendom, who could “sweep you and your subjects off the face of the earth.” ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The king relented and asked to consult his advisers. The next day he received the messengers again, now described as a short, fat, jovial-looking man with a tattooed face, wearing a cloth about his waist and an embroidered silk turban, with gold rings in his ears studded with precious stones. He was eating tortoise eggs from china dishes and drinking palm wine through a cane tube. After questioning the envoys at length, he pricked his right arm, let a little blood fall on a fig-leaf, wrapped it up, and sent it to Magellan as a token of friendship. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Trade was established: the king provided a hut near the shore as a warehouse, and four Spaniards acted as salesmen, bartering iron, cloths, and trinkets for gold — which was found in large quantities in Sebu and the neighboring islands. The natives were peaceable, honest, and fair. They gave gold worth fifteen Spanish ducats for fourteen pounds of iron. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Mass Baptism (April 14, 1521) — Chapters XI-XII

Magellan, eager to plant Christianity, persuaded the king and his court to convert. Preparations were made on an elaborate scale: a high platform was erected in the center of the open space, decked with tapestry, carpets, and palm branches. The king of Sebu, his queen, and the king of Mazzava were to be baptized on Sunday, April 14, 1521. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

On the morning of April 14, forty armored men carrying the royal standard of Spain landed first, followed by a procession: Magellan at the front with his captains wearing velvet cloaks and plumed caps, then the priests, soldiers, and crews. The king stood ready in fantastic attire with freshly painted face, surrounded by courtiers. Magellan and the two kings ascended the scaffold and took their places in chairs of red and violet velvet. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Magellan asked the kings if they truly wished to become Christians, requiring them to burn all idols, set up the cross, and kneel before it daily. The kings agreed. The king of Sebu was baptized Charles (after King Charles of Spain); the king of Mazzava was baptized John; and the eldest Sebu prince was baptized Ferdinand (after Magellan). Fifty principal subjects were then baptized, and mass was performed. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

In the afternoon, the queen of Sebu was baptized as Joan (after King Charles’s mother). She was young and pretty, arrayed in a black and white robe, wearing a large hat of palm-leaves surmounted with a crown. The priest showed her an image of the Virgin and Child, and she begged to keep “the little wooden boy” in place of her idols. Her daughter was baptized Catherine; the queen of Mazzava was baptized Isabella. By nightfall, no less than eight hundred natives — including the royal family and court — had been baptized. Baptisms continued daily for more than a week. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

A cross was set up in the center of town. Magellan later had the king swear allegiance to Spain in a formal ceremony: Magellan drew his sword, held it aloft before an image of the Virgin, and called on the king to swear faithfulness. The king bowed and repeated the oath in his own tongue. Magellan presented him with a splendid velvet chair as a sign of sovereignty. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Magellan also intervened in native religious practices, demanding the destruction of idols. When the king’s nephew lay gravely ill, Magellan insisted he be baptized, promising he would recover. The prince recovered (which the Spaniards hailed as a miracle), and thereafter he himself burned the native temples and idols. Towle describes the idols as made of wood, curved in shape, hollowed out behind, with large painted faces, four large teeth like a wild boar’s, and legs and arms stretched out horizontally. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Spaniards witnessed several native ceremonies during their stay, including the “sacrifice of the swine” (a ritual pig-killing by dancing old women), and a Sebu funeral in which the corpse was laid in a chest, anointed with oil of camphor, and deposited in a tomb of wooden logs. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Description of the Cebu Audience

Pigafetta accompanied the interpreter and a companion to the court of Cebu and provided a vivid description of the king: “When we reached the city we found the king in his palace surrounded by many people, seated on a palm mat on the ground, with only a cotton cloth covering his private parts, and an embroidered scarf on his head, a necklace of great value hanging from his neck, and two large gold earrings fastened in his ears set round with precious gems. He was fat and short, and tattooed with fire in various designs; from another mat on the ground he was eating turtle eggs that were in two porcelain dishes, and he had four jars full of palm wine in front of him covered with sweet-smelling herbs and arranged with four small reeds in each jar, by means of which he drank.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

After the audience, Pigafetta was taken to the prince’s house: “where four young girls were playing [instruments]: one, on a drum like ours, but resting on the ground; the second was striking two suspended metal discs alternately with a stick made thick at the end with palm cloth wrapped around it; the third, one large metal disc in the same manner; and the last, two small metal discs held in her hand, by striking one against the other, which gave forth a sweet sound. They played so harmoniously that it appeared they had great knowledge of music.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

The Easter Mass and Cross Planting

Pigafetta’s account of the Easter Sunday mass at Mazzava (Limasawa) on March 31, 1521 is the sole eyewitness record. “Early on the morning of Sunday, the last of March, and Easter Day, the captain-general sent the priest with many men to prepare for saying mass, and the interpreter went to tell the king that we were not going to land in order to dine with him, but to say mass.” Magellan landed with about fifty men “without our body armour, but carrying our other arms, and dressed in our best clothes.” Before mass, he sprinkled the two kings with musk water. “We set up the cross there, for those people were heathens.” The cross stood as a sign of possession and evangelization. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

The Baptism Ceremony

Pigafetta records that on April 14, 1521, the king of Cebu was baptized as “Charles” (after the Spanish king) and the queen as “Joan.” The queen was “young and beautiful, and was entirely covered with a white and black cloth; her mouth and nails were very red, while on her head she wore a large hat of palm leaves in the manner of a parasol, with a crown about it of the same leaves, like the tiara of the pope.” She was so pleased with an image of the child Jesus that Magellan gave it to her, telling her “to keep it in place of her idols.” By nightfall, approximately 800 souls had been baptized. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Coconut Passage

When the Spaniards first made contact at Homonhon, the natives brought coconuts, among other goods. Pigafetta wrote one of the most quoted passages in the entire journal: “Coconuts are the fruit of the palm tree. Just as we have bread, wine, oil, and milk, so these people get everything from those trees.” He then describes how wine is obtained by making a hole in the heart of the tree from which “distils a liquor that resembles white must.” The coconut itself is “as large as a man’s head or thereabouts,” with an outer husk “green and thicker than two fingers” from which cord is made, a hard shell beneath that, and “a white marrowy substance one finger in thickness” that they eat “fresh with meat and fish as we do bread.” He further describes how oil, vinegar, and milk are made from the coconut — “Milk can also be made from it, for we made some: we scraped that marrowy substance and then mixed the scrapings with its own water, which we strained through a cloth, and so obtained milk like goat’s milk.” He concludes that “a family of ten persons can support itself on two trees.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Account of the Mactan Battle

Pigafetta is the sole eyewitness to the Battle of Mactan. His account describes the coral rocks that prevented the boats from approaching the shore: “The mortars in the boats could not aid us since they were too far away.” The Spaniards waded through water up to their knees. Magellan was struck by “a poisoned arrow” through the right leg. The fatal sequence unfolded as the captain “always stood firm like a good knight.” After the battle, “the Christian king” sent a message to the people of Mactan offering merchandise for Magellan’s body. They “answered that they would not give up such a man, as we had hoped they would, and that they would not give him up for all the riches in the world, but that they intended to keep him as a memorial.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

The Sebu Massacre

Just as Magellan was preparing to depart, a chief named Zula arrived from the neighboring island of Matan (Mactan) with news that the chief Cilapulapu had rebelled against the king of Sebu, enraged at the conversion to Christianity, and was preparing for war. Magellan resolved to defend his new ally. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

After Magellan’s death at Mactan (see below), the king of Sebu betrayed the Spaniards. Under the pretense of a feast, he invited the officers ashore and massacred them — including Edward Barbosa (Magellan’s brother-in-law and successor) and Juan Serrano. The remaining men escaped, abandoned the Conception (scuttling her), and fled. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

MACTAN (Matan)

Mactan (rendered as Matan in Towle’s text) is a small island two or three leagues from Cebu. It was the site of the battle on April 27, 1521 (the source text gives “April 17, 1521,” likely an OCR or printing error; standard history records April 27) in which Ferdinand Magellan was killed by the forces of Chief Cilapulapu (Lapu-Lapu). ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Battle of Mactan — Chapter XIII

Magellan, determined to bear the brunt of the conflict alone, refused the king of Sebu’s offer of troops. He took sixty of his bravest men in three large boats, each with a cannon on the prow. The men wore corslets and helmets, and carried guns and swords. Captain Serrano was left in command of the fleet during Magellan’s absence. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The boats set out at midnight for Mactan under a calm, starlit sky. Magellan hoped to surprise the enemy; but the alarm had already been given — a traitor had apparently escaped from Sebu to warn Cilapulapu. Three hours before daylight, the Spaniards arrived off Mactan to see a formidable array of barbarians posted on a hillock with long wooden shields and weapons. They set up a wild shout and shook their shields and spears in token of hostility. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Magellan sent a Moorish interpreter ashore as his envoy. The Moor was received and conducted to Cilapulapu, who replied defiantly:

“I will not submit; if the white strangers have lances, so have we, though ours are only lances of reeds. Moreover, we have wooden shields hardened by fire. Let the strangers beware.” ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Cilapulapu asked not to be attacked by night — a cunning ruse, as the rebels had dug a deep ditch between the shore and their village, hoping the Spaniards would fall into it in the dark. Magellan saw through the trick and waited until daylight. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

At dawn, forty-nine Spaniards waded ashore (eleven stayed with the boats). The water was shallow and rocky, reaching their waists. Magellan was the first to leap into the water, drawing his sword. Cilapulapu himself appeared — a man of gigantic stature, decked with brilliant feathers and paint, making defiant gestures. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The rebel forces were at least fifteen hundred against forty-nine. They advanced in three bodies, attacking the Spanish flanks and front. The Spaniards’ gunfire and crossbow arrows proved ineffective — bullets whizzed over heads, arrows struck wooden shields harmlessly. A hail of arrows, javelins, spears, and stones fell upon the Spaniards. Magellan ordered the burning of native huts, which redoubled the fury of the barbarians. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Cilapulapu, noticing Magellan’s finer dress and command presence, ordered his men to aim low at the Spaniards’ exposed legs. The Spaniards were forced to retreat toward the shore. The battle raged for over an hour at the water’s edge. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Magellan’s Death

Magellan fought with desperate valor despite his lameness. A poisoned arrow struck his left leg; he pulled it out and threw it back. Twice his helmet was knocked off; each time he calmly picked it up and fastened it again. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The fatal sequence:

  1. A native rushed up and plunged a lance full in Magellan’s face; blood gushed from the wound.
  2. Magellan seized the lance and ran it through his assailant’s body.
  3. He received a javelin wound in his right arm.
  4. The weakness in his arm prevented him from drawing his own sword.
  5. An enormous savage struck him with a large scimitar on his left leg, bringing him down.
  6. A multitude of savages fell upon him, running him through with spears and lances and crushing his head with stones.

Magellan died without a word or a groan. Only seven or eight of the bravest Spaniards remained fighting; the rest fled to the boats. The bodies of the fallen Spaniards — including Magellan’s — were left on the beach. Cilapulapu refused to surrender Magellan’s body, saying he would keep it “as a monument of his triumph.” ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Chief Lapu-Lapu (Cilapulapu)

Cilapulapu (standard spelling: Lapu-Lapu) was the chieftain of Mactan who led the native forces against Magellan. In Towle’s account, he is described as a man of gigantic stature who wore brilliant feathers and paint in battle. He is depicted as cunning (laying a trap with the ditch), strategically astute (ordering his men to aim low at the Spaniards’ exposed legs), and fiercely defiant. He refused to submit to the king of Sebu, defied Magellan’s ultimatum, and after the battle refused to return Magellan’s body. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

SPICE ISLANDS / MOLUCCAS (Tidor)

The Moluccas (also called the Spice Islands) were the ultimate destination of Magellan’s expedition. After fleeing Cebu and spending time at Palawan and Borneo, the fleet — now reduced to two ships, the Trinidad and Victoria, under command of Admiral Espinosa — continued searching for the islands. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Discovery of the Spice Islands — Chapter XVI

After repairs at the island of Cinbonbon (where the ships were careened and caulked), the fleet sailed northward and eastward past Mindanao, capturing junks for provisions of rice, pigs, goats, fowl, figs, sugar-canes, and palm wine. They obtained cinnamon at one island in exchange for knives. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

A tropical storm nearly destroyed them, but they took shelter in a bay where they found a Malay pilot familiar with the region. Espinosa persuaded him, by means of presents, to guide them to the Moluccas. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Early in November 1521, the Malay pilot espied four hilly islands lying close together and exclaimed they were the Moluccas. The ships had been more than two years on the voyage. In their joy, they fired the cannon and made merry on the decks. The islands stood out distinct and bold in a clear atmosphere. The harbor was deep (twenty fathoms) close up to the shore. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Tidor and Sultan Almansor

The island the Spaniards reached was Tidor (Tidore), one of the larger Moluccas. Early the next morning, the sultan of Tidor, named Almansor, came out in a gorgeous barge. He was about forty-five years old, of cream-colored complexion, with a black flowing beard, well-built and strikingly handsome. He wore a fine white tunic with gold lace on the sleeves, a long robe to his feet, a thin silk veil over a garland of flowers, and was shaded by a silk umbrella. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Espinosa went aboard the barge and negotiated friendship and trade. The sultan, who was a Muslim and an astrologer, claimed he had dreamed of the ships’ coming and had consulted the moon about it. He declared that in honor of the Spanish sovereign, his island would no longer be called Tidor but Castile. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Espinosa overwhelmed the sultan with presents: the red velvet chair he had sat in, cloths of linen, brocade, and damask, large mirrors, glass beads, knives, scissors, combs, and goblets. The sultan in turn swore that Tidor and the neighboring island Tarenate (Ternate) should be subject to Spain, and brought several loads of cloves personally from the other islands. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Cloves and Loading of the Victoria

The main object of the trade was to fill the ships’ cargoes with spices. The sultan went in his barge to other islands and returned with several loads of cloves. Espinosa began to suspect the sultan’s sincerity after the arrival of a fleet from Tarenate and warnings from a Portuguese factor, Pedro de Lorosa, who had lived in the Moluccas for ten years. Lorosa warned that the Portuguese had been assassinated on Tarenate, and that the Spaniards should be on guard. Espinosa prudently declined the sultan’s invitation to a great feast, remembering the fate of the captains at Cebu. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Departure: The Victoria Sails for Spain

Just as the final preparations for departure were made, the Trinidad was found to be leaking badly. Despite the efforts of native divers, the leak could not be found. Espinosa decided to abandon the Trinidad and transfer as much cargo as possible to the Victoria. He and fifty-three men remained at Tidor to repair the Trinidad (which was later captured by the Portuguese). ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The command of the Victoria was given to Juan Sebastian Elcano, with a crew of forty-seven Europeans and thirteen Malay prisoners. The Victoria was loaded with spices — primarily cloves — the precious cargo that would prove the expedition’s commercial success. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

On Saturday, December 21, 1521 (the source text says “1520,” an error; it was 1521), the king of Tidor visited the Victoria for the last time, bringing two Malay pilots to guide the ship into the Indian Ocean. The king wept as he bade farewell. The Victoria sailed at mid-day, with Espinosa and his men accompanying her in longboats for some distance. As she emerged from the bay, her guns boomed a parting salute to the disabled Trinidad. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The Victoria would go on to round the Cape of Good Hope, lose 21 more men to starvation and sickness, stop briefly at the Cape Verde Islands (where 13 more were captured by the Portuguese), and finally reach San Lucar, Spain, on September 6, 1522 — the first ship to circumnavigate the globe. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

Pigafetta’s Observations of the Spice Islands

Antonio Pigafetta, the expedition’s chronicler, recorded detailed observations of the spice trees and island customs. Pigafetta himself went ashore to observe: “That same day, I went ashore to see how the clove grows.” His description is the first detailed European account: ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

  • Clove trees: “The clove tree is tall and as thick as a man’s body or thereabout. Its branches spread out somewhat widely in the middle, but at the top they have the shape of a summit. Its leaves resemble those of the laurel, and the bark is olive coloured. The cloves grow at the end of the twigs, ten or twenty in a cluster.” They are white when sprouting, red when ripe, and black when dried. Two crops per year — “at the nativity of our Saviour, and the other at the nativity of St John the Baptist.” Each individual possessed his own trees “although he does not cultivate them.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]
  • Nutmeg trees: “Some nutmeg trees are found in that island: the tree resembles our walnut tree, and has leaves like it. When the nut is gathered it is as large as a small quince, with the same sort of down, and it is of the same color. Its first rind is as thick as the green rind of our walnut. Under that there is a thin layer, under which is found the mace, which is a brilliant red and is wrapped about the rind of the nut, and within that is the nutmeg.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]
  • Ginger: “Ginger is found throughout those islands: we ate it green like bread. Ginger is not a tree, but a small plant that puts forth from the ground certain shoots a span in length that resemble reeds, and whose leaves resemble those of the reed, except that they are narrower. Those shoots are worthless, but the roots form the ginger, and it is not so strong green as dry. Those people dry it in lime, for otherwise it would not keep.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

The Sultan’s Palace at Tidor

Pigafetta described the court of the Sultan of Tidore in detail. The king “had a large house outside of the city, where two hundred of his chief women lived with a like number of women to serve them. When the king eats, he sits alone or with his chief wife in a high place like a gallery from which he can see all the other women who sit about the gallery; and he orders her who best pleases him to sleep with him that night.” No one was allowed to see these women without permission “on pain of death.” The Sultan had twenty-six children — eight sons and the rest daughters. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Loading of the Victoria

Pigafetta records the trading rates at Tidore: “for ten cubits of red cloth of very good quality, they gave us one bahar of cloves, which is equivalent to four quintals and six pounds.” Various goods were exchanged at fixed rates — fifteen hatchets for a bahar, thirty-five glass drinking cups for a bahar, forty caps for a bahar. The Victoria loaded its cargo of cloves, but the ship was so heavily laden that the king of Batjan’s gift of ten bahars of cloves could not be taken aboard — “the ships could not carry them as they were so heavily laden.” Before departure, the Victoria had to unload “sixty quintals of cloves” because the crew feared “the ship might break open, as it was too heavily laden.” On December 21, 1521, the Victoria departed with “forty-seven men and thirteen Indians.” “When that hour came, the ships bid one another farewell amid the discharge of the cannon, and it seemed as though they were bewailing their last goodbye.” The Trinidad, leaking catastrophically, was left behind with fifty-three men. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]
