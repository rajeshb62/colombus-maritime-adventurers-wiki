Sir Francis Drake

Early Life and Family

Francis Drake was born in Tavistock, Devon, around 1540, the eldest of twelve sons of Edmund Drake and his wife of the Mylwaye family. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt] His grandparents, John and Margery Drake, held a lease on the farm of Crowndale, a mile below Tavistock, marking the family as yeoman farmers of modest substance. His father, Edmund, was described as a shearman and also appears in the Lay Subsidy rolls as a landholder. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Drake family were staunch Protestants. Edmund Drake fled Tavistock under a cloud after being indicted in 1548 for horse theft and highway robbery (charges of which he received a pardon), and the family’s departure was accelerated by the Western Rebellion of 1549 against the Act of Uniformity, a Catholic uprising that terrified local Protestants. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Edmund took his family to Kent, where they lived in the hull of a ship beached in the River Medway. He found work reading prayers to the seamen of the king’s navy and later became Vicar of Upchurch (1561). Young Francis was taught to read and write by his father, then apprenticed to the master of a small coasting bark that plied the Channel and North Sea. The old master died without heirs and bequeathed the bark to Drake. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Drake family had a kinship connection with the Hawkins family of Plymouth. Francis Russell (later Earl of Bedford), the son of the Drakes’ landlord, stood as godfather to Francis Drake and gave him his Christian name. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Early Voyages with John Hawkins

Drake sold his bark and moved to Plymouth, where he contacted his kinsmen, the shipowning brothers William and John Hawkins. He served as purser on a trading voyage to north-eastern Spain around 1564. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

In 1566 Drake made his first voyage to the Guinea coast under the auspices of the Hawkins family, part of the slave-trading ventures that carried enslaved Africans to the Spanish Caribbean. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Disaster at San Juan de Ulúa (1568)

Drake served under John Hawkins in the third slave-trading voyage (1567–1569). After selling their human cargo, the English fleet — including Hawkins’s flagship the Jesus of Lübeck and Drake’s first command, the 50-ton Judith — sought refuge at the Mexican port of San Juan de Ulúa from a storm. A Spanish fleet arrived, and despite an initial truce, the Spanish attacked treacherously. Most of the English ships were destroyed or captured. Drake escaped in the Judith, bringing about thirty men back to England. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The massacre at San Juan de Ulúa was formative for Drake. It gave him a deep personal hatred of Spain, which he considered perfidious, and served as the moral justification for his subsequent private war against Spanish interests in the Americas. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Raids on the Spanish Main (1570–1573)

Between 1570 and 1571 Drake made reconnaissance voyages to the West Indies. In 1572 he led a larger expedition to the Isthmus of Panama with two ships, the Pascha and the Swan, and about seventy-three men. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

He surprised the town of Nombre de Dios, wounding the governor, but was himself wounded and forced to withdraw. With the help of cimarrón (runaway African) allies, Drake ambushed the Spanish silver train near Nombre de Dios in early 1573, capturing a substantial haul. During this expedition he climbed a tree in the Panamanian highlands and became the first Englishman to see the Pacific Ocean, vowing to sail upon it. He returned to Plymouth in August 1573. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Service in Ireland (1575–1576)

Drake served under the Earl of Essex in Ireland, commanding a squadron that operated off the Irish coast, hunting Spanish and papal supply vessels. He gained valuable military experience and the patronage of Essex. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Circumnavigation (1577–1580)

Preparation and Departure

Secretly backed by Queen Elizabeth I, Francis Walsingham, the Earl of Leicester, Sir Christopher Hatton, and other investors, Drake assembled a fleet for a voyage aimed at the Pacific coast of Spanish America. The squadron consisted of: ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

  • Pelican (later renamed Golden Hind), ~150 tons, Drake’s flagship, ~80 feet long, carrying 18 brass and iron guns
  • Elizabeth, 80 tons, 16 guns, commanded by John Winter (second-in-command)
  • Marigold, 30 tons, 16 guns, commanded by John Thomas
  • Swan (flyboat), 50 tons, 5 guns, commanded by John Chester
  • Benedict (pinnace), 15 tons, 1 gun, commanded by Tom Moone

The fleet of about 160 men departed Plymouth on 13 December 1577. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Strait of Magellan

After crossing the Atlantic, Drake reached the coast of Brazil and then made for the Strait of Magellan. On 20 August 1578, at the entrance to the strait, he rechristened the Pelican the Golden Hind in honour of Sir Christopher Hatton (whose crest featured a hind). Drake navigated the 300-mile strait in fourteen days — the fastest passage of the century (Magellan took thirty-seven days). He reached the Pacific (the South Seas) on 6 September 1578. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Loss of Ships and Storm

Emerging into the Pacific, Drake’s squadron was struck by a ferocious storm that lasted over fifty days. The Marigold was lost with all twenty-nine hands. The Elizabeth, under Captain Winter, was separated and eventually turned back to England, arriving in June 1579. The Golden Hind was driven southwards, where Drake discovered that Tierra del Fuego was not part of a southern continent but a chain of islands with open sea beyond — a discovery that proved the existence of a passage south of the Strait of Magellan (later named Drake Passage). He formally claimed the Elizabeth Islands for England. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Pacific Raids

Now alone in the Pacific, Drake sailed north along the coast of Chile and Peru, raiding Spanish ports that had never seen an enemy ship:

  • Valparaíso: captured and plundered
  • Callao (the port of Lima): raided the harbour on 15 February 1579, cutting ships’ cables and hacking down masts
  • Captured the San Cristobal and other prizes ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Capture of the Cacafuego

On 1 March 1579, Drake overtook the Nuestra Señora de la Concepción (nicknamed Cacafuego), a Spanish treasure ship of 120 tons under Captain San Juan de Anton. After a brief exchange of fire, she surrendered. Her cargo was immense: thirteen or fourteen chests of silver reals, 80 pounds of gold, and 26 tons of silver bars — the registered cargo alone was worth 360,000 pesos (about £126,000, roughly half the annual revenue of the English Crown), plus unregistered bullion. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Nova Albion and the Pacific Crossing

Continuing north, Drake explored the coast of California, which he claimed for England as Nova Albion. He repaired his ship and traded with the Miwok people. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

From there, Drake struck west across the Pacific, reaching the Moluccas (Spice Islands). He made a trade agreement with Sultan Babu of Ternate, loading six tons of cloves. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Reef and the Indian Ocean Crossing

Leaving Ternate, the Golden Hind grounded on a reef off the east coast of Celebes (9 January 1580). After desperate efforts, the ship was freed by a shift in wind. Drake threw three tons of cloves, two artillery pieces, and other cargo overboard to lighten her. He then navigated south of Java — becoming the first European to chart its southern coast — and struck across the Indian Ocean, making landfall at the Cape of Good Hope in June 1580. He reached Sierra Leone for water and fresh provisions. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Return to Plymouth

The Golden Hind arrived in Plymouth Sound on 26 September 1580, almost three years after departure. Drake had become the first English commander to circumnavigate the globe (and the second circumnavigation in history after the remnants of Magellan’s crew). ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Knighthood and Rise to Prominence

Queen Elizabeth I visited the Golden Hind at Deptford on 1 April 1581, where she had Drake knighted aboard his ship. The ceremony was performed by the French ambassador, Monsieur de Marchaumont, at the queen’s direction. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Drake was granted a coat of arms: sable with a fesse wavy between two stars argent, with a crest featuring a terrestrial globe with a ship under sail, inscribed “Auxilio Divino” (By Divine Help). He acquired the estate of Buckland Abbey in Devon in December 1580, about four miles north of Plymouth, for £3,400. He served as Mayor of Plymouth in 1581 and became one of the largest property holders in the town, purchasing forty separate freeholds in 1582. He was elected as a Member of Parliament and served as a magistrate. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Spanish War

Raid on Santiago (1585)

In September 1585, Drake led a fleet of some twenty-five ships and over 2,000 men — the largest England had ever sent from home waters — on a major expedition against Spanish possessions. His flagship was the Elizabeth Bonaventure (600 tons). After a brief stop at Vigo, he crossed the Atlantic and raided the Cape Verde Islands, including Santiago. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Capture of Cartagena (1586)

In February 1586, Drake captured and sacked Cartagena de Indias (on the Caribbean coast of present-day Colombia), one of the wealthiest Spanish cities in the New World. He held the city for several weeks and extracted a substantial ransom. He also sacked Santo Domingo (Hispaniola) before sailing north to evacuate the failing Roanoke colony in Virginia in June 1586. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Raid on Cadiz (1587) — “Singeing the King of Spain’s Beard”

With the Spanish Armada preparing for the invasion of England, Drake proposed a pre-emptive strike. Commissioned on 15 March 1587, he assembled a fleet of some twenty-four ships and about 3,000 men. His flagship was again the Elizabeth Bonaventure, with the Golden Lion as vice-admiral under William Borough and the Dreadnought as rear-admiral under Thomas Fenner. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

On 19 April 1587, Drake attacked the harbour of Cadiz, catching the Spanish completely by surprise. He destroyed an estimated twenty-five to thirty-nine ships, including the 1,500-ton flagship of the Armada’s commander, the Marquis of Santa Cruz. He called the exploit “singeing the King of Spain’s beard.” The raid delayed the Armada’s sailing by a year. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Spanish Armada (1588)

Drake served as Vice-Admiral of the English fleet under Lord Charles Howard of Effingham. He flew his flag aboard the Revenge, one of the queen’s finest galleons. John Hawkins served as Rear-Admiral aboard the Victory, and Martin Frobisher commanded the Triumph. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Armada of 130 ships under the Duke of Medina Sidonia was sighted off the Lizard on 29 July 1588. The English fleet of about 140 ships sailed from Plymouth on 19 July. Legend has it that Drake insisted on finishing his game of bowls on Plymouth Hoe before setting sail. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The English harried the Armada up the Channel in a series of running engagements off Plymouth, Portland Bill, and the Isle of Wight. On the night of 28–29 July, the English launched eight fireships into the Spanish anchorage off Calais, forcing the Armada to cut its cables and scatter in disorder. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

In the decisive Battle of Gravelines on 29 July 1588, the English fleet closed with the scattered Spanish ships. Drake led the attack. Superior English gunnery and ship-handling inflicted severe damage. The Armada was driven into the North Sea, unable to link up with the Duke of Parma’s invasion army. Storms completed its destruction as it fled around Scotland and Ireland. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Later Voyages

The Portugal Expedition (1589)

In 1589, Drake commanded a joint naval and military expedition to Portugal aimed at restoring the Portuguese pretender Dom Antonio to the throne (Portugal had been annexed by Spain in 1580). The expedition sacked Corunna but failed to capture Lisbon. The campaign was strategically unsuccessful and suffered heavy losses from disease. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The Final Voyage: West Indies (1595–1596)

In 1595, Drake and Sir John Hawkins co-commanded a large expedition to the West Indies, their last joint venture. The fleet sailed from Plymouth on 28 August 1595. Hawkins, ageing and ill, died at Puerto Rico on 12 November 1595. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

The expedition fared poorly. An attack on Puerto Rico was repulsed. Drake sacked Rio de la Hacha and other towns but failed to capture Panama. Disease, demoralisation, and strong Spanish defences had fundamentally changed the Caribbean. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Death

Drake fell ill with dysentery (the “bloody flux”) and died aboard his flagship, the Defiance, off the island of Buena Ventura, near Portobelo (Puerto Bello), Panama, on 28 January 1596, at about four o’clock in the morning. He was aged approximately fifty-five. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

His body was encased in a lead coffin and, after a sermon by Mr Bride aboard the Defiance, was lowered into the sea west or south-west of Bajo Salmedina island, to the sound of trumpets and a gun salute from the fleet. The English subsequently captured and burned Portobelo in tribute to their fallen commander. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]

Key Relationships

  • Queen Elizabeth I: his sovereign and patron; knighted him and supported his ventures, though often ambivalently
  • Sir Francis Walsingham: principal patron and spymaster; a key backer of Drake’s circumnavigation and later operations
  • Sir John Hawkins: kinsman, mentor, and fellow naval commander; served as Rear-Admiral during the Armada campaign; died together with Drake on the 1595–96 West Indies voyage
  • Lord Charles Howard of Effingham: Lord High Admiral, commander of the English fleet against the Armada; worked harmoniously with Drake as his vice-admiral
  • Sir Christopher Hatton: courtier and patron; the Golden Hind was named in honour of his family crest
  • King Philip II of Spain: principal adversary; Drake’s raids humiliated Philip and intensified the Anglo-Spanish War
  • Dom Antonio: Portuguese pretender to the throne; Drake’s 1589 expedition attempted (unsuccessfully) to restore him
  • Thomas Doughty: gentleman adventurer executed by Drake during the circumnavigation for mutiny, a controversial episode
  • Martin Frobisher: fellow sea commander; served in the Armada campaign, strained relations with Drake
  • John Winter: captain of the Elizabeth, turned back from the circumnavigation and was accused of deserting Drake

Key Places

  • Tavistock, Devon: Drake’s birthplace
  • Plymouth, Devon: Drake’s home port; served as Mayor (1581); the starting and ending point for his voyages
  • Buckland Abbey, Devon: Drake’s country seat, purchased in 1580, now a National Trust museum
  • Nombre de Dios, Panama: raided by Drake in 1572
  • Cartagena (Colombia): captured and sacked in 1586
  • San Juan de Ulúa (Mexico): site of the 1568 Spanish attack that shaped Drake’s hatred of Spain
  • Cadiz, Spain: raided in 1587 (“singeing the King of Spain’s beard”)
  • Calais, France: site of the fireship attack against the Armada (1588)
  • Portobelo (Puerto Bello), Panama: Drake died and was buried at sea nearby
  • Strait of Magellan: navigated by Drake in 1578 in fourteen days, a record passage
  • Spice Islands (Moluccas): Drake made a trade agreement at Ternate
  • Nova Albion (California): claimed for England by Drake in 1579
  • The Cape of Good Hope: rounded by Drake in 1580 on his return voyage

Ships

  • Golden Hind (originally Pelican): Drake’s flagship during the circumnavigation, ~150 tons, 18 guns
  • Revenge: Drake’s flagship during the Armada campaign of 1588
  • Judith: 50-ton bark, Drake’s first command under Hawkins, which he sailed to safety from San Juan de Ulúa (1568)
  • Elizabeth: 80-ton ship under John Winter during the circumnavigation, forced by storms to abandon the voyage and return to England
  • Elizabeth Bonaventure: Drake’s flagship during the 1585 West Indies expedition and the 1587 Cadiz raid (~600 tons)
  • Defiance: Drake’s flagship during his final voyage (1595–96), aboard which he died
  • Marigold: 30-ton ship lost with all hands during storms after passing the Strait of Magellan (1578)
  • Pelican: the ship’s original name before rechristening as Golden Hind
  • Benedict: 15-ton pinnace carried aboard the Pelican/Golden Hind
  • Swan: 50-ton flyboat in the circumnavigation fleet

Legacy

Drake was the most celebrated Englishman of his age, famous throughout Europe. The poet Charles FitzGeffrey wrote a full-length epic in his honour. His exploits inspired a generation of English and Dutch privateers. The coinage of the phrase “singeing the King of Spain’s beard” and the legendary game of bowls on Plymouth Hoe became part of English folklore. The portrait of the Golden Hind once adorned the British halfpenny. Buckland Abbey is preserved as a monument to his memory. ^[source: sugden_drake.txt]
