Armada de Molucca — Magellan’s Five Ships

The Armada de Molucca (Spanish: Armada de Molucca, “Fleet of the Moluccas”) was the five-ship fleet commanded by Ferdinand Magellan that departed San Lucar de Barrameda, Spain, on September 20, 1519, in search of a westward route to the Spice Islands (Moluccas). The fleet — the Trinidad, San Antonio, Victoria, Concepcion, and Santiago — was the first expedition to circumnavigate the globe. Only one ship, the Victoria under Juan Sebastian Elcano, completed the return voyage to Spain on September 6, 1522. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt]

The five vessels carried approximately 250 men, 80 cannon, and provisions for two years. They were small by modern standards — the largest was only 120 tons burden — but were considered well-appointed for a long-distance voyage in the early 16th century. Crews were distributed as follows: the Trinidad (flagship) carried about 62 men, the San Antonio about 60, the Victoria and Concepcion about 45 each, and the Santiago about 32 — making the Trinidad and San Antonio the largest complements and the Santiago the smallest. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV; bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. III]

Pigafetta’s Account of Shipboard Life

Pigafetta’s journal provides the most detailed surviving description of daily life aboard the Armada de Molucca. He records the fleet’s signal system and watch organization in precise detail:

Signal System: Magellan prescribed that his ship would always precede the others at night and carry “a large torch of wood, which they call farol… hanging from the poop of his ship.” One light was a signal for the other ships to follow; two lights meant to veer or change tack; three lights meant to take in the bonnet sail; four lights meant to strike all sails; many lights or a mortar shot signalled land or shoals. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Three-Watch System: “Three watches were set nightly: the first at the beginning of the night, the second (which they call modora) in the middle, and the third at the end [of the night]. All of the men in the ships were divided into three colonelli: the first was that of the captain or boatswain, those two alternating nightly; the second, of either the pilot or boatswain’s mate; and the third, of the master.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

St. Elmo’s Fire: During storms in the Atlantic crossing, Pigafetta describes a phenomenon that brought the crew to tears: “During those storms the Holy Body, that is to say St Elmo, appeared to us many times in the form of light. Once he appeared during an exceedingly dark night, with the brightness of a blazing torch, on the maintop, where he stayed for about two hours or more, to our consolation, for we were weeping. When that blessed light was about to leave us, so dazzling was the brightness that it cast into our eyes, that we all remained for more than an eighth of an hour blinded and calling for mercy, for truly we thought that we were dead men. The sea suddenly grew calm.” ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Ship Taking Water During Storms: Pigafetta records that during severe storms the ships were in constant danger: “We were unable to advance, and so as to avoid being wrecked, all the sails were struck; and in this manner did we wander here and there on the sea, waiting for the squall to cease.” Later, at the strait, “the ships were almost lost because of the furious winds; but God and the holy bodies aided them.” At Tidore, the Trinidad began taking on water catastrophically: “The water was rushing in as through a pipe, but we were unable to find where it was coming in. All that day and the next, we did nothing but work the pump, but to no avail.” Native divers were sent down but could not locate the leak. ^[source: pigafetta_magellan.txt]

Trinidad (Flagship)

The Trinidad (“Trinity”) was the flagship of the Armada de Molucca, carrying Magellan’s admiral’s flag. She was a stout, ocean-going vessel of approximately 110 tons burden, armed with four large iron cannon — the heaviest armament in the fleet. Magellan commanded the expedition from her deck, and she led the squadron throughout the Atlantic crossing, the exploration of the Patagonian coast, the passage through the strait, and the Pacific crossing. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV, VIII]

Role in the Voyage

The Trinidad was the largest of the ships by rank but not by tonnage — that distinction belongs to the San Antonio. She served as Magellan’s command platform for the entire westward journey. It was from her deck that the mutiny at Port St. Julian was suppressed (March–April 1520), the Strait of Magellan discovered (October 21, 1520), and the Pacific Ocean crossed (99 days, November 1520 – March 1521). ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI–IX]

After Magellan’s death at the Battle of Mactan on April 27, 1521, and the subsequent massacre of his successor Barbosa at Sebu, command passed to Espinosa. The Trinidad continued on with the Victoria to the Moluccas, but upon arrival at Tidor she was found to be too leaky for the long voyage home. Despite efforts by native divers to locate the leak, the ship was judged unseaworthy. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVII]

Espinosa and 53 men remained at Tidor to attempt repairs, intending to sail east to Panama. After extensive patching and caulking, the Trinidad finally set sail for Panama on April 6, 1522. What followed was a grueling 7-month ordeal: the ship was battered by relentless headwinds and contrary currents in the Molucca Sea, provisions ran desperately short, and 30 of the 53 crew died from starvation and scurvy — including Captain Espinosa’s own brother. Forced back to the Moluccas by the elements, the crippled ship limped into a bay on the island of Tidore, only to find the Portuguese — under Antonio de Brito — already in possession. The Portuguese seized the Trinidad and her surviving crew as prisoners. The ship itself, rotten and worm-eaten beyond repair, was stripped of anything useful and left to rot. It eventually sank at anchor or was broken apart by the tides, ending its days as a piece of driftwood on the shore. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. XIV]

San Antonio (Largest Ship)

The San Antonio was the largest vessel of the fleet at 120 tons burden, carrying approximately 60 men — the largest crew in the fleet alongside the Trinidad — and originally second in command under her captain, Juan de Cartagena. Cartagena was a proud Spanish nobleman appointed by King Charles himself, and his resentment of Magellan’s Portuguese command was the root of the fleet’s gravest internal crisis. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV, VI; bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. III, VII]

The Mutiny at Port St. Julian

Cartagena, along with Luis de Mendoza (captain of the Victoria) and Gaspar de Quesada (captain of the Concepcion), conspired to seize the fleet and depose or kill Magellan. The mutiny broke out at Port St. Julian on the Patagonian coast in March 1520. Magellan suppressed it with characteristic ruthlessness: Mendoza was stabbed to death on his own deck by the fleet constable, Quesada was imprisoned, and Cartagena was later executed by firing squad. The mutineers’ bodies were quartered and displayed as a warning. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

After the mutiny, Magellan appointed his cousin Alvaro de Mesquita as captain of the San Antonio. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Desertion at the Strait (Esteban Gomez’s Mutiny)

In October–November 1520, while navigating the newly discovered Strait of Magellan, the San Antonio was sent ahead to explore one of the branching channels. The pilot Esteban Gomez — a skilled Portuguese-born navigator who had long resented Magellan’s command and had earlier urged the fleet to turn back — seized the opportunity. Gomez persuaded the crew that Magellan was leading them to certain death and that returning to Spain with the ship would make them rich. Under his influence, the crew mutinied a second time. They seized the loyal Captain Mesquita, wounded him, put him in irons, and imprisoned him in his own cabin. Gomez then took command and sailed the ship back through the strait and across the Atlantic to Spain. The desertion was a devastating blow: it cost Magellan his largest vessel, a vast store of provisions, and his authority at the most critical phase of the voyage. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VIII; bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. VII]

Upon her return to Seville, the San Antonio’s officers spread false accusations against Magellan, claiming he had mistreated the crew and mismanaged the expedition. Mesquita was imprisoned upon arrival. The desertion deprived Magellan of his largest vessel at the most critical phase of the voyage. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VIII]

Victoria (The Ship That Completed the Circumnavigation)

The Victoria (named after the Church of Santa Maria de la Victoria in Seville) was the only ship of the Armada de Molucca to complete the circumnavigation of the globe. At approximately 85 tons burden, she was the third-smallest vessel of the fleet but proved the most fortunate and durable. With a crew of about 45 men — one of the smallest complements in the fleet — she nevertheless outlasted all her larger consorts. Under the command of Juan Sebastian Elcano, she returned to San Lucar on September 6, 1522, with only 18 survivors from the original 250 men. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV, XVIII; bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. III, XIV]

Early Voyage and the Mutiny

The Victoria was originally commanded by Luis de Mendoza, one of the three Spanish captains who conspired against Magellan in the mutiny at Port St. Julian. Mendoza was killed on his own deck by Magellan’s constable, Fernandes, who stabbed him while pretending to deliver a message. Thereafter the Victoria was placed under loyal command. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Command Transfer to Elcano

After the massacre of Barbosa and Serrano at Sebu, the depleted fleet — now reduced to the Trinidad and Victoria — continued to the Moluccas under Espinosa’s command. Sebastian del Cano (Juan Sebastian Elcano) was given command of the Victoria at this time. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XIV]

When the Trinidad proved unseaworthy at Tidor, Espinosa remained behind and entrusted the Victoria to Elcano, who sailed westward on the final leg of the circumnavigation. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVII]

The Return Voyage — The Ghost Ship

Elcano’s voyage aboard the Victoria was a desperate ordeal. From Tidor the ship crossed the Indian Ocean, rounded the Cape of Good Hope despite contrary winds (and knowing it was in Portuguese waters), and refused to stop at Portuguese Mozambique for fear of capture. Provisions ran desperately short; 21 men died of scurvy and starvation before the ship could reach a friendly port. A brief stop at the Cape Verde Islands resulted in the capture of 13 men by the Portuguese. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVII–XVIII]

On September 6, 1522, the Victoria — battered, leaking, and with only 18 emaciated survivors of the original 250 — arrived at San Lucar, near Seville. Bergreen describes the vessel as a “ghost ship”: her hull was so thoroughly riddled by shipworms that she barely stayed afloat, the planking rotted and porous in places, her sails patched and torn. The crew — skin stretched tight over bone, gums black with scurvy, too weak to stand — staggered ashore on tarred and blistered feet. The ship’s cargo of cloves, cinnamon, and sandalwood, when unloaded, was found to have kept the vessel buoyant by filling the worm-eaten cavities in her timbers. The crew walked in barefoot procession to church carrying tapers in thanksgiving. Elcano was honored with a coat of arms and a pension. ^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt, Ch. XIV; towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XVIII]

Concepcion (Burned at Bohol)

The Concepcion (sometimes spelled Conception; named for the Immaculate Conception) was a vessel of approximately 90 tons commanded by Gaspar de Quesada. She was an active participant in the mutiny at Port St. Julian but was later recovered by Magellan. Her end came after the Sebu massacre, when the survivors, reduced in number, found themselves unable to crew three ships. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV, XIV]

Role in the Mutiny

Quesada was the captain whom the mutineers first seized — boarding his ship by night, taking him prisoner, and winning his crew over to the conspiracy. After the mutiny was suppressed, Quesada was imprisoned and his ship placed under a loyal captain. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VI]

Destruction at Bohol

Following Magellan’s death at Mactan (April 27, 1521) and the massacre of Barbosa, Serrano, and most of the officers at a feast on Sebu (where the king had treacherously reverted to his old allegiances), the remaining men fled in the Trinidad and Victoria, with the Concepcion as the third vessel. The crews were too depleted by battle, massacre, and illness to manage three ships. At the island of Bohol, Espinosa made the decision to sacrifice the Concepcion as the weakest and least seaworthy of the remaining three. Her cargo was transferred to the other ships, and she was hauled up onto the shore and burned. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. XIV]

Santiago (Wrecked on the Patagonian Coast)

The Santiago (“Saint James”) was the smallest vessel of the fleet at approximately 75 tons, commanded by Juan Serrano — a relative of Magellan’s old friend Francisco Serrano and one of the most trusted captains in the expedition. Her life was brief: she was the first ship lost, wrecked during a scouting mission along the Patagonian coast while the fleet was wintering at Port St. Julian. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. IV, VII]

The Wreck

In August 1520, while the fleet was at anchor in Port St. Julian, Magellan sent the Santiago southward under Serrano to explore the coast ahead and scout for a passage. A powerful current seized the small vessel and swept it uncontrollably forward; before Serrano could react, the Santiago grounded upon rocks. The ship was hopelessly wrecked — a total loss. The crew managed to scramble into their boats and save themselves, along with such provisions as could be quickly gathered. They made their way back to Port St. Julian and reported the loss to Magellan. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VII]

Aftermath

The loss of the Santiago reduced the fleet to four ships just as Magellan was preparing to depart St. Julian for the southward search that would lead him to the strait. Serrano and his crew were distributed among the remaining vessels; Serrano himself was reassigned and later perished in the Sebu massacre. ^[source: towle_magellan.txt, Ch. VII, XIV]

Faleiro’s Navigational Instruments

The expedition’s scientific backbone was the extraordinary collection of navigational instruments assembled by Ruy Faleiro, the cosmologist who co-created the expedition with Magellan. When Faleiro was removed from the voyage for mental instability, Magellan insisted on retaining his instruments. The trove consisted of 35 compasses (supplemented by 15 more purchased in Seville), a wooden astrolabe constructed by Faleiro himself, six metal astrolabes, 21 wooden quadrants, 18 hourglasses, and 24 top-secret charts kept under lock and armed guard. Six of the charts had been drafted by Faleiro; 18 by cosmographer Nuno Garcia (7 under Faleiro’s direction, 11 under Magellan’s). Unauthorized possession of such charts was punishable by death. The fleet also carried blank parchment and dried skins for making additional maps during the voyage.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt]

Francisco Albo’s Pilot Log

The expedition’s navigational record survives in two forms: Pigafetta’s narrative diary and the technical log kept by pilot Francisco Albo. Albo’s log followed the ship’s custom of beginning the day at noon rather than midnight, causing systematic date discrepancies between his record and Pigafetta’s — an event recorded on a given morning by one diarist would appear on a different day in the other’s account. Albo’s navigational calculations were respected by Magellan, who ordered him to record the strait’s twists and turns “as accurately as possible.” It was Albo who logged the discovery of the Cape of the Eleven Thousand Virgins on October 21, 1520, and later the sighting of Shark Island (Isla de los Tiburones) in the Pacific, where the crew caught many sharks. When the Victoria reached the Cape Verde Islands on the return voyage, Albo’s calculations — together with Pigafetta’s — demonstrated that they had lost a day, providing the first empirical evidence of the need for an international date line.^[source: bergreen_magellan.txt, pigafetta_magellan.txt]
