Caonabo

Caonabo (fl. 1492–1496) was the Carib cacique of Maguana, one of the five principal domains of Hispaniola, ruling over a territory that included the gold mines of Cibao. He was the most powerful and warlike native chieftain encountered by the Spanish in the early years of colonization, and the only cacique who possessed the strategic vision and military talent to mount organized resistance against European encroachment. He led the destruction of La Navidad, besieged Santo Tomas, and was eventually captured by Alonso de Ojeda through a celebrated stratagem involving polished steel manacles. He died in captivity during the return voyage to Spain.

Domain of Maguana

Hispaniola at the time of Columbus’s arrival was divided into five caciquates (chiefdoms). Maguana occupied the central and mountainous part of the island, including the rich gold mines of Cibao. Caonabo was described by Washington Irving as the most fierce and puissant of the savage chieftains, and the inveterate enemy of the white men. His wife was Anacaona, the sister of Behechio, cacique of Xaragua, celebrated throughout the island for her charms and graces.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII (territorial divisions)]

Attack on La Navidad (1493)

After Columbus departed for Spain in January 1493, the 39 volunteers left behind at La Navidad — a fortress built from the timbers of the wrecked Santa Maria — fell into dissension and licentiousness, abusing the hospitality of the native population. Taking advantage of the disorder, Caonabo led a surprise assault on the fortress. He burned La Navidad to the ground and massacred its entire garrison. The attack marked the first large-scale native retaliation against Spanish settlement in the New World.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. III–IV]

When Columbus returned on his second voyage in November 1493, he found only ruins and corpses. The friendly cacique Guacanagari tearfully described the massacre to Columbus and showed the wounds he claimed to have received defending the Spaniards.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII]

Siege of Santo Tomas (1494)

In the wake of the destruction of La Navidad, Columbus established a chain of fortified posts across the island. The most important of these was the fortress of Santo Tomas, built in the Cibao mountains at the heart of Caonabo’s territory — a direct challenge to his authority. The command of Santo Tomas was given to Alonso de Ojeda, with a garrison of only fifty men.

Caonabo, enraged by this intrusion, assembled a reported 10,000 warriors armed with war-clubs, bows and arrows, and fire-hardened lances. Marching secretly through the forests, he suddenly appeared near the fortress, expecting to find the garrison in careless security. Ojeda, however, had drawn up his men warily within a stone tower perched on an almost insulated height, with a river nearly surrounding it and the remaining approach traversed by a deep ditch — making it impervious to an open assault by unarmoured natives.

Foiled in his attempt to storm the fort, Caonabo laid siege, spreading his army through the adjacent forests to intercept supplies and foraging parties. The siege lasted thirty days, reducing the garrison to great distress. At one point, an Indian gained access to the fort bringing two wood-pigeons for the commander’s table, a measure of the famine within. Ojeda held out, however, until reinforcements eventually relieved the pressure.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. III]

Capture by Ojeda’s Stratagem

With Caonabo remaining the most formidable threat to Spanish control — and the rugged mountains of his territory making conventional warfare perilous — Columbus was receptive when Ojeda proposed a daring scheme to capture the cacique alive by trickery.

Choosing ten bold and hardy followers, well armed and well mounted, Ojeda rode sixty leagues into the wilderness to Caonabo’s most populous town. Approaching with great deference, he announced he had come on a friendly embassy from Columbus bearing an invaluable present. Caonabo, who had witnessed Ojeda’s prowess in battle and admired him as a fellow warrior, received him with savage courtesy. Ojeda so ingratiated himself that he convinced the cacique to visit Isabella and make a treaty with Columbus.

A particular lure was the bell of the chapel of Isabella, which the Indians regarded as supernatural — saying it had come from Turey, or the skies. Caonabo had longed to see this wonder, and the promise of it proved irresistible.

When the time came to depart, Caonabo assembled a large force of warriors, insisting it was unfitting for a prince to travel unattended. Ojeda, suspecting treachery, resolved on a bolder stratagem. Halting near the river Yagui, Ojeda produced a set of manacles of polished steel, so highly burnished that they looked like silver. He told Caonabo they were royal ornaments from heaven (the Turey of Biscay), worn by the monarchs of Castile on solemn occasions, and intended as gifts. He proposed that the cacique should bathe in the river, after which he would be decorated with the ornaments, mounted on Ojeda’s horse, and paraded in state before his subjects.

Dazzled by the glitter and flattered by the prospect of bestriding one of the terrifying Spanish horses, Caonabo agreed. He accompanied Ojeda to the river with few attendants. After bathing, he mounted behind Ojeda, and the shackles were adjusted. Ojeda and his horsemen then made several circuits to gain space, the Indians shrinking back from the prancing steeds. At length, Ojeda swept into the forest until the trees hid him from view. His followers closed around, drew their swords, and threatened Caonabo with instant death if he resisted. They bound him with cords to Ojeda and dashed across the Yagui, making off through the wilderness with their prize.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. IV]

Imprisonment

Ojeda entered Isabella in triumph with his captive bound behind him. Columbus received the dangerous foe with great satisfaction. Caonabo, however, maintained a haughty and unsubdued bearing. He boasted of his achievement in surprising and burning La Navidad and slaughtering its garrison, and declared he had secretly reconnoitred Isabella with an intention of wreaking the same destruction upon it.

Columbus, recognizing that Caonabo was too dangerous to remain on the island, determined to send him to Spain. In the meantime, he ordered that the cacique be treated with kindness and respect, lodging him in a part of the admiral’s own dwelling house — though kept a close prisoner in chains, probably the very manacles that had ensnared him. The prison was insecure: Las Casas observes that the admiral’s house was not spacious and had few chambers, so the captive chieftain could be seen by passers-by from the portal.

Throughout his confinement, Caonabo maintained a haughty deportment towards Columbus, refusing to rise when the admiral entered the room. When Ojeda entered, however, the cacique would rise and salute him with profound respect. Asked why he honoured a subject more than the supreme chief, Caonabo replied that Columbus had never dared to come personally to seize him — it was only through the valour of Ojeda that he was a prisoner; to Ojeda, therefore, he owed reverence, not to the admiral.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. IV]

Death

Caonabo was sent to Spain on one of the ships returning with Columbus in 1496. During the voyage, which lasted about three months, the proud cacique died. Contemporary chroniclers pass over the event briefly; his death is principally ascribed to the morbid melancholy of a proud but broken spirit. Some later writers speculated that he perished in a hurricane that sank caravels in the harbour of Isabella, but the united testimony of the curate of los Palacios, Peter Martyr d’Anghiera, and Fernando Columbus confirms that he sailed with the admiral and died at sea. The ships anchored in the bay of Cadiz on June 11, 1496.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book IX Ch. I]

Despite the brevity of the record, Irving notes that Caonabo was an extraordinary character in savage life — a simple Carib warrior who rose by enterprise and courage to become the dominant spirit of the most populous island of the Antilles, possessing sagacity sufficient to foresee the fatal effects of Spanish ascendancy and military talent to organize resistance against its inroads. His death on a Spanish ship, in chains, marked the end of the most formidable native challenge to European colonization in the Caribbean during its first decade.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book IX Ch. I]

Attempted Rescue

The captivity of Caonabo was deeply felt by his subjects. One of his brothers, Manicaotex, a warrior of great courage and address, assembled an army of more than 7,000 men and led them secretly to the neighbourhood of Santo Tomas, where Ojeda was again in command. His plan was to surprise a number of Spaniards and exchange them for his brother. Ojeda, reinforced by a detachment from the adelantado Bartholomew Columbus, met Manicaotex in open battle. The brother showed considerable military skill, disposing his army in five battalions, but the onslaught of Ojeda’s horsemen threw the Indian warriors into panic. Manicaotex was captured fighting bravely.^[source: irving-columbus-vol2.md, Book VIII Ch. IV]
